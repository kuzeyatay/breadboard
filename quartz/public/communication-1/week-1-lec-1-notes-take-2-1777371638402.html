From Analog Experience to Digital Communication: Frequency, Decibels, and Sampling

The central problem: how can a changing physical signal become a reliable digital message?

Communication begins with a deceptively simple situation. At one side there is a physical phenomenon: a voice, a sound pressure wave, a voltage from a microphone, a time-varying waveform. At the other side there is a receiver that must reproduce the message, perhaps as sound again through a loudspeaker. The difficulty is that the world does not naturally speak in bits. A microphone output is not a sequence of zeros and ones; it is a voltage that varies continuously in time and, in principle, can take infinitely many possible values. Yet modern communication systems mostly operate digitally. That means the course begins with one essential question: how do we take something analog, preserve its information, transmit it through a physical channel, and reconstruct it again at the other end? The communication chain answers this by passing through sampling, quantization, line coding, possible modulation, physical transmission, reception, decoding, error detection or correction, and finally signal reconstruction. The message changes form many times, but the goal is always the same: the receiver should recover the original information with sufficient fidelity.

The first step is sampling. Sampling means choosing values of a continuous-time signal at specific time instants. But sampling alone is not yet digitization. If a voltage waveform is sampled at seven time instants, the result is merely seven analog values. One value might be 1.3V, another 1.25V, another 1.135V. The time axis has become discrete, but the amplitude values are still continuous. To become digital, these sampled amplitudes must later be quantized: mapped to a finite set of levels and represented by binary codewords. This distinction is crucial. Sampling answers “when do I look at the signal?” Quantization answers “which finite numerical value do I assign to what I saw?” Only after quantization and encoding do we truly have a stream of bits.

Why go through all this trouble? Digital communication is not automatically simpler. In fact, it is technologically more complicated than simply sending an analog waveform. Early radio transmission was analog, and in a walkie-talkie-like system the channel is occupied continuously while someone speaks. But digital representation gives benefits that are so strong that the extra complexity is worth paying. Digital information can be stored as files. It can be processed by computers. It can be compressed, encrypted, copied, buffered, corrected, and transmitted in bursts. Most importantly, a digital receiver does not need to interpret every small analog variation as meaningful. If the receiver expects only two levels, for example a binary 0 and a binary 1, then moderate noise does not necessarily destroy the information. The receiver can decide whether the noisy received voltage is above or below a threshold. In an analog system, if noise is added to the waveform, the receiver simply receives the noisy waveform and cannot always know which part is signal and which part is noise. In a digital system, because the allowed messages are structured and finite, the receiver can often detect and correct errors. That is one of the deep reasons digital communication dominates modern systems.

A second deep benefit is that digital communication disconnects the duration of the original experience from the duration of transmission. A song may last three and a half minutes when played, but once sampled, quantized, and encoded, it becomes a finite collection of bits. Those bits can be sent slowly or quickly depending on the bit rate of the channel. The same song might be transmitted over one second, one tenth of a second, or much longer, depending on the network. This is impossible in the same way for a purely analog real-time waveform. In digital systems, information can be compressed into packets and different users can share the same physical medium because no single user needs to occupy the channel continuously. This is the conceptual root of ideas such as time-division multiplexing: many users can share a radio channel because each one uses it only during small time slots rather than continuously.

Frequency as a coordinate system for signals

To understand sampling, we first need the language of frequency. A signal is usually observed in time: a voltage goes up and down as seconds pass. But communication systems are not designed only in the time domain. Channels have bandwidths. Filters pass some frequencies and suppress others. Modulation shifts signals to new frequency regions. Noise may occupy certain spectral regions. Therefore, one of the strongest tools in communication engineering is the ability to move between time and frequency representations.

The idea begins with ordinary vectors. A point in three-dimensional space can be represented using three perpendicular basis directions: the x, y, and z axes. A vector has a certain projection on each basis direction. If the vector lies entirely in the x-y plane, its projection on the z-axis is zero. This is not magic; it is the dot product. The dot product tells us how much one vector points in the direction of another. Orthogonal basis vectors are valuable because each component can be found independently. If two basis vectors are orthogonal, their dot product is zero, so one direction does not contaminate the measurement of another.

Functions can be treated in the same spirit. A time signal can be thought of as a vector with infinitely many components, one value for every instant of time. Instead of projecting a spatial vector onto x, y, and z, we project a function onto basis functions. These basis functions may be sines, cosines, or complex exponentials. The dot product becomes an integral. If x(t) is a signal and ϕk​(t) is a candidate basis function, then the coefficient measuring how much of ϕk​(t) is present in x(t) is found by multiplying them and integrating over the relevant interval. If the positive and negative areas cancel, the coefficient is zero: that basis function is not present in the signal. If the net area is nonzero, that frequency contributes to the signal. This is the exact bridge from linear algebra to Fourier analysis.

For a periodic signal with period T, the fundamental angular frequency is

ω0​=T2π​

The Fourier series says that such a signal can be represented as a sum of harmonically related basis functions. In complex exponential form,

x(t)=k=−∞∑∞​ck​ejkω0​t

Here, ejkω0​t is the k-th harmonic basis function, and ck​ is its coefficient. The coefficient is found by projection:

ck​=T1​∫0T​x(t)e−jkω0​tdt

This equation should not be seen as a formula to memorize blindly. It is simply the function-space version of asking: “How much of this basis direction is inside my signal?” The multiplication compares the signal with the candidate harmonic; the integral sums the overlap over one period; the factor 1/T normalizes the result.

Euler’s formula,

ejθ=cos(θ)+jsin(θ)

explains why complex exponentials, sines, and cosines are different languages for the same idea. A periodic signal can also be written as

x(t)=a0​+k=1∑∞​[ak​cos(kω0​t)+bk​sin(kω0​t)]

The term a0​ is the DC component: the constant or average part of the signal. A constant signal has no time variation, so it has zero frequency. In the frequency domain, a constant amplitude corresponds to a delta function at zero frequency. This matters later because when a line code or waveform has a nonzero average value, we say it has a DC component, and in the spectrum we expect energy at zero frequency.

A square wave gives the intuition particularly well. If you multiply a square wave by a harmonic and integrate over a period, some harmonics contribute and some vanish. For certain even harmonics, the positive area cancels the negative area, so the coefficient is zero. Those harmonics are absent. The first harmonic usually contributes the most; higher contributing harmonics still matter, but their weights become smaller. This explains why adding more and more sinusoidal harmonics can reconstruct a sharper square wave: each harmonic adds a missing piece of the shape. It also explains why a frequency-domain plot is not a different signal. It is the same signal expressed in a different coordinate system. The time-domain waveform and the frequency-domain spectrum are two representations of the same object.

For non-periodic signals, we use the Fourier transform rather than the Fourier series. A periodic signal has discrete spectral lines because its frequencies occur at integer multiples of the fundamental frequency. A non-periodic signal generally has a continuous spectrum. The Fourier transform is

F(ω)=∫−∞∞​f(t)e−jωtdt

Again, the meaning is projection: compare the signal with every possible complex exponential frequency and record how much of each frequency is present. A rectangular pulse in time, for example, transforms into a sinc-shaped spectrum. This is one of the first major time-frequency trade-offs: a signal sharply limited in time spreads out in frequency. Conversely, a signal perfectly limited in frequency cannot be perfectly limited in time. A waveform can be time-limited or band-limited, but not strictly both. In practical engineering, however, we often call a signal band-limited if its spectral components above some frequency are small enough to neglect.

Decibels: ratios, absolute power, and the mistakes that destroy calculations

Communication engineering constantly compares powers: output power to input power, received power to transmitted power, antenna gain, amplifier gain, attenuation, noise figures, and link budgets. These comparisons often involve very large or very small numbers, so engineers use logarithmic units. The decibel, dB, is not itself a power. It is a ratio. For powers,

GdB​=10log10​(Pin​Pout​​)

If the output power is ten times the input power, the gain is 10dB. If the output power is twice the input power, then

10log10​(2)≈3dB

So a doubling of power is approximately a 3dB increase. If two power levels differ by 0dB, they are equal. If a dB value is negative, it does not mean “negative power.” It means the quantity is smaller than the reference quantity. A gain of −10dB means a factor of 0.1, not an impossible physical power.

Voltage ratios use 20log10​, not 10log10​, when the same impedance is assumed. The reason is that electrical power is proportional to voltage squared:

P∝V2

When the square enters the logarithm, the exponent becomes a factor of 2, turning 10log10​ into 20log10​. Therefore, a voltage ratio of 2:1 corresponds to approximately 6dB, while a power ratio of 2:1 corresponds to approximately 3dB. Confusing these two is one of the classic errors.

The distinction between dB and dBm is even more important. dB is a unitless ratio. dBm is an absolute power level referenced to 1mW:

PdBm​=10log10​(1mWP​)

Thus 0dBm is not zero power. It is 1mW. A power of 2mW is 3dBm because it is twice 1mW. A power of 0.1mW is −10dBm. Very weak received wireless signals are often expressed as negative dBm values, such as −70dBm, which simply means a very small positive power.

You may add dB gains to dBm powers because one is a ratio and the other is an absolute level. For example, 3dBm followed by 10dB of gain becomes 13dBm. But you cannot “convert dB to dBm.” That statement has no meaning because dB is a ratio and dBm is an absolute power relative to 1mW. You can convert between dBm and dBW because both are absolute power units with different references: 1mW for dBm and 1W for dBW. Since 1W equals 1000mW, 0dBW equals 30dBm. That is a conversion between two absolute power scales, not between dB and dBm.

A useful habit is always to return to linear units before adding physical powers. For example, 0dBm plus 0dBm does not equal 0dBm. Each 0dBm signal is 1mW, so together they make 2mW, which is 3dBm. The increase is 3dB because the power doubled, and the final absolute level is 3dBm because the result is 2mW relative to 1mW.

Sampling: capturing enough information without capturing everything

Now we can return to sampling with the correct language. Suppose an analog signal is a(t). The sampled signal will be called s(t). Ideal sampling is modeled by multiplying the original signal by a sampling function x(t). This sampling function is an infinite train of impulses separated by the sampling period Ts​:

x(t)=n=−∞∑∞​δ(t−nTs​)

The sampling frequency is

fs​=Ts​1​

Multiplying the analog signal by the impulse train keeps only the values at the sampling instants:

s(t)=a(t)x(t)=n=−∞∑∞​a(nTs​)δ(t−nTs​)

This expression says something subtle. The sampled signal is a sequence of impulses whose heights are the sampled values of the original signal. It is not yet a finite digital sequence. It is still a continuous-time mathematical object because the impulses are located on the continuous time axis. The amplitudes are still analog values. Quantization comes later.

The first practical question is: how close together must the sampling points be? The intuitive answer comes from thinking about the highest frequency hidden inside the signal. If a signal contains a sinusoidal component of 100Hz, then one period lasts 10ms. If we sample only once per period, the receiver receives too little information. It may connect the dots in a way that produces a completely different waveform. To recognize a sinusoid, at least two samples per cycle are needed. This gives the Nyquist criterion:

fs​≥2B

where B is the highest frequency component, or bandwidth, of the signal. If the signal contains frequencies only up to B, then sampling at at least 2B samples per second is sufficient in the ideal theory to reconstruct the signal exactly.

The frequency-domain explanation shows why this condition is not just a rule of thumb. Multiplication in the time domain becomes convolution in the frequency domain. Since ideal sampling multiplies a(t) by an impulse train, the spectrum of the sampled signal becomes repeated copies of the original spectrum. If the original spectrum is A(f), then ideal sampling creates copies of A(f) centered at multiples of the sampling frequency. In angular-frequency notation, the reader writes the sampled spectrum as

S(ω)=Ts​1​k=−∞∑∞​A(ω−kωs​)

where

ωs​=2πfs​

The important result is not the algebraic decoration; it is the picture. Sampling repeats the spectrum periodically in frequency. If these copies are far enough apart, the original copy around zero frequency can be isolated with a low-pass filter. If they overlap, the original spectrum is deformed, and no filter can recover what was lost.

That overlap is aliasing. Aliasing means that different frequency components have become indistinguishable after sampling. In the time domain, aliasing appears as the possibility of connecting the sample points with the wrong waveform. In the frequency domain, it appears as overlapping spectral replicas. These are not two different phenomena; they are two views of the same failure. Once aliasing has occurred, the information is no longer merely hidden. It is mixed into the wrong frequency region. Even if we apply a low-pass filter afterward, the spectrum inside the passband is already contaminated. There is then no way to reconstruct the original a(t) exactly.

If there is no aliasing, reconstruction is theoretically simple. Because the sampled spectrum contains repeated copies of the original spectrum, we select the central copy using an ideal low-pass filter. The filter must pass the original bandwidth and reject all the repeated spectra. Its amplitude must also compensate for the 1/Ts​ scaling introduced by impulse sampling. In the frequency domain, this ideal recovery filter is rectangular. In the time domain, its impulse response is sinc-shaped, and reconstruction becomes sinc interpolation:

a(t)=n=−∞∑∞​an​πfs​[t−fs​n​]sin(πfs​[t−fs​n​])​

This formula means that each sample contributes a shifted sinc function, and the original waveform is rebuilt by adding all those sinc functions together. This is a beautiful result: under ideal assumptions, the continuous-time signal is not approximated from its samples; it is exactly reconstructed from them.

But the assumptions matter. The ideal reconstruction filter is non-causal in the time domain, because the sinc function extends into both negative and positive time. A real-time system cannot use future samples before they arrive. The ideal rectangular filter also has infinitely sharp transitions in frequency, which is not physically realizable. Therefore, ideal sampling is a theorem about what is possible in principle, not a direct circuit implementation. Practical systems use approximations, such as sample-and-hold circuits, finite filters, oversampling, and digital signal processing techniques, accepting controlled imperfections.

The theory also explains why audio is commonly sampled around 44.1kHz or 48kHz. Human hearing is roughly limited to 20kHz, so Nyquist requires a sampling frequency somewhat above 40kHz. Sampling much lower causes audible aliasing and distortion. Sampling much higher may make engineering easier in some ways, but it does not create fundamentally new independent information about a strictly band-limited signal. Once we sample at the Nyquist rate, we already have enough independent samples for exact reconstruction in the ideal theory.

This last statement leads to the dimensionality theorem. If a real waveform is band-limited to bandwidth B and we want to describe it over a time interval T0​, then the number of independent pieces of information required is

N=2BT0​

This is not a random formula. Since Nyquist says we need 2B samples per second, over T0​ seconds we need 2BT0​ samples. The theorem says that the amount of independent information in a band-limited waveform is proportional to the time-bandwidth product. If we sample more densely than this, the additional samples may be useful for practical filtering or robustness, but they are not independent new degrees of freedom in the ideal mathematical sense.

The entire logic now closes. Digital communication begins with an analog world, but to use digital storage, computation, coding, error correction, and flexible transmission, we must first turn continuous-time information into a manageable representation. Fourier analysis tells us what frequencies are inside a signal. Decibel notation gives us a compact language for ratios and power levels. Sampling theory tells us how often we must observe the waveform so that its information is not lost. Nyquist tells us the minimum rate. Aliasing warns us what happens if we violate it. Sinc reconstruction shows why the samples can be enough. The dimensionality theorem tells us that “enough” has a precise meaning: for bandwidth B and duration T0​, the signal contains 2BT0​ independent pieces of information. Everything that follows in the communication chain—quantization, line coding, modulation, channel distortion, noise, decoding, and error correction—builds on this foundation.
