15.1 Double Integrals

Until now, integration has mostly meant adding a quantity along a one-dimensional interval. In one-variable calculus, the definite integral

∫ab​f(x)dx

adds the values of a function over the interval [a,b]. When f(x)≥0, this integral can be interpreted geometrically as the area under the graph y=f(x), above the x-axis, and between x=a and x=b. If part of the graph lies below the x-axis, that part contributes negatively, so the integral measures signed area rather than ordinary geometric area.

In multivariable calculus, the same idea appears one dimension higher. A function of two variables,

f(x,y),

does not assign a height to a point x on a line. It assigns a height to a point (x,y) in the plane. The immediate question is therefore no longer “What is the accumulated area under a curve?” but “What is the accumulated volume under a surface?” Double integrals are introduced to answer this question. They allow us to add the values of a scalar field over a two-dimensional region.

This topic appears at this point in the course because we have already studied functions of several variables, their domains, graphs, level curves, continuity, partial derivatives, gradients, and local approximations. Those topics describe local behaviour: how a function behaves near a point, how it changes in a direction, or how it can be approximated near a point. Double integrals begin the global part of the course. Instead of asking how f(x,y) changes near one point, we ask how much of f(x,y) accumulates over an entire planar region.

Let D be a region in the xy-plane. This region is called the domain of integration, meaning the part of the plane over which we want to add the values of the function. Suppose first that f(x,y)≥0 on D. The graph z=f(x,y) is a surface above the plane. If we draw vertical line segments upward from every point of D until they reach the surface, these vertical line segments fill a three-dimensional solid. The bottom of this solid is D, the top is the surface z=f(x,y), and the vertical sides lie above the boundary of D. In this positive case, the double integral

∬D​f(x,y)dA

is defined so that it gives the volume of this solid.

Here D is the planar region of integration, f(x,y) is the height of the surface above the point (x,y), and dA denotes a very small element of area in the xy-plane. Conceptually, the product

f(x,y)dA

means

height at (x,y)×tiny base area.

That product is the volume of a very thin vertical column. The double integral adds all these tiny column volumes over the whole region D.

If f(x,y) is sometimes negative, the interpretation becomes signed volume. Parts of the surface above the xy-plane contribute positively; parts below the xy-plane contribute negatively. This is the two-variable version of signed area in one-variable calculus. Therefore, a double integral is not automatically an ordinary positive volume. It is an accumulated signed quantity unless the integrand is known to be non-negative.

To define the double integral carefully, we begin with the simplest type of domain: a rectangle with sides parallel to the coordinate axes. Let

D=[a,b]×[c,d].

This means that D consists of all points (x,y) satisfying

a≤x≤b,c≤y≤d.

The numbers a and b are the left and right x-bounds of the rectangle, and c and d are the lower and upper y-bounds.

We partition the interval [a,b] into smaller x-intervals:

a=x0​<x1​<x2​<⋯<xm​=b,

and we partition the interval [c,d] into smaller y-intervals:

c=y0​<y1​<y2​<⋯<yn​=d.

Together these two partitions divide D into mn small rectangles. The rectangle in the i-th x-interval and the j-th y-interval is denoted by

Rij​=[xi−1​,xi​]×[yj−1​,yj​].

Here i identifies the horizontal x-subinterval, and j identifies the vertical y-subinterval.

The side lengths of Rij​ are

Δxi​=xi​−xi−1​,Δyj​=yj​−yj−1​.

Therefore its area is

ΔAij​=Δxi​Δyj​=(xi​−xi−1​)(yj​−yj−1​).

Here ΔAij​ is the area of the small rectangle Rij​. This is the two-dimensional replacement for the one-dimensional interval length Δx.

To measure how fine the whole partition is, we use the diagonal length of each small rectangle:

dij​=(Δxi​)2+(Δyj​)2​.

The number dij​ is the distance from one corner of Rij​ to the opposite corner. The norm of the partition, written ∣P∣, is the largest of these diagonal lengths:

∣P∣=1≤i≤m,1≤j≤nmax​dij​.

The symbol P denotes the whole partition. The number ∣P∣ tells us the size of the largest remaining rectangle. Saying ∣P∣→0 means that every rectangle in the partition is becoming small, so the approximation is being refined throughout the whole domain.

Now choose one sample point in each rectangle Rij​. Write that point as

(xij∗​,yij∗​)∈Rij​.

The star means that this is a chosen point inside the small rectangle, not necessarily a corner or the centre. At this point, the function value

f(xij∗​,yij∗​)

is used as the approximate height of the surface over the entire small rectangle Rij​. The approximate volume of the corresponding vertical column is therefore

f(xij∗​,yij∗​)ΔAij​.

Adding all these approximate column volumes gives the Riemann sum

R(f,P)=i=1∑m​j=1∑n​f(xij∗​,yij∗​)ΔAij​.

Here R(f,P) is the Riemann sum for the function f and the partition P. The outer sum runs through the x-subintervals, and the inner sum runs through the y-subintervals. The order of the two sums is not conceptually important at this stage; together they add one contribution for every small rectangle in the partition.

The idea is the same as in one-variable integration. If the rectangles are large, the column approximation may be rough. If the rectangles become smaller and smaller, then for a well-behaved function the surface does not vary much across any one rectangle, so the column approximation improves. The double integral is the limiting value of these Riemann sums, provided the limit does not depend on the particular sample points chosen.

Formally, we say that f is integrable over the rectangle D, and that

I=∬D​f(x,y)dA,

if for every ε>0 there exists a δ>0 such that

∣R(f,P)−I∣<ε

whenever ∣P∣<δ, for every possible choice of sample points (xij∗​,yij∗​) in the subrectangles. Here ε is the allowed error, δ is how fine the partition must be, R(f,P) is the Riemann sum, and I is the limiting value. This definition says that once the partition is fine enough, every possible Riemann sum must be close to the same number I. That number is the double integral.

The symbol dA is called the area element. It represents the limiting version of the small rectangle area ΔAij​. In Cartesian coordinates it can also be written as dxdy or dydx. At the level of the double integral itself, dA, dxdy, and dydx all refer to area in the xy-plane. The order becomes important only when the double integral is evaluated as repeated one-variable integrals.

A useful practical fact is that continuous functions on ordinary closed, bounded regions are integrable. The course also uses a slightly more flexible principle: a bounded function can still be integrable if its discontinuities occur only on a set of measure zero. A set of measure zero is a set with no area, such as a curve, a finite collection of curves, or isolated points in the plane. This matters because when we integrate over a non-rectangular domain, we often introduce an auxiliary function that jumps at the boundary of the domain. Since the boundary of an ordinary region is a curve and therefore has no area, such a jump usually does not destroy integrability.

Consider the square

D=[0,1]×[0,1]

and the function

f(x,y)=x2+y.

Divide D into four equal squares by the lines x=21​ and y=21​. Each small square has area

ΔA=21​⋅21​=41​.

Using the centre of each square as the sample point gives

(41​,41​),(41​,43​),(43​,41​),(43​,43​).

The Riemann sum is

R(f,P)=∑f(xij∗​,yij∗​)ΔA.

Substituting the four centre points gives

R(f,P)=[(41​)2+41​]41​+[(41​)2+43​]41​+[(43​)2+41​]41​+[(43​)2+43​]41​.

This simplifies to

R(f,P)=165​⋅41​+1613​⋅41​+1613​⋅41​+1621​⋅41​=6452​=1613​.

Thus this four-rectangle centre-point Riemann sum gives the approximation

∬D​(x2+y)dA≈1613​=0.8125.

This is not yet the exact integral. It is one approximation obtained from one particular partition and one particular choice of sample points. The exact double integral is the value approached as the partition becomes arbitrarily fine.

Most useful regions are not rectangles. A disk, a triangle, a region under a curve, or a region between two curves is not generally rectangular. To define a double integral over such a region, we place the region D inside a larger rectangle R. Then we define an extended function f~​ on all of R by

f~​(x,y)={f(x,y),0,​(x,y)∈D,(x,y)∈/D.​

Here f~​ agrees with f inside the domain D, and is set equal to zero in the part of the surrounding rectangle that lies outside D. The double integral over D is then defined by

∬D​f(x,y)dA=∬R​f~​(x,y)dA.

This works because the part of R outside D contributes nothing: the height there is defined to be zero. Geometrically, we have embedded the original solid into a rectangular base, but the added parts have zero height, so they add no volume.

The boundary of D is where f~​ may jump from f(x,y) to 0. For ordinary domains whose boundaries consist of finitely many curves of finite length, this boundary has no area. Therefore such jumps do not affect the value of the integral. This is why double integration over disks, triangles, and regions bounded by familiar curves is possible without redefining the whole theory from the beginning.

Several basic properties of double integrals follow from the idea of adding small signed volumes. These properties are not separate tricks; they express how accumulation behaves.

First, if D has zero area, then

∬D​f(x,y)dA=0.

A curve or a point may contain infinitely many or finitely many points, but it has no two-dimensional area, so there is no two-dimensional accumulation over it.

Second, the area of a domain can be written as a double integral:

area(D)=∬D​1dA.

The integrand 1 means that the height is constantly equal to 1. The resulting solid is a cylinder of height 1 above the base D, so its volume equals the base area. This formula is one of the most important practical uses of Section 15.1. If a problem asks for the area of a plane region using double integrals, the integrand should be 1. The hard part is then not the function being integrated, but the accurate description of the region D.

For example, suppose a region D is bounded by the curves

y=x2

and

y=x+2.

Before writing an integral, we must find where the curves meet. Their intersection points satisfy

x2=x+2.

Rearranging gives

x2−x−2=0,

so

(x−2)(x+1)=0.

Thus the curves meet at

x=−1andx=2.

Between these values, the line y=x+2 lies above the parabola y=x2, for instance at x=0, where the line has value 2 and the parabola has value 0. Therefore the region can be described as

D={(x,y):−1≤x≤2,x2≤y≤x+2}.

The area is then

area(D)=∬D​1dA.

At the level of Section 15.1, this formula expresses the correct meaning of the area as a double integral. The next section develops the systematic method for evaluating it as an iterated integral:

area(D)=∫−12​∫x2x+2​1dydx.

This example illustrates the typical workflow for area problems: sketch the region, find boundary intersections, determine which curve is above or below, describe D, and only then integrate 1.

Third, if f(x,y)≥0 on D, then

∬D​f(x,y)dA≥0,

and the integral is the ordinary volume under the surface z=f(x,y). If f(x,y)≤0 on D, then the integral is non-positive and represents signed volume below the xy-plane.

Fourth, double integrals are linear in the integrand. If f and g are integrable over D, and α and β are constants, then

∬D​(αf(x,y)+βg(x,y))dA=α∬D​f(x,y)dA+β∬D​g(x,y)dA.

Here α and β scale the functions before integration. The formula says that scaling and adding functions before integration gives the same result as scaling and adding their integrals afterward. Geometrically, multiplying a height function by α multiplies every column height by α, and adding two height functions adds the corresponding column heights.

Fifth, inequalities are preserved. If

f(x,y)≤g(x,y)

for every (x,y)∈D, then

∬D​f(x,y)dA≤∬D​g(x,y)dA.

This is natural: if one surface lies everywhere below another surface over the same base region, then its accumulated signed volume cannot be larger.

Sixth, the triangle inequality for double integrals is

​∬D​f(x,y)dA​≤∬D​∣f(x,y)∣dA.

The left-hand side first allows positive and negative contributions to cancel, and then takes the absolute value of the final result. The right-hand side first makes every contribution non-negative and then integrates. Since cancellation can only reduce the size of the final signed total, the inequality follows.

Finally, double integrals are additive over nonoverlapping domains. If D1​,D2​,…,Dk​ are domains that do not overlap in their interiors, and if

D=D1​∪D2​∪⋯∪Dk​,

then

∬D​f(x,y)dA=j=1∑k​∬Dj​​f(x,y)dA.

The domains may share boundary points, because boundaries have no area. The important condition is that their interiors do not overlap; otherwise some area would be counted more than once. This property lets us split complicated regions into simpler pieces.

Some double integrals can be evaluated without doing any antiderivative calculation. This is called evaluating by inspection. Inspection works when the integral can be recognized as a known area, a known volume, or a cancellation by symmetry. The assigned exercises from this section emphasize exactly this skill: before calculating, decide whether the integral is really asking for an area, a simple volume, or a cancellation pattern.

Let

R={(x,y):a≤x≤b, c≤y≤d}

be a rectangle. Then

∬R​3dA=3area(R)=3(b−a)(d−c).

Here the integrand 3 is a constant height. The solid is a rectangular box of height 3, base length b−a, and base width d−c. Therefore its volume is height times base area.

This example also shows how to interpret units. If x and y are measured in metres and f(x,y) is measured in kilograms per square metre, then f(x,y)dA has units of kilograms, and the double integral gives total mass. If f(x,y) is a height in metres, then f(x,y)dA has units of cubic metres, and the double integral gives volume.

Now consider

I=∬x2+y2≤1​(sinx+y3+4)dA.

The domain is the disk of radius 1 centred at the origin. By linearity,

I=∬D​sinxdA+∬D​y3dA+∬D​4dA,

where

D={(x,y):x2+y2≤1}.

The function sinx is odd in x, meaning that

sin(−x)=−sinx.

The disk is symmetric about the y-axis. For every point (x,y) in the disk, the reflected point (−x,y) is also in the disk. The contributions from these two points cancel because sinx has opposite values at them. Therefore

∬D​sinxdA=0.

Similarly, y3 is odd in y, and the disk is symmetric about the x-axis. For every point (x,y), the reflected point (x,−y) gives the opposite value of y3. Hence

∬D​y3dA=0.

The remaining term is constant:

∬D​4dA=4area(D).

Since D is the unit disk, its area is π. Thus

I=0+0+4π=4π.

The important warning is that symmetry cancellation requires two things at the same time. First, the integrand must change sign under reflection in one variable. Second, the domain must contain matching reflected points. Oddness alone is not enough. For example, an odd function in x will not necessarily integrate to zero over a region that lies only in the right half-plane.

A second inspection example uses a known volume. Let D again be the unit disk. The function

f(x,y)=1−x2−y2​

is the upper half of the sphere

x2+y2+z2=1.

Solving this sphere equation for z≥0 gives

z=1−x2−y2​.

Therefore

∬D​1−x2−y2​dA

is the volume of the upper hemisphere of radius 1. The volume of a sphere of radius 1 is

34​π,

so the volume of the upper hemisphere is

21​⋅34​π=32​π.

Thus

∬D​1−x2−y2​dA=32π​.

This example should be read carefully: we did not compute the integral by algebraic integration. We recognized the graph as a known surface and interpreted the integral as a known volume.

Known-volume recognition also applies to cones. Consider the function

f(x,y)=a−x2+y2​

over the disk

D={(x,y):x2+y2≤a2},

where a>0. The quantity x2+y2​ is the distance from the point (x,y) to the origin. At the centre of the disk, where (x,y)=(0,0), the height is

f(0,0)=a.

On the boundary of the disk, where x2+y2​=a, the height is

f(x,y)=a−a=0.

Thus the graph forms a cone of height a and base radius a. Therefore

∬D​(a−x2+y2​)dA

is the volume of this cone. The volume of a cone with base radius a and height a is

31​πa2⋅a=31​πa3.

Hence

∬D​(a−x2+y2​)dA=3πa3​.

This kind of problem is not mainly about integration technique. It is about recognizing what the integrand represents as a height above the domain.

It is useful to separate four common problem types in this section. If the problem asks for the area of a planar region, the integrand is 1, and the central task is describing the domain D. If the problem gives a non-negative function over a region, the double integral gives an ordinary volume under the graph. If the function has positive and negative parts, the double integral gives signed volume, so cancellation may occur. If the domain and integrand have symmetry, some terms may vanish without calculation. If the integrand and domain describe a familiar solid, such as a box, hemisphere, or cone, the integral may be found from known geometry.

A common mistake is to start calculating before deciding which of these situations applies. In Section 15.1, the first question should not be “Which antiderivative do I use?” The first question should be “What is being accumulated, and over which region?” Once that is clear, the integral may turn out to be an area, a volume, a signed cancellation, or a setup for a later iterated integral.

This distinction also explains the role of Section 15.2. Section 15.1 defines what the double integral means. It builds the integral from Riemann sums and explains its interpretation as accumulated signed volume or accumulated density over area. Section 15.2 then develops the main computational method: converting

∬D​f(x,y)dA

into repeated one-variable integrals with explicit bounds. Therefore, when a problem asks for a double integral by choosing an order of integration, the computation belongs mainly to the next section, but the ability to read the region D comes from the present section.

The double integral is therefore the two-dimensional version of accumulation. It begins with the same idea as the one-variable definite integral: cut the domain into small pieces, approximate the quantity on each piece, multiply by the size of the piece, and add. For functions of two variables, the pieces are small rectangles of area ΔA, the local contribution is f(x,y)ΔA, and the limiting sum is

∬D​f(x,y)dA.

Geometrically, this gives signed volume under a surface. Practically, it can represent area, mass, charge, or any quantity obtained by accumulating a density over a plane region. The essential skill in this section is to understand what the integral means before calculating it: identify the domain, identify the accumulated quantity, use area or symmetry when available, recognize known volumes when possible, and only then move toward systematic computation.
