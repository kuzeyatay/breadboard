10.5 Quadric Surfaces

The previous section introduced three-dimensional Cartesian space as a coordinate system in which a point is described by three numbers (x,y,z). Once points can be described, the next problem is to describe sets of points. In three-dimensional geometry, a single equation involving x, y, and z usually describes a surface: a two-dimensional object sitting inside three-dimensional space. A plane is the simplest example, because an equation such as

ax+by+cz=d

describes a flat surface. The purpose of this section is to understand the next level of complexity: surfaces described by equations containing squared terms such as x2, y2, and z2. These surfaces are curved, but their equations are still simple enough that their shapes can be recognized systematically.

A quadric surface is a surface in R3 described by a second-degree equation in the variables x, y, and z. The word “quadric” refers to the geometric surface, while “quadratic” refers to the algebraic fact that the equation has degree two. The most general second-degree equation in three variables has the form

Ax2+By2+Cz2+Dxy+Exz+Fyz+Gx+Hy+Iz=J.

Here x,y,z are the Cartesian coordinates of a point in space, and A,B,C,D,E,F,G,H,I,J are constants. The terms Ax2, By2, and Cz2 are pure squared terms. The terms Dxy, Exz, and Fyz are mixed terms, because they multiply two different coordinates. The terms Gx, Hy, and Iz are linear terms, and J is a constant. In this course, the main task is not to classify every rotated equation in its most general possible form. The practical task is to recognize the standard quadric surfaces after the equation has been simplified, usually by rearranging terms, completing squares, and comparing the result with standard forms.

The standard nondegenerate quadric surfaces fall into six main families: spheres, cylinders, cones, ellipsoids, paraboloids, and hyperboloids. The word nondegenerate means that the equation produces a genuine curved surface of the intended type, rather than collapsing into something simpler. For example, the equation

(A1​x+B1​y+C1​z−D1​)(A2​x+B2​y+C2​z−D2​)=0

is second degree, but it does not describe one new curved surface. A product is zero exactly when at least one of its factors is zero, so the graph is the union of the two planes

A1​x+B1​y+C1​z=D1​

and

A2​x+B2​y+C2​z=D2​.

This is called a degenerate case. It is algebraically quadratic, but geometrically it is made from planes. Separating such cases prevents a common mistake: not every second-degree equation automatically gives one of the usual curved shapes.

The first recognition idea is the cylinder idea. A cylinder in this section means any surface obtained by extending a plane curve parallel to a fixed direction. It does not have to be a circular tube. Algebraically, a cylinder usually appears when one coordinate is absent from the equation. For example,

x2+y2=a2

contains x and y, but not z. In the xy-plane, the equation describes a circle of radius a centered at the origin. Since z is not restricted, every point of that circle may be moved to any height. The result is a circular cylinder whose axis is parallel to the z-axis. Here a>0 is the radius of the circular cross-section.

The same principle gives other cylinders. The equation

a2x2​+b2y2​=1

describes an elliptic cylinder parallel to the z-axis, because its trace in the xy-plane is an ellipse and z is free. The constants a>0 and b>0 are the semi-axis lengths of the ellipse in the x- and y-directions. The equation

a2x2​−b2y2​=1

describes a hyperbolic cylinder parallel to the z-axis, because its xy-trace is a hyperbola. The equation

z=x2

describes a parabolic cylinder parallel to the y-axis, because y is absent and the curve z=x2 in the xz-plane is extended along the y-direction. Thus the immediate rule is: if one coordinate is missing, identify the two-dimensional curve in the remaining coordinate plane, then extend that curve parallel to the missing coordinate axis.

The second recognition idea is the trace method. A trace is the curve obtained by intersecting a surface with a plane. To find the trace in the plane z=k, where k is a constant, replace z by k in the equation of the surface. The result is an equation in x and y, so it describes a plane curve in that horizontal slice. Traces are useful because they turn a three-dimensional surface into a family of familiar two-dimensional curves. For quadric surfaces, these curves are usually conic sections: circles, ellipses, parabolas, or hyperbolas.

For example, if a surface has equation

x2+y2=z2,

then the trace in z=k is

x2+y2=k2.

For k=0, this gives only the point (0,0,0). For k=0, it gives a circle of radius ∣k∣. The circular traces grow as the plane moves away from z=0, so the surface is a double cone. The trace method explains the geometry without relying on memorized pictures.

A cone is a surface made of straight lines passing through a common point called the vertex. The simplest circular cone with vertex at the origin and axis along the z-axis is

z2=x2+y2.

This equation can also be written as

x2+y2=z2.

At height z=k, the horizontal trace is x2+y2=k2, a circle of radius ∣k∣. This shows two important features. First, the radius is zero when k=0, so the cone narrows to a vertex at the origin. Second, the radius grows in direct proportion to ∣k∣, so the sides are straight rather than curved like a paraboloid.

A more general right circular cone with axis along the z-axis is

x2+y2=a2z2.

Here a>0 controls the opening of the cone. In the vertical plane y=0, the equation becomes

x2=a2z2,

so

x=±az.

Thus the cone is generated by rotating the straight lines x=az and x=−az around the z-axis. The semi-vertical angle of a cone is the angle between the cone’s axis and one of its straight side lines. If this angle is called α, then

tanα=a.

Therefore

α=tan−1a.

The variable α is the semi-vertical angle, and a is the slope factor in the equation x2+y2=a2z2. This formula means that a larger a gives a wider cone.

An elliptic cone has unequal scaling in the x- and y-directions. A standard form is

a2x2​+b2y2​=c2z2​,

where a,b,c>0. At z=k, the trace is

a2x2​+b2y2​=c2k2​.

For k=0, this is an ellipse. For k=0, it collapses to the vertex. The surface is still a cone because the cross-sections scale linearly as one moves away from the vertex, but the cross-sections are ellipses rather than circles.

A sphere is the set of points at a fixed distance from a fixed center. The sphere of radius r>0 centered at (x0​,y0​,z0​) has equation

(x−x0​)2+(y−y0​)2+(z−z0​)2=r2.

The variables x,y,z describe an arbitrary point on the sphere. The constants x0​,y0​,z0​ describe the center. The constant r is the radius. The equation follows directly from the distance formula: the distance from (x,y,z) to (x0​,y0​,z0​) is

(x−x0​)2+(y−y0​)2+(z−z0​)2​,

and requiring this distance to equal r gives the sphere equation after squaring both sides. For example, the sphere of radius r>0 centered at (1,2,3) is

(x−1)2+(y−2)2+(z−3)2=r2.

An ellipsoid is a sphere stretched or compressed along the coordinate axes. Its standard centered equation is

a2x2​+b2y2​+c2z2​=1.

The constants a,b,c>0 are the semi-axis lengths. The surface reaches x=±a on the x-axis, y=±b on the y-axis, and z=±c on the z-axis. This is seen by setting the other two coordinates equal to zero. For example, if y=0 and z=0, then x2/a2=1, so x=±a. If a=b=c, then the ellipsoid becomes a sphere.

A shifted ellipsoid centered at (x0​,y0​,z0​) has equation

a2(x−x0​)2​+b2(y−y0​)2​+c2(z−z0​)2​=1.

The shifts x−x0​, y−y0​, and z−z0​ reveal the center. The denominators reveal the stretching. This is why completing the square is not merely an algebraic trick; it uncovers the geometry hidden in the equation.

Consider the equation

x2+y2+2z2=2y−8z.

To classify it, first move all terms to one side:

x2+y2−2y+2z2+8z=0.

Now complete the square in y and z. Since

y2−2y=(y−1)2−1

and

2z2+8z=2(z2+4z)=2((z+2)2−4)=2(z+2)2−8,

the equation becomes

x2+(y−1)2−1+2(z+2)2−8=0.

Therefore

x2+(y−1)2+2(z+2)2=9.

Dividing by 9 gives

9x2​+9(y−1)2​+9/2(z+2)2​=1.

This is an ellipsoid centered at (0,1,−2). Its semi-axis lengths are 3 in the x-direction, 3 in the y-direction, and 3/2​ in the z-direction. The important lesson is that the original equation did not visibly display the ellipsoid, but completing the square changed it into a recognizable standard form.

A paraboloid is a quadric surface in which one coordinate occurs linearly and the other two occur quadratically. The elliptic paraboloid has standard form

z−z0​=a2(x−x0​)2​+b2(y−y0​)2​,

where a,b>0. The point (x0​,y0​,z0​) is the vertex. The right-hand side is always nonnegative, so z≥z0​. This means the surface opens upward in the z-direction. If the signs are reversed, it opens downward.

The traces explain the shape. In the plane z=k, the equation becomes

a2(x−x0​)2​+b2(y−y0​)2​=k−z0​.

If k<z0​, there are no points, because the left-hand side is nonnegative while the right-hand side is negative. If k=z0​, the trace is only the vertex. If k>z0​, the trace is an ellipse. Thus the surface is built from ellipses that expand as z increases. If a=b, these traces are circles, and the surface is a circular paraboloid. Parabolic mirrors have this circular paraboloid shape because all vertical sections through the axis are parabolas.

The hyperbolic paraboloid has standard form

z−z0​=a2(x−x0​)2​−b2(y−y0​)2​.

This surface is often called a saddle. The reason comes directly from the two main vertical traces. If y=y0​, then

z−z0​=a2(x−x0​)2​,

which is an upward-opening parabola. If x=x0​, then

z−z0​=−b2(y−y0​)2​,

which is a downward-opening parabola. Thus the surface bends upward in one coordinate direction and downward in the other.

The horizontal traces are hyperbolas. Setting z=k gives

a2(x−x0​)2​−b2(y−y0​)2​=k−z0​.

Depending on the sign of k−z0​, the hyperbola opens in one coordinate direction or the other. This is why the sign between the two squared terms is decisive. In an elliptic paraboloid, the squared terms have the same sign and produce a bowl. In a hyperbolic paraboloid, the squared terms have opposite signs and produce a saddle.

The hyperbolic paraboloid is also a ruled surface. A ruled surface is a surface through every point of which there passes at least one straight line lying entirely on the surface. This is surprising because the surface looks curved, but it contains straight lines. For the standard hyperbolic paraboloid

z=a2x2​−b2y2​,

factor the right-hand side:

z=(ax​−by​)(ax​+by​).

Let

p=ax​−by​,q=ax​+by​.

Then the surface equation is z=pq. If λ=0 is fixed and we impose

p=λz,q=λ1​,

then pq=z, so the resulting points lie on the surface. Written out, this family is

ax​−by​=λz,ax​+by​=λ1​.

These are two linear equations in x,y,z, so their intersection is a straight line. Varying λ gives one family of straight lines on the surface. A second family is obtained by fixing μ=0 and imposing

ax​+by​=μz,ax​−by​=μ1​.

Every point of the hyperbolic paraboloid lies on one line from each family. Cylinders and cones are also ruled surfaces: cylinders are swept out by parallel straight lines, and cones are swept out by straight lines through the vertex.

A hyperboloid is a quadric surface with squared terms of opposite signs and a nonzero constant. A hyperboloid of one sheet aligned with the z-axis has standard form

a2x2​+b2y2​−c2z2​=1,

where a,b,c>0. The phrase “one sheet” means that the surface is connected in one continuous piece. To see this, take the horizontal trace z=k. Substituting z=k gives

a2x2​+b2y2​=1+c2k2​.

The right-hand side is positive for every real k. Therefore every horizontal plane intersects the surface in an ellipse. The smallest ellipse occurs at z=0, and the ellipses grow as ∣z∣ increases. Since there is no missing band of heights, the surface forms one connected sheet.

The equation

a2x2​+b2y2​=z2+1

is a hyperboloid of one sheet. Moving the z2 term to the left gives

a2x2​+b2y2​−z2=1.

This matches the one-sheet pattern: two positive squared terms, one negative squared term, and a positive constant on the right. For fixed z=k, the trace is

a2x2​+b2y2​=1+k2,

which is an ellipse for every k. This is the immediate algebraic reason that the surface has one sheet.

A shifted version appears in the equation

x2−2x+y2=z2.

Complete the square in x:

x2−2x=(x−1)2−1.

Then

(x−1)2−1+y2=z2,

so

(x−1)2+y2=z2+1.

Equivalently,

(x−1)2+y2−z2=1.

This is a hyperboloid of one sheet shifted one unit in the positive x-direction. Its central axis is the line x=1, y=0, parallel to the z-axis.

This same equation also shows how to parametrize a bounded part of the surface. Suppose

S={(x,y,z)∈R3:x2−2x+y2=z2, 0≤z≤1}.

After completing the square,

(x−1)2+y2=1+z2.

For each fixed height z, the cross-section is a circle centered at (1,0,z) with radius 1+z2​. Let u be the angle around this circle and let v be the height. Then a suitable parametrization is

r(u,v)=(1+1+v2​cosu)i+(1+v2​sinu)j+vk,

with bounds

0≤u<2π,0≤v≤1.

Here u controls the circular direction around the surface, and v controls the height. This parametrization is not a new type of geometry; it is simply the trace description written as coordinates.

The hyperboloid of one sheet is also a ruled surface. Although it has elliptical horizontal traces, it contains straight lines lying entirely on it. This matters geometrically because it shows that a surface can be curved as a whole while still being built from straight-line generators. The basic recognition of the one-sheet hyperboloid still comes from its equation and traces; the ruled-surface property adds a structural detail about how the surface can be formed.

A hyperboloid of two sheets aligned with the z-axis can be written as

a2x2​+b2y2​−c2z2​=−1.

This equation is close to the one-sheet equation, but the geometry is different. Rearrange it as

a2x2​+b2y2​=c2z2​−1.

In the horizontal plane z=k, the trace is

a2x2​+b2y2​=c2k2​−1.

The left-hand side is always nonnegative, so the right-hand side must be nonnegative. Therefore

c2k2​−1≥0,

which means

∣k∣≥c.

There are no points for −c<z<c. The surface exists only above z=c and below z=−c, giving two disconnected pieces. This is why the surface has two sheets.

The one-sheet and two-sheet hyperboloids are easy to confuse because their equations differ only in the sign of the constant. The trace test prevents the mistake. In the one-sheet case, the horizontal trace has right-hand side 1+k2/c2, which is always positive. In the two-sheet case, the horizontal trace has right-hand side k2/c2−1, which is negative near k=0. The gap near the middle is the algebraic reason for the two separate sheets.

Both hyperboloids are asymptotic to an elliptic cone. To say that a surface is asymptotic to another surface means that, far from the origin, the two surfaces get closer in shape and direction, even though they are not the same surface. For both equations

a2x2​+b2y2​−c2z2​=1

and

a2x2​+b2y2​−c2z2​=−1,

the constant 1 or −1 becomes small compared with the squared terms when ∣x∣, ∣y∣, and ∣z∣ are large. The corresponding asymptotic cone is obtained by replacing the constant by 0:

a2x2​+b2y2​−c2z2​=0.

Equivalently,

a2x2​+b2y2​=c2z2​.

This cone gives the large-scale outline approached by both types of hyperboloid.

The relationship between conic sections and quadric surfaces can now be stated clearly. A conic section is a curve in a plane described by a second-degree equation in two variables. A quadric surface is a surface in space described by a second-degree equation in three variables. When a plane cuts a quadric surface, the intersection is usually a conic section. For example, horizontal traces of an ellipsoid are ellipses, horizontal traces of a cone are ellipses or circles, and horizontal traces of a hyperboloid are ellipses when the axis is vertical. Other cutting planes may produce parabolas or hyperbolas. Thus quadric surfaces are three-dimensional objects whose planar slices reveal the familiar conic curves.

A useful course example is the intersection of the ellipsoid

x2+2y2+3z2=6

with the plane

x=y.

To find the intersection, substitute y=x into the ellipsoid:

x2+2x2+3z2=6.

Thus

3x2+3z2=6,

so

x2+z2=2.

Since y=x, the curve can be parametrized by

x=2​cost,y=2​cost,z=2​sint,

where

0≤t<2π.

Here t is an angular parameter. The parametrization stays in the plane x=y because the first two coordinates are equal, and it stays on the ellipsoid because substituting the coordinates gives

(2​cost)2+2(2​cost)2+3(2​sint)2=2cos2t+4cos2t+6sin2t=6.

At first glance, x2+z2=2 looks like a circle. However, the actual curve lies in the tilted plane x=y, not in the xz-plane. In the coordinates of the tilted plane, the curve is an ellipse. This distinction matters: an algebraic substitution may make an equation look circular in two symbols, but the actual geometric distances must be interpreted in the plane where the curve lies.

Another useful problem pattern is recognizing a solid bounded by a hyperboloid. Consider

D={(x,y,z)∈R3:a2x2​+b2y2​≤1+z2, −1≤z≤1},

where a,b>0. The boundary

a2x2​+b2y2​=1+z2

is a one-sheet hyperboloid. For each fixed z=w, the cross-section is an ellipse whose size depends on 1+w2​. This motivates the substitution

x=au1+w2​cosv, y=bu1+w2​sinv, z=w.

Here w is the height, v is the angular coordinate around the ellipse, and u is a radial scaling variable inside the elliptical cross-section. The fixed bounds are

0≤u≤1,0≤v<2π,−1≤w≤1.

This example shows why recognizing the quadric is not a decorative skill. Once the surface is recognized, the correct parameters and bounds become natural.

A final way to organize the classification is by algebraic patterns. If one variable is missing, expect a cylinder. If all three variables are squared and the signs are the same, expect an ellipsoid or sphere after scaling and shifting. If all terms are squared and the equation has zero constant after rearrangement, expect a cone when the signs are mixed. If one variable is linear and two are squared, expect a paraboloid. If all three variables are squared, the signs are mixed, and the constant is nonzero, expect a hyperboloid. Completing squares handles shifts; traces confirm the shape.

The section fits naturally at this point in the course because three-dimensional calculus constantly uses surfaces as domains, boundaries, graphs, and coordinate surfaces. But its immediate role is simpler and more concrete: given an equation, decide what surface it describes, where it is centered, how it opens or extends, and how its slices look. Quadric surfaces are therefore the first systematic dictionary between algebraic equations in x,y,z and geometric objects in space.
