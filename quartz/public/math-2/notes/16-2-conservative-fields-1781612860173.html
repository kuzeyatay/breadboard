16.2 Conservative Fields

A vector field assigns a vector to each point of a domain. In physical language, the vector may describe a force, an electric field, or a velocity. In mathematical language, it is a function whose output is a vector rather than a scalar. The central question in this section is whether such a vector field can be described more economically by a single scalar function. Instead of storing a full vector at every point, can we store one number at every point and recover the vector field by differentiating that number in space?

This question appears naturally after the gradient. The gradient turns a scalar field into a vector field. If φ(x,y,z) is a scalar function, then its gradient is

∇φ(x,y,z)=∂x∂φ​i+∂y∂φ​j+∂z∂φ​k.

Here φ assigns one real number to each point, while ∇φ assigns a vector to each point. The vector ∇φ points in the direction in which φ increases fastest, and its length gives the maximum rate of increase. Conservative fields ask the reverse question: given a vector field F, can we find a scalar field φ such that F is exactly the gradient of φ?

A vector field F on a domain D is called conservative if there exists a scalar function φ:D→R such that

F=∇φon D.

The function φ is called a scalar potential, or simply a potential, for the vector field. In three dimensions, if

F(x,y,z)=F1​(x,y,z)i+F2​(x,y,z)j+F3​(x,y,z)k,

then the equation F=∇φ means

F1​=∂x∂φ​,F2​=∂y∂φ​,F3​=∂z∂φ​.

This is a strong requirement. A general vector field has several component functions, and those components may vary independently. A conservative field is special because all its components come from differentiating one underlying scalar function.

The potential is not unique. If φ is a potential for F, then φ+C is also a potential for every constant C, because

∇(φ+C)=∇φ.

This is the multivariable version of the fact that an antiderivative in one-variable calculus is determined only up to an additive constant. If the domain has several disconnected pieces, the additive constant may even be chosen separately on each connected component, because there is no path inside the domain connecting those pieces.

A useful physical convention must be separated from the mathematical definition. In this section, a conservative field is usually written as

F=∇φ.

In many physics problems, especially force-field problems, the force is instead written as

F=−∇U,

where U is potential energy. The minus sign means that the force points in the direction in which potential energy decreases fastest. For example, if a spring has potential energy

U(x)=21​kx2,

then

F(x)=−dxdU​=−kx.

The force points back toward x=0, where the potential energy is smaller. The difference between F=∇φ and F=−∇U is only a sign convention: if a force satisfies F=−∇U, then it also satisfies F=∇φ with φ=−U. In exam problems, the sign in the question matters. If the question asks for U such that F=−∇U, then the components of −F, not F, must be integrated to find U.

The simplest conservative fields are obtained directly by differentiating a given potential. For example, let

φ(x,y,z)=(a2x2​+b2y2​+c2z2​)1/2,a,b,c>0.

Define

S(x,y,z)=a2x2​+b2y2​+c2z2​.

Then φ=S1/2. By the chain rule,

∂x∂φ​=21​S−1/2a22x​=a2S​x​.

Similarly,

∂y∂φ​=b2S​y​,∂z∂φ​=c2S​z​.

Therefore the conservative field generated by this potential is

∇φ=a2S​x​i+b2S​y​j+c2S​z​k.

This direction, from potential to field, is mechanically straightforward: compute the gradient. The harder direction is the reverse direction: given a vector field, decide whether a potential exists and then find it.

To understand the test for conservativeness, begin in two dimensions. Suppose

F(x,y)=F1​(x,y)i+F2​(x,y)j

is conservative, so F=∇φ. Then

F1​=∂x∂φ​,F2​=∂y∂φ​.

If the relevant second partial derivatives are continuous, then mixed partial derivatives of φ are equal:

∂y∂x∂2φ​=∂x∂y∂2φ​.

Therefore,

∂y∂F1​​=∂x∂F2​​.

This is a necessary condition for a plane vector field to be conservative. It says that the way the first component changes in the y-direction must agree with the way the second component changes in the x-direction.

In three dimensions, if

F=F1​i+F2​j+F3​k

is conservative, then the corresponding necessary conditions are

∂y∂F1​​=∂x∂F2​​,∂z∂F1​​=∂x∂F3​​,∂z∂F2​​=∂y∂F3​​.

These equations say that the cross-partial derivatives of the vector-field components must match. The same condition can be expressed by saying that the Jacobian matrix DF is symmetric. The Jacobian matrix of a vector field is the matrix containing all first partial derivatives of its component functions:

DF=​∂x∂F1​​∂x∂F2​​∂x∂F3​​​∂y∂F1​​∂y∂F2​​∂y∂F3​​​∂z∂F1​​∂z∂F2​​∂z∂F3​​​​.

A matrix is symmetric when entries mirrored across the main diagonal are equal. In this context, symmetry is exactly the system of equalities above.

The partial-derivative test must not be used without thinking about the domain. A domain is connected if any two points in it can be joined by a curve that stays inside the domain. A connected domain is simply connected if every closed curve in the domain can be continuously shrunk to a point without leaving the domain. Intuitively, a simply connected domain has no holes. On a simply connected domain, for a continuously differentiable vector field in two or three dimensions, the matching partial-derivative conditions are enough to conclude that the field is conservative. On a domain with a hole, the same derivative conditions may not be enough.

A basic nonconservative example is the rotational field

F(x,y)=ω(−yi+xj),

where ω is a constant. Its components are

F1​(x,y)=−ωy,F2​(x,y)=ωx.

Then

∂y∂F1​​=−ω,∂x∂F2​​=ω.

These are equal only if ω=0. Therefore a genuine rotational field, with ω=0, is not conservative. Geometrically, this agrees with the picture: the field circulates around the origin rather than arising as the gradient of a scalar height function.

The domain warning is subtler. A hole in the domain does not automatically mean that a field is not conservative. It only means that the simple partial-derivative test is no longer sufficient by itself. Compare the two fields

F(x,y)=x2+y2xi−yj​,(x,y)=(0,0),

and

G(x,y)=x2+y2xi+yj​,(x,y)=(0,0).

Both are defined on the punctured plane R2∖{(0,0)}, which has a hole at the origin.

For F,

F1​=x2+y2x​,F2​=x2+y2−y​.

The cross-partials are

∂y∂F1​​=(x2+y2)2−2xy​,

and

∂x∂F2​​=(x2+y2)22xy​.

These are not equal in general, so F is not conservative. This conclusion follows before any deeper domain discussion is needed.

For G,

G1​=x2+y2x​,G2​=x2+y2y​.

This field is conservative, even though the domain has a hole, because it has the explicit potential

φ(x,y)=21​ln(x2+y2).

Indeed,

∂x∂φ​=21​⋅x2+y22x​=x2+y2x​,

and

∂y∂φ​=21​⋅x2+y22y​=x2+y2y​.

Thus ∇φ=G. This example prevents a common mistake: a non-simply connected domain does not automatically destroy conservativeness. It only prevents the cross-partial test from being a complete guarantee.

The standard example of a field that passes the cross-partial test locally but still fails to have a global potential on the punctured plane is

H(x,y)=x2+y2−y​i+x2+y2x​j,(x,y)=(0,0).

This field satisfies

∂y∂H1​​=∂x∂H2​​

on its domain, but it has no single-valued potential on the whole punctured plane. Locally it behaves like the gradient of the polar angle θ, but θ cannot be chosen continuously as one single-valued function all the way around the origin. After one full turn, the angle has changed by 2π. The hole prevents a global potential from existing.

Once a field passes the appropriate conservative-field test, the potential is found by integrating one component at a time. The crucial point is that when integrating with respect to one variable, the “constant” of integration may depend on the other variables.

For example, consider

F(x,y,z)=(2xy−z2)i+(2yz+x2)j−(2zx−y2)k.

First check the compatibility conditions. The components are

F1​=2xy−z2,F2​=2yz+x2,F3​=−(2zx−y2)=−2zx+y2.

Now

∂y∂F1​​=2x=∂x∂F2​​, ∂z∂F1​​=−2z=∂x∂F3​​,

and

∂z∂F2​​=2y=∂y∂F3​​.

The field is defined on all of R3, which is simply connected, so these equalities show that the field is conservative.

To find φ, start from

∂x∂φ​=2xy−z2.

Integrating with respect to x gives

φ(x,y,z)=x2y−xz2+C1​(y,z).

The term C1​(y,z) may depend on y and z, because it disappears when differentiating with respect to x. Now use the second component:

∂y∂φ​=x2+∂y∂C1​​=2yz+x2.

Therefore,

∂y∂C1​​=2yz,

so

C1​(y,z)=y2z+C2​(z).

Thus

φ(x,y,z)=x2y−xz2+y2z+C2​(z).

Finally use the third component:

∂z∂φ​=−2xz+y2+C2′​(z).

This must equal

F3​=−2zx+y2.

Hence C2′​(z)=0, so C2​ is constant. A potential is therefore

φ(x,y,z)=x2y+y2z−xz2+C.​

This is the main constructive method: integrate one component, keep a function of the remaining variables, and use the other components to determine that function.

A particularly simple three-dimensional example is

F(x,y,z)=yzi+zxj+xyk.

The components are

F1​=yz,F2​=zx,F3​=xy.

The mixed partial derivatives match:

∂y∂F1​​=z=∂x∂F2​​, ∂z∂F1​​=y=∂x∂F3​​,

and

∂z∂F2​​=x=∂y∂F3​​.

Since the field is defined on all of R3, it is conservative. A potential is easy to recognize:

φ(x,y,z)=xyz+C.​

Indeed,

∇(xyz)=yzi+xzj+xyk.

This example also connects conservative fields to field lines. A field line is a curve whose tangent direction at each point is parallel to the vector field at that point. For a vector field

F=F1​i+F2​j+F3​k,

field lines may be described by

F1​dx​=F2​dy​=F3​dz​,

where this notation means that the curve’s tangent vector is proportional to the field vector. For

F=yzi+zxj+xyk,

the field-line equations are

yzdx​=zxdy​=xydz​.

Comparing the first two fractions gives

yzdx​=zxdy​.

Cross-multiplying gives

zxdx=yzdy.

Away from special zero cases, this simplifies to

xdx=ydy.

Integrating,

21​x2=21​y2+C,

so

x2−y2=C1​.

Similarly, comparing the first and third fractions gives

x2−z2=C2​.

Therefore the field line through (x0​,y0​,z0​) is contained in the intersection of the two surfaces

x2−y2=x02​−y02​,

and

x2−z2=x02​−z02​.

This illustrates the difference between a potential and field lines: the potential φ=xyz gives scalar level surfaces, while the field lines are curves tangent to ∇φ.

Equipotential curves and surfaces describe where the potential has the same value. If F=∇φ, then a surface

φ(x,y,z)=C

is called an equipotential surface. In two dimensions, a curve

φ(x,y)=C

is called an equipotential curve. The word “equipotential” means that every point on the same curve or surface has the same value of the potential.

Equipotential surfaces are perpendicular to field lines. To see this, suppose a curve r(t) lies on an equipotential surface. Then

φ(r(t))=C

is constant. Differentiating with respect to t gives

dtd​φ(r(t))=∇φ(r(t))⋅r′(t)=0.

Here r′(t) is tangent to the curve lying on the equipotential surface. Since ∇φ=F, the vector field is perpendicular to tangent directions on the equipotential surface. Therefore the field lines cross equipotential surfaces at right angles, except at points where F=0, because there the field has no definite direction.

This gives a quick way to find a normal vector to an equipotential surface. If

F=∇φ,

then F is normal to the surface

φ(x,y,z)=C.

At a point where F=0, a unit normal vector is

N=±∣F∣F​.

The sign depends on which orientation is requested. If no orientation is specified, both directions are normal.

For example, for

F(x,y)=xi−yj,

a potential is

φ(x,y)=21​x2−21​y2+C.

The equipotential curves are

21​x2−21​y2=C,

or equivalently

x2−y2=C1​.

These are hyperbolas. The field lines are found from

xdx​=−ydy​.

Integrating gives

ln∣x∣=−ln∣y∣+C,

so

xy=C2​.

Thus the equipotential curves are x2−y2=C1​, while the field lines are xy=C2​. The two families are not the same; they intersect orthogonally. This distinction is important. Field lines show the direction of the vector field. Equipotential curves show where the scalar potential is constant.

Conservative fields can also be recognized in polar coordinates, but the test looks different because the polar unit vectors and scale factors are different. In plane polar coordinates, the gradient of a scalar field φ(r,θ) is

∇φ=∂r∂φ​r+r1​∂θ∂φ​θ.

Here r is the unit vector pointing radially outward, and θ is the unit vector in the direction of increasing angle. Suppose a polar vector field is written as

F(r,θ)=Fr​(r,θ)r+Fθ​(r,θ)θ.

If this field is conservative, then

Fr​=∂r∂φ​,Fθ​=r1​∂θ∂φ​.

The second equation is equivalent to

rFθ​=∂θ∂φ​.

Now differentiate the first equation with respect to θ:

∂θ∂Fr​​=∂θ∂r∂2φ​.

Differentiate the equation rFθ​=∂φ/∂θ with respect to r:

∂r∂​(rFθ​)=∂r∂θ∂2φ​.

Since the mixed partial derivatives agree,

∂θ∂Fr​​=∂r∂​(rFθ​).

Expanding the right-hand side gives

∂θ∂Fr​​=Fθ​+r∂r∂Fθ​​.

Therefore a necessary polar-coordinate condition for conservativeness is

∂θ∂Fr​​=r∂r∂Fθ​​+Fθ​.​

This formula is a warning against applying the Cartesian test blindly in polar form. In Cartesian coordinates, one compares partial derivatives of Cartesian components. In polar coordinates, the components are measured along r and θ, and the factor r in the gradient changes the condition.

There is also a differential notation for the same idea. If

F=F1​i+F2​j+F3​k,

then the expression

F1​dx+F2​dy+F3​dz

is called a differential form of degree one in this context. If F=∇φ, then

dφ=∂x∂φ​dx+∂y∂φ​dy+∂z∂φ​dz=F1​dx+F2​dy+F3​dz.

In that case, the differential form is called exact. Thus, saying that F is conservative is equivalent to saying that F1​dx+F2​dy+F3​dz is the exact differential of a potential φ. This is the multivariable analogue of recognizing an expression as the derivative of something simpler.

Many physical conservative fields arise from inverse-distance potentials. Let x be the position vector of the point where the field is being measured, and let x0​ be the position of a point source, mass, or charge. A typical potential has the form

φ(x)=∣x−x0​∣Cq​,

where q is the strength of the source, C is a constant depending on the physical model and units, and ∣x−x0​∣ is the distance from the source point. Differentiating this potential gives a field proportional to

∣x−x0​∣3x−x0​​,

possibly with a minus sign depending on the convention. The magnitude of this vector is proportional to

∣x−x0​∣21​.

This is why inverse-distance potentials generate inverse-square fields. The singular source point x=x0​ must be excluded from the domain because the formula is undefined there.

If there are several sources or charges, potentials add. For point sources at x1​,…,xn​, a combined potential may be written as

φ(x)=i=1∑n​∣x−xi​∣Cqi​​.

Because the gradient is linear,

∇(i=1∑n​φi​)=i=1∑n​∇φi​.

Therefore the total field is the sum of the individual fields. This is one of the practical advantages of potentials: it is often easier to add scalar potentials first and then differentiate than to reason directly about the full vector field.

A source is a point from which field lines move outward. A sink is a point toward which field lines move inward. A dipole consists of a source and a sink of equal strength placed close together. The product of the source strength and the separation distance is called the dipole moment. Dipoles are useful because they show that even complicated-looking field-line patterns can come from scalar potentials. The key point for this section is not the full physical theory of dipoles, but the method: identify or construct the potential, then obtain the vector field by taking its gradient.

Conservative fields also simplify line integrals. A line integral of a vector field along a parametrized curve

r(t)=x(t)i+y(t)j+z(t)k,a≤t≤b,

is

∫C​F⋅dr=∫ab​F(r(t))⋅r′(t)dt.

This integral measures the accumulated tangential component of the vector field along the curve. If F is a force field, it represents work.

If F=∇φ, then the chain rule gives

dtd​φ(r(t))=∇φ(r(t))⋅r′(t)=F(r(t))⋅r′(t).

Therefore

∫C​F⋅dr=∫ab​dtd​φ(r(t))dt=φ(r(b))−φ(r(a)).

If C starts at P and ends at Q, this becomes

∫C​F⋅dr=φ(Q)−φ(P).​

Thus, for a conservative field, the vector line integral depends only on the endpoints, not on the path. If the curve is closed, then P=Q, and

∮C​F⋅dr=0.​

This is the mathematical form of zero net work around a closed loop. It is also a practical shortcut: if a field is known to be conservative and a potential is known, do not compute a complicated parametrized line integral unless the problem explicitly asks for a direct computation. Use the potential difference.

The main ideas of this section fit together as follows. A conservative field is a vector field generated by the gradient of a scalar potential. To test conservativeness, compare cross-partials, but always pay attention to the domain. To find a potential, integrate one component at a time, remembering that integration constants may depend on the other variables. Equipotential curves and surfaces are level sets of the potential, and the vector field is normal to them. Field lines follow the vector field and therefore cross equipotentials at right angles. In physical force problems, the sign convention F=−∇U must be handled carefully. Finally, conservative fields make line integrals depend only on endpoints, which is the computational payoff of the entire concept.
