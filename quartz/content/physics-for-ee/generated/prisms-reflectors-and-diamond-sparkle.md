## Prisms, Reflectors, and Diamond Sparkle

Source: [[977689-english|Refraction, Fermat's Principle, Apparent Images, and Total Internal Reflection]]

Locations: Page 13, Page 14

The lecture gives several applications of total internal reflection beyond optical fibers. In prisms, internal reflection can be used deliberately by choosing the geometry and incidence angle so that light reflects at a far face instead of exiting. Bicycle reflectors use a related principle: light entering from many directions is reflected back so that the reflector lights up when illuminated. Diamonds sparkle strongly because they have a high refractive index compared with ordinary glass. The lecturer gives typical values: glass has refractive index about $1.5$, while diamond is around $2.4$. Since the critical angle satisfies $\sin \theta_c = n_B/n_A$, a high internal refractive index produces a small critical angle at the diamond-air boundary. This means many internal rays meet the condition for total internal reflection, and the cut of the diamond is designed to maximize light returning through the top.

### Key points

- Prisms can use total internal reflection to redirect light.
- Problems may ask what angle is needed for total internal reflection at the far end of a prism.
- Bicycle reflectors are designed so incoming light is reflected back over many angles.
- Diamonds sparkle partly because of their high refractive index.
- Glass has a typical refractive index of about $1.5$.
- Diamond has a typical refractive index of about $2.4$.
- A higher refractive index gives a smaller critical angle at an interface with air.
- Diamond cuts are designed to maximize light return through the top.
