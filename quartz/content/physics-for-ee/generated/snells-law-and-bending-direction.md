## Snell's Law and Bending Direction

Source: [[977689-english|Refraction, Fermat's Principle, Apparent Images, and Total Internal Reflection]]

Locations: Page 12, Page 13

The lecture repeatedly uses the rule that light bends differently depending on whether it enters a medium of higher or lower refractive index. When light travels from a higher refractive index to a lower refractive index, it bends away from the normal. This direction of bending explains the apparent displacement of objects underwater and is also the condition under which total internal reflection can occur. Snell's law is used explicitly in the derivation of the critical angle for total internal reflection. At the critical case, the refracted angle is $90^\circ$, so the refracted ray travels along the interface. Substituting this into Snell's law gives $n_A \sin \theta_A = n_B$, because $\sin 90^\circ = 1$. This relation becomes the basis for finding the critical angle.

### Key points

- Light bends away from the normal when it travels from high refractive index to low refractive index.
- Light can only undergo total internal reflection in the high-to-low refractive index direction.
- At perpendicular incidence, the transmitted ray continues perpendicularly.
- Increasing the incidence angle increases the refracted angle in the lower-index medium.
- At the critical angle, the refracted ray has $\theta_B = 90^\circ$.
- Snell's law is written in the lecture as $n_A \sin \theta_A = n_B \sin \theta_B$.
- At the critical angle, Snell's law becomes $n_A \sin \theta_c = n_B$.
- The critical-angle condition depends on the ratio $n_B/n_A$.
