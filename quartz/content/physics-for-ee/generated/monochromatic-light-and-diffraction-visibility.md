## Monochromatic Light and Diffraction Visibility

Source: [[979070-english|Diffraction, Interference, and Course Feedback]]

Locations: Page 16, Page 17

The lecture repeatedly distinguishes clean diffraction patterns formed with monochromatic light from messier real-world cases involving many colors. Monochromatic light has a single frequency, so its wavelength is well-defined and the diffraction condition can produce clear dark and bright fringes. If a hole or aperture is illuminated by light of all colors, the effects from different wavelengths overlap and become mingled. This makes the observed pattern harder to interpret because each wavelength satisfies the diffraction condition at a different angle. The CD example also illustrates this: non-monochromatic incident light produces a rainbow because different wavelengths are dispersed. Thus, monochromatic light is not always required for diffraction to occur, but it is important for seeing and analyzing a clean pattern.

### Key points

- Monochromatic light means light of a single frequency.
- A single wavelength makes diffraction fringe positions easier to define.
- Light containing all colors produces overlapping diffraction effects.
- Different wavelengths satisfy diffraction conditions at different angles.
- CD rainbow colors arise because non-monochromatic light is dispersed.
- Clean diffraction analysis is easiest with monochromatic illumination.
