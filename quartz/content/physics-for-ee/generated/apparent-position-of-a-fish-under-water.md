## Apparent Position of a Fish Under Water

Source: [[977689-english|Refraction, Fermat's Principle, Apparent Images, and Total Internal Reflection]]

Locations: Page 12

The lecture explains why a fish under water appears to be in a different position from its true location. A ray of light from the fish travels from water, which has a higher refractive index, into air, which has a lower refractive index. At the water-air boundary, the ray bends away from the normal. The observer's eye interprets incoming light as if it has traveled in a straight line, so it extrapolates the refracted ray backward into the water. This makes the fish appear higher, or closer to the surface, than it really is. The lecturer also notes that if the water surface is choppy, the geometry becomes difficult because the local normal varies across waves. The apparent size of the fish may also be affected, creating additional errors in judging distance or position.

### Key points

- A fish underwater is seen by light rays that leave water and enter air.
- The ray travels from high refractive index to lower refractive index.
- The ray bends away from the normal at the water-air boundary.
- The eye assumes light traveled in a straight line.
- Backward extrapolation of the refracted ray gives an apparent fish position above the real one.
- A flat water surface gives a simpler vertical displacement interpretation.
- Choppy water changes the local normal and makes apparent location harder to determine.
- Apparent size can also mislead the observer about distance or depth.
