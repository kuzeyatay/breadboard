## Single-Slit Diffraction Pattern

Source: [[979070-english|Diffraction, Interference, and Course Feedback]]

Locations: Page 16

A single slit does not simply form a sharp image on a distant screen. If the slit width and wavelength satisfy the right conditions, the screen shows a diffraction pattern: a central bright region, followed by dark spots and weaker light regions. The construction resembles earlier interference reasoning, but instead of using two discrete slits, the slit is treated as a continuum of sources. The resulting brightness depends on whether waves from different points in the slit arrive in phase or out of phase at a point on the screen. The dominant spreading is usually associated with the smallest dimension of the aperture. For a rectangular slit, the pattern can repeat in perpendicular directions related to the slit orientation, while a circular hole gives a circular diffraction pattern with a central bright spot and rings of light and shadow.

### Key points

- A single slit can produce a central bright region and alternating dark and light regions.
- The pattern arises from interference between waves originating across the width of the slit.
- The construction is similar to interference, but uses a continuum of sources rather than two slits.
- The smallest dimension of an aperture often produces the largest diffraction effect.
- A rectangular slit can produce diffraction structure in directions related to its orientation.
- A circular aperture produces a central bright spot with circular rings of shadow and light.
