## Diffraction from Everyday Pixel and Disc Structures

Source: [[979070-english|Diffraction, Interference, and Course Feedback]]

Locations: Page 16

The lecture introduces diffraction through familiar objects whose small-scale structures interact with light. Colored pixels on a phone can cause diffraction or interference patterns because light reflects from many closely spaced pixel elements. A CD also shows diffraction because information is stored as many small bits or patterned structures on its underside. When light reflects from these structures, the repeated spacing can create path differences that lead to diffraction. Because the light falling on a CD is not monochromatic, different wavelengths are separated, producing a rainbow-like pattern through dispersion as well as diffraction. These examples show that diffraction is not limited to laboratory slits: any structure with dimensions comparable to the wavelength of light can redirect waves into visible patterns.

### Key points

- Phone pixels can act as small reflecting structures that produce diffraction or interference patterns.
- CDs diffract light because their underside contains many information-storing bits or patterned structures.
- The visible rainbow on a CD involves both diffraction and dispersion.
- Non-monochromatic light causes different wavelengths to appear as different colors.
- Diffraction becomes visible when structural spacing is relevant compared with the wavelength.
- Everyday diffraction examples help connect slit models to real surfaces.
