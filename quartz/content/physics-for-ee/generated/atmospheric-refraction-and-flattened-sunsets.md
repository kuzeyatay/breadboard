## Atmospheric Refraction and Flattened Sunsets

Source: [[977689-english|Refraction, Fermat's Principle, Apparent Images, and Total Internal Reflection]]

Locations: Page 12, Page 13

The lecture explains the flattened appearance of the Sun near sunset as an effect of atmospheric refraction. When the Sun is high overhead, rays arrive more nearly perpendicular to the atmospheric layers, so different parts of the Sun are affected more similarly. Near sunset, however, rays from the top and bottom of the Sun enter the atmosphere at different angles and pass through different atmospheric layers. Because the atmosphere has curvature and varying refractive properties, the normal to the effective interface and the angle of refraction vary with ray position. Rays from the bottom of the Sun travel through lower atmospheric layers and are bent differently from rays from the top. Since these rays arrive at the observer's eye at different angles, the circular solar disk appears distorted, especially flattened from the bottom. The lecturer postpones explanation of the red color of sunsets to the next lecture.

### Key points

- The Sun can appear flattened near sunset because of refraction.
- Rays from the top and bottom of the Sun travel through the atmosphere differently.
- At sunset, rays enter atmospheric layers at oblique angles.
- The curvature of the atmosphere makes the local normal vary with ray path.
- Different rays experience different angles of refraction.
- The lower part of the Sun is affected differently because its rays pass through different layers.
- The observer receives top and bottom rays at altered angles, distorting the apparent disk.
- The red color of sunsets is explicitly deferred to a later lecture.
