## Bell Inequality as a Test of Entanglement

Source: [[986217-english-1|Quantum Entanglement, Bell Inequality, and Course Summary]]

Locations: Page 14

John Stewart Bell devised a correlation experiment and inequality to test whether hidden variables could explain quantum correlations. The lecture describes Bell's inequality as a condition where the sum of several probabilities must be less than two if entanglement is not truly needed. A measured correlation value larger than two rules out the hidden-variable explanation described in the lecture and leaves entanglement as the only possible explanation of that experiment. This turns the philosophical question about whether quantum systems have hidden predetermined properties into an experimentally testable claim. Bell's inequality therefore functions as a boundary between classical hidden-variable-style correlations and correlations that require quantum entanglement.

### Key points

- Bell designed a correlation experiment to distinguish hidden variables from entanglement.
- The experiment uses an inequality involving sums of probabilities.
- The lecture states that the probability sum must be less than two if entanglement is not true.
- A measured value larger than two supports entanglement as the only explanation.
- Bell's test makes the hidden-variable debate experimentally decidable.
- The inequality compares classical limits with quantum correlation strength.
