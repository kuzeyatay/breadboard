## Resonance and Harmonics on a String

Source: [[974965-english-1|Standing Waves, Reflection, and Resonance on Strings]]

Locations: Page 11, Page 12

Standing waves on a string appear at resonant frequencies called harmonics. The lecture demonstration describes sending a rhythmic pulse to a fixed end: at the fundamental frequency, the fundamental mode forms; at twice the frequency, the second harmonic forms; at higher suitable frequencies, the third harmonic and further modes appear. Because the string is a continuous medium, infinitely many harmonics are mathematically possible, although it is physically impossible to excite all of them. Each harmonic corresponds to a different number of half-wavelengths fitting along the string and therefore a different pattern of nodes and antinodes. The key condition is that the forward and backward traveling waves must have exactly the right frequency to interfere into a stable standing wave.

### Key points

- A rhythmic pulse reflecting from a fixed end can form standing waves.
- The fundamental mode occurs at the fundamental frequency.
- Doubling the frequency can excite the second harmonic.
- Higher suitable frequencies can excite the third harmonic and further modes.
- A continuous medium has infinitely many possible harmonics in principle.
- Not all harmonics can be physically excited in practice.
- Each harmonic corresponds to a different allowed standing-wave pattern.
