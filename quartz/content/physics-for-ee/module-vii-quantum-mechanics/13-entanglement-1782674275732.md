## Entanglement

Up to this point, the wave function has mostly been used to describe one quantum object at a time. For one particle in three dimensions, we write a wave function such as

[
\psi(x,y,z),
]

and the probability density is

[
|\psi(x,y,z)|^2.
]

This tells us where that particle is likely to be detected. But quantum mechanics becomes even stranger when we ask about more than one object. If two particles are produced together, interact, and then separate, can we always describe particle (A) with its own wave function and particle (B) with its own wave function? Entanglement is the answer: sometimes the correct quantum description belongs to the pair as a whole.

Classically, correlations between two objects are not mysterious. Imagine placing a left glove in one box and the matching right glove in another box, then sending the boxes far apart. If someone opens one box and finds the left glove, they immediately know the other box contains the right glove. Nothing physical had to travel between the boxes at that moment. The gloves already had definite identities before the boxes were opened. The measurement only revealed what was already true.

That glove example is a classical correlation. It is a useful starting point because entanglement also produces correlations, but not of the same kind. In an entangled quantum pair, the theory does not simply say that each particle secretly carries its own prewritten answer. Instead, the pair is described by one joint quantum state. The measurement outcomes can be strongly correlated, but those correlations come from the structure of the joint state, not from two independent classical states hidden inside the particles.

To see what “independent” would mean, suppose a two-particle state could be split into one wave function for particle (A) and one wave function for particle (B). In a simple position notation, such a separable state would have the form

[
\Psi(x_A,x_B)=\psi_A(x_A)\psi_B(x_B).
]

Here (x_A) is the position coordinate of particle (A), (x_B) is the position coordinate of particle (B), (\psi_A(x_A)) describes particle (A), and (\psi_B(x_B)) describes particle (B). The product form means that the two-particle state can be built from two independent single-particle descriptions. The whole state is just “state of (A)” multiplied by “state of (B).”

Entanglement is precisely the case where this cannot be done. A two-particle state is entangled when the joint state cannot be written as

[
\Psi(x_A,x_B)=\psi_A(x_A)\psi_B(x_B).
]

This non-factorability is the mathematical centerpiece of entanglement. It says that the pair has a joint quantum state that cannot be reduced to separate complete states for the two particles. The important information is not stored independently in (A) and independently in (B); it is stored in the relationship between the possible joint outcomes.

A simple two-outcome example makes the idea more concrete. Consider the state

[
|\Psi\rangle
============

\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}
\left(
|0\rangle_A|1\rangle_B
+
|1\rangle_A|0\rangle_B
\right).
]

The symbols (|0\rangle_A) and (|1\rangle_A) represent two possible measurement outcomes for particle (A), while (|0\rangle_B) and (|1\rangle_B) represent two possible outcomes for particle (B). The state says that there are two joint alternatives: either (A) is found in state (0) while (B) is found in state (1), or (A) is found in state (1) while (B) is found in state (0). The factor

[
\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}
]

normalizes the state, so that the total probability is one.

The key point is that this is a superposition of joint alternatives. It is not saying that particle (A) has its own complete state and particle (B) has its own complete state. The alternatives are tied together. If particle (A) is measured and the result is (0), then particle (B) will be found in state (1) when measured in the same basis. If particle (A) is measured and the result is (1), then particle (B) will be found in state (0). The individual outcome is not predictable in advance, but the relationship between the two outcomes is predictable.

This is where entanglement differs from the glove boxes. With gloves, opening one box reveals a property that was already definite: left or right. With an entangled quantum state, the joint state gives probabilities and correlations, but it does not describe the particles as ordinary objects carrying pre-existing independent answers in the same classical way. Measurement produces a definite outcome from the joint quantum state, and after that outcome, the prediction for the other particle is correspondingly updated.

A natural reaction is to try to rescue the classical picture. Perhaps, one might say, the particles really did carry hidden instructions from the beginning, and quantum mechanics merely failed to list them. Such an idea is called a hidden-variable picture. The point of Bell’s theorem, at the conceptual level needed here, is that this rescue does not work in general if the hidden instructions are local. Certain entangled correlations are stronger than any local hidden-variable model can reproduce. Experiments support the quantum prediction: entangled systems behave as joint quantum states, not merely as classical objects carrying prewritten local answers.

This does not mean entanglement allows faster-than-light communication. That is the most important misconception to remove. Suppose two entangled particles are separated by a large distance. Measuring one particle gives a random outcome. The observer cannot choose whether the result will be (0) or (1). Because the result cannot be controlled, it cannot be used to send a message. The correlation with the distant particle becomes visible only when the two observers later compare their measurement records using ordinary communication.

So entanglement is non-classical, but it is not a controllable faster-than-light signal. It changes the structure of the quantum state and the correlations between measurement outcomes. It does not let one observer transmit information instantly by choosing an outcome.

Entanglement also connects back to the earlier idea of probability amplitudes. In the double-slit experiment, interference appeared because amplitudes for indistinguishable alternatives had to be added before probabilities were calculated. In an entangled state, the alternatives involve more than one object at once. The amplitudes belong to combined possibilities of the pair, such as “(A) gives 0 and (B) gives 1” together with “(A) gives 1 and (B) gives 0.” The quantum state is therefore not a list of separate particle properties; it is a superposition of joint possibilities.

This is also why measurement plays such a central role. Measuring one part of an entangled system does not merely reveal one independent property belonging to that particle alone. It selects one outcome from a joint state, and that changes the state description used for the pair. After the measurement, predictions about the other particle are conditional on the result obtained. The correlation is real, but it is not a classical mechanical signal travelling between the particles.

Entanglement is therefore the final extension of the wave-function idea in this module. For one particle, the wave function gives a probability amplitude for that particle’s possible measurement outcomes. For two particles, the wave function may describe the combined system in a way that cannot be separated into independent single-particle wave functions. That is the essential content of entanglement: the whole quantum state can contain information that is not reducible to its parts.

The conceptual chain now closes. We began with classical failures: photons were needed for light–matter energy exchange, matter waves were needed for electron diffraction, and wave functions were needed to describe probability amplitudes. The Schrödinger equation then showed how potentials shape allowed quantum states. Entanglement takes the final step: when quantum systems are combined, the amplitudes may belong to the combined system itself. This explains why quantum mechanics is not merely a theory of tiny particles moving along hidden paths, but a theory of states, measurements, and correlations that can exceed classical separable descriptions.
