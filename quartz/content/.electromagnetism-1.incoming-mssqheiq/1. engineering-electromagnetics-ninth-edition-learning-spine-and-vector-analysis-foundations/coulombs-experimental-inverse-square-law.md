# 1.31 Coulomb's Experimental Inverse-Square Law

Source: [[engineering-electromagnetics-9th-ed-9nbsped-compress|Engineering Electromagnetics, Ninth Edition: Learning Spine and Vector Analysis Foundations]]

Locations: Page 38, Section: Coulomb's Law and Electric Field Intensity, Section: 2.1 The Experimental Law of Coulomb, Page 39

Coulomb's law quantifies the electrostatic force between two sufficiently small charged objects in free space when their separation is large compared with their size. Its scalar magnitude is proportional to the product of the charges and inversely proportional to the square of their separation:
$$
F=k\frac{Q_1Q_2}{R^2},\qquad k=\frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}
$$
 In SI units, charge is measured in coulombs, separation in meters, and force in newtons. The free-space permittivity is
$$
\epsilon_0=8.854\times10^{-12}\ \mathrm{F/m}=\frac{1}{36\pi}10^{-9}\ \mathrm{F/m}
$$
 Charge signs determine attraction or repulsion once direction is included in the vector law. The source stresses the scale of the coulomb by comparing it with the elementary charge and by noting the enormous force between one-coulomb charges separated by one meter.

## Core Ideas

- Electrostatic force is proportional to $Q_1Q_2$.
- Electrostatic force varies as $1/R^2$.
- $k=1/(4\pi\epsilon_0)$ in SI units.
- $\epsilon_0=8.854\times10^{-12}\ \mathrm{F/m}$.
- The equivalent approximation is $\epsilon_0=(1/36\pi)10^{-9}\ \mathrm{F/m}$.
- The elementary charge magnitude is $1.602\times10^{-19}\ \mathrm{C}$.
- One coulomb corresponds to approximately $6\times10^{18}$ elementary charges.
- Like charges repel and unlike charges attract.
