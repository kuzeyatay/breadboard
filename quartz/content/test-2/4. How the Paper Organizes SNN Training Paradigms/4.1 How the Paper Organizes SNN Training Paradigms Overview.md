### How the Paper Organizes SNN Training Paradigms

After presenting spiking neural networks as a brain-inspired alternative to conventional neural models, the paper turns to a more practical question: if an SNN computes through discrete spikes, how should it be trained? The source does not treat "SNN training" as one single method. Instead, it organizes the comparison around three named paradigms: **surrogate gradient descent**, **ANN-to-SNN conversion**, and **Spike-Timing Dependent Plasticity (STDP)**. In this subsection, a *training paradigm* means the overall route by which a usable spiking network is obtained. That choice of organization matters because the paper's stated research gap is that earlier work often focused on only one training route or only one performance metric at a time, which makes practical comparison difficult.

The first route, **surrogate gradient descent**, keeps learning inside the spiking model itself. The supplied source names this paradigm clearly, but it does not provide the governing equations or update rules in the available material, so the safest explanation here is conceptual: this is the direct-training route for SNNs. The second route, **ANN-to-SNN conversion**, begins from a different starting point. A conventional artificial neural network is trained first, and that trained model is then converted into a spiking form. Conceptually, this asks whether the strengths of established ANN training can be carried into an event-driven system. The third route, **STDP**, shifts the basis of learning again. Here the source frames learning around spike timing itself. Because the provided material does not include the STDP update equations, this subsection must remain qualitative, but the contrast is still clear: one route trains the SNN directly, one converts a trained ANN, and one ties learning to spike timing.

Seen this way, the paper is not merely listing three techniques. It is setting up three different answers to the same design problem: how do we obtain a useful spiking network? One answer is direct optimization in the spiking setting, another is conversion from a trained conventional network, and a third is spike-timing-based learning. That structure makes the paper's larger contribution easier to understand. It argues that isolated claims are not enough. A result about high accuracy, a separate result about low energy use, and another about biologically inspired learning do not tell us much by themselves if they come from different training routes and are judged by different standards.

That is why the paper places all three paradigms under a **unified evaluation**. In plain language, **accuracy** asks how often predictions are correct, **latency** asks how quickly an inference is produced, **energy per inference** asks how much energy one prediction uses, **spike count** asks how much spiking activity is generated during inference, and **convergence** asks how training behavior changes over time. The source names all five dimensions explicitly, but the supplied material does not provide formal mathematical definitions for them, so they should be understood here in this plain-language sense. The key organizing idea is that the same lens is applied across all three training families.

**Question.** Why does the paper separate surrogate-gradient training, ANN-to-SNN conversion, and STDP instead of discussing SNN training as one single method?
**Answer.** Because the source treats them as genuinely different learning routes. Surrogate-gradient methods train the spiking network directly, ANN-to-SNN conversion starts from a trained conventional network and then converts it, and STDP organizes learning around spike timing. Since these routes are compared through accuracy, latency, energy per inference, spike count, and convergence, the paper keeps them distinct so their tradeoffs can be judged side by side.

**Question.** What research gap does this three-paradigm organization help the paper address?
**Answer.** The paper states that earlier work often focused on only one training paradigm or only one metric at a time. That makes it hard to see the full tradeoff structure of SNNs. By organizing the field around the same three paradigms and comparing them under one shared evaluation lens, the paper creates a clearer basis for deciding which route best fits a low-power, latency-sensitive, or otherwise constrained application.
