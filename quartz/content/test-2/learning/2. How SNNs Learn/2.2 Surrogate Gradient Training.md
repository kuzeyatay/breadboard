## Surrogate Gradient Training

Imagine a spiking network that already knows how to listen for events: a neuron receives incoming spikes, its internal voltage rises, and when that voltage crosses a threshold it emits its own spike. That spike is useful because it is clean, sparse, and timed. A downstream neuron can treat the event as a message: something significant happened now. The learning problem begins when the network makes a wrong decision and needs to adjust the earlier connection weights that helped produce that spike pattern. Gradient-based training asks, "If this weight had been a little larger or smaller, would the final error have gone down?" Spikes make that question difficult because the output of a spiking neuron changes in a jump: below threshold there is no spike, and at threshold there is a spike.

A smooth artificial neuron gives gradient descent a slope to follow. If its output changes gradually as its input changes, the learning algorithm can measure how sensitive the output is to the input. A spiking neuron has a sharp threshold event. For many small changes in voltage, the emitted spike stays the same; then, at the threshold, the output switches abruptly. The true derivative of this step-like spike function is zero almost everywhere and undefined at the jump. If training used that derivative directly, most learning signals would disappear before they reached earlier weights. The network could still produce spikes during the forward pass, but the backward pass would have no useful path for credit assignment.

Surrogate gradient training solves this by keeping the spike in the forward computation and replacing only the troublesome derivative during learning. During the forward pass, the neuron behaves like a spiking neuron: it integrates input, compares voltage to a threshold, and emits a discrete event. During the backward pass, the training rule pretends that the hard threshold had a small smooth slope near the firing point. That substitute slope is the surrogate gradient. "Surrogate" means it stands in for the true gradient where the true gradient cannot guide learning well. The network still learns to use spikes, but the learning signal can pass through the spike event.

A simple way to picture the mechanism is to focus on one synapse feeding one spiking neuron. Let $w$ be the weight on an incoming connection, $x$ be the input spike signal arriving through that connection, $u$ be the neuron's membrane voltage, $\theta$ be the firing threshold, $s$ be the output spike, and $L$ be the loss, the number training tries to reduce. The neuron fires when $u$ reaches $\theta$, so the spike can be written as a threshold decision: $s = H(u - \theta)$, where $H$ is a step function that outputs 0 before threshold and 1 after threshold. The difficult part is the derivative of $H$. Surrogate gradient training replaces that hard derivative with a chosen smooth function $g(u-\theta)$ that is largest near the threshold and small farther away.

That gives the weight update the same overall shape as ordinary gradient descent:
$$
W_{\text{new}} = w_{\text{old}} - \eta \frac{\partial L}{\partial w}
$$
Here, $w_{\text{old}}$ is the current synaptic weight, $w_{\text{new}}$ is the adjusted weight after one learning step, $\eta$ is the learning rate that controls the size of the step, $L$ is the loss, and $\frac{\partial L}{\partial w}$ estimates how much the loss would change if the weight changed. For a spiking neuron, the estimate uses the surrogate slope when the chain of influence passes through the spike:
$$
\frac{\partial L}{\partial w}
\approx
\frac{\partial L}{\partial s}
\; g(u-\theta) \;
\frac{\partial u}{\partial w}
$$
The term $\frac{\partial L}{\partial s}$ says how the final error depends on this neuron's spike. The term $g(u-\theta)$ says how close the neuron was to threshold and therefore how trainable that spike decision should be. The term $\frac{\partial u}{\partial w}$ says how the weight affected the membrane voltage. Together, these terms tell the learning rule whether to strengthen or weaken the synapse.

A concrete interpretation makes the rule feel less abstract. Suppose a neuron should have fired because the correct class needed its signal, but its voltage stopped just below threshold. Then $u-\theta$ is close to zero, so the surrogate slope $g(u-\theta)$ is large enough to pass a learning signal. If the incoming spike $x$ helped raise the voltage, increasing its weight may help the neuron fire next time. If the neuron fired at an unhelpful time, the sign of $\frac{\partial L}{\partial s}$ can push the weight in the opposite direction. The update does more than reward every spike; it asks whether each spike helped reduce the task error.

**Question.** Why can't ordinary gradient descent simply train the spike threshold directly as a step function?

**Answer.** Ordinary gradient descent needs a useful slope. A hard threshold gives almost no slope away from the exact firing point, so earlier weights receive little guidance about how to change. Surrogate gradient training keeps the hard spike for activity while using a smooth substitute slope during the learning calculation.

This method makes directly trained SNNs practical for accuracy-focused tasks because it lets them use supervised error signals while preserving event-based computation. Instead of first training a dense artificial network and then translating it into spikes, a surrogate-trained SNN learns its spiking patterns directly. That direct training helps the network discover when to emit spikes, which neurons should stay silent, and how quickly enough evidence can accumulate for a decision. The result is a trainable SNN that can approach ANN-level accuracy while keeping the temporal and sparse behavior that makes spiking computation attractive.

The accuracy-latency target gives surrogate gradients their practical role. A useful spiking model should answer correctly and quickly. If the network waits for too many time steps, it may gather more evidence, but the response becomes slower and the spike count rises. If it responds too early, it may save time and energy while losing accuracy. Surrogate gradient training can optimize this balance because the loss can pressure the network toward correct outputs that appear within a short inference window. Reported benchmark behavior places directly trained surrogate-gradient SNNs around low-latency responses of about 10 ms with competitive accuracy, while reaching accuracy within roughly 1-2% of ANN performance in the discussed comparisons.

The energy side follows from the same learned sparsity. Dense artificial networks repeatedly process continuous-valued activations, while SNNs compute around spike events. Surrogate-gradient SNNs are described as using about 15 mJ per inference with roughly 12,000 spikes in the comparison, lower than the ANN level of about 200 mJ and also lower than converted SNNs at about 20 mJ with roughly 20,000 spikes. These numbers show the practical meaning of learning spike patterns directly: fewer events can carry enough task information, so the network spends less computation per inference. STDP-based SNNs can be even sparser and lower-energy, around 5 mJ and 4,000 spikes in the same comparison, while surrogate training aims more strongly at the supervised accuracy and latency target.

Training cost is the price paid for that control. Surrogate-gradient SNNs need backpropagation through time-like learning signals across neuron states and spike events, so training can be more demanding than purely local spike-timing rules. The benefit is that the method gives a clear task-driven route to high accuracy and fast inference. The reported convergence behavior, with surrogate-gradient SNNs reaching strong performance by about the 20th epoch, shows why this method is attractive when the goal is a trained model that responds quickly rather than only a sparse one. The training procedure spends computation upfront so that inference can be event-driven and efficient later.

**Question.** Does the surrogate gradient mean the network stops being a real spiking network during training?

**Answer.** The network still emits discrete spikes in the forward pass. The surrogate appears in the backward learning calculation, where it supplies a usable slope near the threshold. The trained model is learning through spikes, while the update rule uses a smooth approximation to decide how weights should change.

The mental model is a two-view picture of the same neuron. In activity mode, the neuron is event-based: voltage accumulates, a threshold is crossed, and a spike is sent at a particular time. In learning mode, the training rule asks how close that event was to changing and uses a smooth surrogate slope to carry responsibility backward through the network. Weights are then adjusted by a gradient rule so useful spike patterns become more likely, harmful spike patterns become less likely, and decisions can arrive with low latency. Surrogate gradient training turns the sharpness of spikes from a barrier to learning into a manageable approximation, making SNNs trainable for the combined goal of accuracy, speed, and efficient event-driven computation.
