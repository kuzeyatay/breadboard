### Spike-Timing Dependent Plasticity

Imagine the network from the previous lesson receiving a stream of spikes from a quiet sensor. Most of the time, nothing changes, so most neurons stay silent. Then an edge moves, a sound begins, or a gesture starts, and a small pattern of spikes travels through the network. A learning rule for this kind of system should respect the same event-based structure: it should change the network mainly when spikes occur, and it should use the timing of those spikes as useful information. Spike-Timing Dependent Plasticity, usually shortened to **STDP**, does exactly that. It lets a connection learn from the order and closeness of spikes on its two sides.

A connection between two spiking neurons is called a **synapse**, and its strength is represented by a **weight**. A larger weight means a spike from the sending neuron has a stronger effect on the receiving neuron. In an SNN, the sending neuron is often called the **pre-synaptic neuron**, because its spike arrives before the synapse. The receiving neuron is the **post-synaptic neuron**, because its spike is produced after synaptic inputs have influenced it. STDP asks a very local question at each synapse: when the pre-synaptic neuron fired and the post-synaptic neuron fired, which one happened first, and how close together were they?

If the pre-synaptic spike arrives shortly before the post-synaptic neuron fires, the connection is treated as useful for helping cause that output spike. The synapse is strengthened, so the same input pattern can more easily help produce a similar response in the future. If the pre-synaptic spike arrives after the post-synaptic neuron has already fired, the connection is treated as less useful for that particular event. The synapse is weakened, so that late input has less influence next time. The timing window is the heart of the rule: nearby spikes cause stronger learning changes, while spikes far apart in time cause little or no change.

A simple way to picture the rule is to imagine a doorbell and a light. If pressing the doorbell is followed almost immediately by the light turning on, you naturally connect the press with the light. If the light turned on a moment before the press, the press did not help cause that event. STDP applies the same causal intuition to spike trains. A pre-synaptic spike that arrives just before a post-synaptic spike looks like a helpful contributor. A pre-synaptic spike that arrives too late looks like background activity for that output event.

This makes STDP a form of **local timing-based learning**. "Local" means the synapse can update using information available right at that connection: the time of the incoming spike, the time of the outgoing spike, and the current synaptic weight. It does not need a separate global error signal sent backward through many layers. It does not need every neuron to wait for a full dense calculation over the whole network before a small change can happen. The learning event is attached to the spike event itself.

A beginner confusion often comes from thinking that learning must always mean comparing the final answer with a label such as "cat," "car," or "gesture." STDP can support **unsupervised learning**, where the network adjusts to regularities in the input stream without being handed an explicit correct answer for every example. If a certain group of input spikes repeatedly arrives in the same temporal pattern, the synapses that consistently help a neuron fire become stronger. Over time, that neuron becomes more responsive to that pattern. The network is learning structure in the stream: which events tend to arrive together, which events tend to precede others, and which timing relationships reliably predict a response.

For example, suppose an event-based vision sensor watches a hand moving from left to right. Pixels on the left side may spike first, nearby pixels may spike slightly later, and pixels farther right may spike after that. A neuron receiving these inputs can become sensitive to that left-to-right timing pattern if the synapses from earlier useful spikes are strengthened. The learned feature is temporal: the order of events helps define what the neuron detects. A dense frame-based system would repeatedly process full arrays of values, while the spiking system can focus its changes around the small number of events that actually occurred.

STDP also connects directly to energy use. In a spiking network, activity costs energy because spikes trigger communication and computation. A learning rule that encourages useful sparse firing can support low-power operation: fewer spikes mean fewer event transmissions and fewer synaptic updates. STDP-based SNNs are especially associated with low spike counts because they can learn from precise event timing instead of relying on large amounts of continuous-valued activity. In reported comparisons, an ANN-style model may consume around 200 mJ per inference, while a converted SNN may reduce that to about 20 mJ, and a surrogate-gradient SNN may reach about 15 mJ. An STDP-based SNN is described at about 5 mJ per inference with roughly 4,000 spikes, compared with much larger spike counts for other SNN approaches. The trend is the useful part to understand: as computation becomes more event-driven and sparse, energy per inference can fall sharply.

The tradeoff is that STDP learns gradually from repeated timing relationships. A network using STDP may need more exposure to patterns before its responses settle, and it may give up some raw accuracy compared with training methods that optimize a task objective more directly. That makes STDP especially natural for settings where energy and autonomy matter: small edge devices, sensors that run for long periods, and systems that adapt to unlabeled streams of events. The learning rule fits the hardware-friendly behavior of SNNs because both the representation and the training update are driven by spikes.

**Question.** If STDP strengthens a connection when the input spike comes before the output spike, does that mean every earlier spike should always make the weight bigger?

**Answer.** The timing has to be close enough to plausibly matter. A pre-synaptic spike that happens shortly before the post-synaptic spike can be treated as a contributor, so the weight increases. A spike that happened long before the output gives weak timing evidence, so it causes little or no change. STDP depends on relative timing within a learning window, not on simple "earlier than" order across any length of time.

The phrase **ultra-low-power inference** means the trained network can make predictions or responses using very little energy. STDP contributes to that goal during learning by shaping connections around useful spike timing, and the resulting network can often operate with sparse activity. During inference, the network does not need to constantly update every unit with continuous numbers. It can wait for events, send spikes when needed, and let silence carry meaning. A quiet input stream stays cheap because very few spikes move through the system.

A useful mental model is to treat STDP as a tiny timing judge sitting at every synapse. Each judge watches two clocks: the arrival time of the input spike and the firing time of the output neuron. When the input arrives just in time to help the output fire, the judge strengthens that pathway. When the input arrives after the response, the judge weakens that pathway. Repeated across many synapses and many examples, this simple local rule turns temporal regularity into learned structure. The same sparse spikes that carry information also trigger the learning changes, which is why STDP fits naturally with low-power SNNs: the network learns from events, communicates through events, and spends most of its energy only when events actually occur.
