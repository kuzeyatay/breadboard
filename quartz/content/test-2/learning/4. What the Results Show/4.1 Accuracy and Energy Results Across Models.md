## Accuracy and Energy Results Across Models

Now that accuracy, energy consumption, and normalized energy efficiency have separate meanings, they can be read together as one practical question: if a model gives the right answer often, how much energy does it spend to get there? Imagine choosing a vision model for a small battery-powered device. A high accuracy score is attractive, but the device also has to survive on limited power. A model that recognizes digits or images well while burning through energy quickly may fit a server, while a model that spends only a small fraction of that energy may fit an edge sensor, wearable device, or mobile robot.

The comparison begins with a familiar reference point: the conventional artificial neural network, or ANN. An ANN processes information through continuous numerical activations. During inference, many units carry values forward layer by layer, even when the final decision feels simple. That style of computation supports strong classification accuracy, so the ANN sits near the top of the accuracy ranking. In the results here, the ANN reaches about 92% accuracy on CIFAR-10, and it serves as the high-energy baseline, using about 200 millijoules per inference in the reported comparison. This baseline gives every other model a clear point of comparison: lower energy only becomes meaningful when we ask how much accuracy remains.

A spiking neural network changes the cost structure by communicating through spike events. A spike is a brief event sent at a particular time. When fewer spikes are produced, fewer event-driven computations are needed, so energy can drop sharply. The model comparison contains three SNN approaches: a converted SNN, a surrogate-gradient SNN, and an STDP-based SNN. Each one uses spikes, but each reaches the accuracy-energy balance in a different way. A converted SNN begins from an ANN-like model and translates its activity into spikes, often through rate-coded spike patterns. That helps preserve performance, but it can require many spikes and longer simulation windows. A surrogate-gradient SNN trains spiking behavior more directly with a gradient-based method that works around the difficulty of differentiating spike events. That makes it especially strong when the goal is to approach ANN accuracy while gaining energy efficiency. An STDP-based SNN uses spike-timing dependent plasticity, where learning depends on the timing relationship between spikes, and it reaches the lowest energy use in this comparison with a larger accuracy tradeoff.

The table is the cleanest place to practice reading these results jointly. The MNIST and CIFAR-10 columns show classification accuracy on two datasets, while normalized energy consumption turns raw energy into a relative cost. A useful way to read normalized energy is:

Normalized energy consumption = model energy per inference / ANN energy per inference

Here, "model energy per inference" means the energy used by the model to make one prediction, and "ANN energy per inference" means the energy used by the ANN baseline for the same kind of comparison. The formula lets you compute a ratio: a value of 1 means "the same energy as the ANN baseline," while a value of 0.10 means "one tenth of the ANN energy." This ratio helps the eye compare models without constantly converting raw millijoules.

The table should be read row by row, then across columns. The ANN row gives the strongest accuracy reference, especially on CIFAR-10, where the task is more difficult than MNIST. The converted SNN remains competitive because it inherits much of the behavior of the ANN, but its energy advantage is limited by the relatively heavy spike traffic created by rate-coded propagation. The surrogate-gradient SNN is the most balanced row for many accuracy-sensitive settings: its accuracy comes close to the ANN, within about one to two percentage points in the reported results, while its energy cost falls far below the ANN baseline. The STDP-based SNN moves furthest toward efficiency. It reaches the lowest energy consumption, about 5 millijoules per inference in the reported energy comparison, and produces the sparsest spike activity, while accepting a modest reduction in classification accuracy.

The same comparison becomes more intuitive when plotted as performance beside energy. A chart lets the eye see a pattern that a single accuracy number hides: the model with the strongest raw accuracy can also be the most expensive, and the most energy-efficient model can sit slightly lower on the accuracy scale. The result is a ranking that depends on the design goal.

The graph shows the ANN at the high-energy end, around 200 millijoules per inference. The converted SNN drops that energy sharply to about 20 millijoules, which already represents a major reduction. The surrogate-gradient SNN moves slightly lower again, around 15 millijoules, while keeping accuracy close to the ANN. The STDP-based SNN reaches the lowest energy point, around 5 millijoules. Reading the graph as a tradeoff curve gives a better mental picture than declaring a single winner. If the device needs the strongest accuracy and has enough power, the ANN is attractive. If the device needs near-ANN accuracy with much lower energy, the surrogate-gradient SNN becomes the natural candidate. If the device lives under severe power limits, the STDP-based SNN becomes compelling because each inference costs so little energy.

A beginner confusion often appears here: if SNNs save so much energy, why would anyone still use the ANN? The answer comes from the accuracy side of the table. Classification difficulty increases from MNIST-scale tasks to CIFAR-10-scale tasks, and accuracy gaps can widen as the problem becomes more complex. In the reported CIFAR-10 comparison, the ANN reaches about 92%, while direct SNN approaches can sit lower, around the mid-80% range depending on the method. That difference may be acceptable for a low-power sensor, yet too large for a setting where each misclassification is costly. The practical decision depends on the acceptable accuracy loss per unit of energy saved.

**Question.** Why can't we choose the best model by looking only at the highest accuracy number?

**Answer.** Accuracy shows how often the model predicts correctly, but it leaves out the cost of producing those predictions. In this comparison, the ANN has the strongest raw accuracy, yet it also uses the most energy. Surrogate-gradient SNNs approach ANN accuracy while using far less energy, and STDP-based SNNs use the least energy with a larger accuracy tradeoff. The best choice depends on whether the application values maximum correctness, low power use, or a balance of both.

The phrase "accuracy-energy comparison" means that every performance claim is read beside its cost. A one-point accuracy gain can be valuable when the energy increase is small. The same one-point gain can be unattractive when it requires ten times the energy. Surrogate-gradient SNNs are interesting because they occupy the middle of this practical space: they preserve much of the ANN's accuracy while gaining the efficiency advantages of spiking computation. STDP-based SNNs show the other end of the spectrum: they demonstrate how far energy can fall when sparse spike activity is the dominant priority.

**Question.** What does it mean when a surrogate-gradient SNN "approaches ANN accuracy"?

**Answer.** It means the surrogate-gradient SNN reaches accuracy close to the ANN baseline, roughly within one to two percentage points in the reported results, while operating with much lower energy consumption. The phrase points to a practical balance: the model behaves almost like the ANN in classification performance, but its spike-based computation reduces the energy cost per inference.

The mental model is a three-way reading of each row: ask how accurate the model is, ask how much energy it spends, and ask whether that exchange fits the deployment setting. The ANN anchors the high-accuracy, high-energy end. The converted SNN shows that ANN-like behavior can be transferred into spikes with a large energy reduction, though spike traffic remains relatively high. The surrogate-gradient SNN shows the strongest balance between near-ANN accuracy and low energy. The STDP-based SNN shows the lowest-energy path, especially attractive when power is the strictest constraint. The results therefore teach a practical habit: model ranking becomes meaningful only when accuracy and energy are read together.
