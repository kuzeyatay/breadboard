### Reading Accuracy and Energy Results Together

Now that spike trains and sparse activity have a concrete meaning, the performance numbers can be read as more than a race for the highest accuracy score. Imagine choosing a model for a small device that must recognize images again and again while running from a limited power budget. If the model gets nearly every image right but burns much more energy on every inference, its usefulness depends on where it will live. A server plugged into reliable power can tolerate a heavier computation. A battery-powered sensor gains value from every avoided operation. The comparison between artificial neural networks and spiking neural networks therefore needs two eyes at once: one eye on correctness, and one eye on the cost of producing that correctness.

Accuracy answers a direct question: out of all test examples, what fraction does the model classify correctly? MNIST accuracy measures this on handwritten digit images, a relatively simple visual recognition benchmark. CIFAR-10 accuracy measures it on small natural images spread across ten object classes, a more demanding setting because the images contain color, texture, and varied backgrounds. Energy consumption answers a different question: how much physical computation does the model spend per inference? In this comparison, the energy values are reported per inference, so each number can be read as the cost of making one prediction.

The table places the four model types side by side: an ANN, a converted SNN, a surrogate gradient SNN, and an STDP-based SNN. The MNIST and CIFAR-10 columns show how well each model predicts labels on the two datasets, while the normalized energy column puts energy use onto a common scale. Reading across a row gives the personality of one model. Reading down a column gives a ranking for one metric. The useful interpretation comes from doing both at once. The ANN sits at the top for raw accuracy, especially as the task becomes harder, because it uses continuous-valued operations throughout the network. The surrogate gradient SNN stays close to that accuracy, within the small gap reported for this comparison, while spending much less energy. The converted SNN also reaches competitive accuracy, but its rate-coded spike propagation produces more spikes than the more directly optimized spiking methods. The STDP-based SNN reaches the lowest energy use and sparsest activity, while accepting a lower accuracy level.

Normalized energy is a way to compare models without forcing the learner to keep every raw millijoule value in mind. If the ANN is used as the reference, its normalized energy is set to 1. A model that uses one tenth as much energy has normalized energy 0.10. The computation is:

Normalized energy = model energy per inference / ANN energy per inference

Here, "model energy per inference" means the millijoules used by the model being evaluated to make one prediction, and "ANN energy per inference" means the millijoules used by the ANN for the same kind of prediction. With the reported energy values, a converted SNN at about 20 mJ compared with an ANN at about 200 mJ gives 20 / 200 = 0.10, so it uses about 10% of the ANN's energy. A surrogate gradient SNN at about 15 mJ gives 15 / 200 = 0.075, or 7.5% of the ANN's energy. An STDP-based SNN at about 5 mJ gives 5 / 200 = 0.025, or 2.5% of the ANN's energy. The same calculation also explains the reported energy reduction range: using 10% of the energy means a 90% reduction, and using 2.5% of the energy means a 97.5% reduction.

A beginner often expects the "best" model to be the one with the largest accuracy number. That instinct works only when all other costs are roughly equal. In this comparison, the costs differ sharply. A small drop in accuracy can come with a large drop in energy, so the preferred model depends on the goal of deployment. If the goal is maximum classification accuracy and energy is plentiful, the ANN is naturally attractive. If the goal is strong accuracy with a major energy reduction, the surrogate gradient SNN becomes compelling. If the goal is extreme energy savings and sparse event-driven activity, the STDP-based SNN becomes attractive even though its accuracy is lower.

![Performance analysis and energy comparison of SNN models versus ANN](/test-2/assets/source-visuals/2510-27379v1-page-7-graph-g1-performance-analysis-and-energy-comparison-of-sn.png)

*Performance analysis and energy comparison of SNN models versus ANN* *(p. 7)*

The graph makes the tradeoff visible by pairing energy consumption with spike count across the same model family. The ANN appears near the high-energy end, around 200 mJ per inference, because dense continuous operations keep the computation active. The converted SNN drops energy to roughly 20 mJ, but its spike count remains relatively high at about 20,000 spikes per inference. That pattern fits rate coding: the model communicates values through many spikes over time, so the computation becomes spiking but still produces substantial activity. The surrogate gradient SNN moves lower again, around 15 mJ and about 12,000 spikes, because training directly shapes useful spiking patterns. The STDP-based SNN sits at the sparsest and lowest-energy end, around 5 mJ and about 4,000 spikes. The shape of the comparison teaches a practical rule: fewer spikes generally mean fewer event-driven operations, and fewer operations tend to reduce energy.

**Question.** If the ANN has the highest accuracy, why would anyone choose a spiking model?

**Answer.** A spiking model can deliver a large reduction in energy per inference while keeping accuracy in a usable range. The surrogate gradient SNN is the clearest balanced case here: it stays close to ANN accuracy while using only a small fraction of the ANN's energy. The STDP-based SNN pushes energy even lower, which can be valuable when power savings matter more than matching the ANN's accuracy exactly.

The difference between converted SNNs and surrogate gradient SNNs also shows why "spiking" alone does not determine efficiency. A converted SNN begins from an ANN-like solution and translates activity into spikes. That translation can preserve useful performance, but the model may need many spikes to represent what the original continuous activations were doing. A surrogate gradient SNN is trained with spiking behavior in the loop, so the learning process can shape when spikes occur and reduce unnecessary activity. In practical terms, the converted SNN is like replacing spoken words with repeated taps after the message has already been written, while the surrogate-trained SNN learns to compose the message in taps from the beginning.

STDP-based learning follows a different logic. Spike-Timing Dependent Plasticity changes connection strength based on the timing relationship between spikes. When this produces very sparse activity, the network spends very little energy because most neurons remain silent for much of the inference. Silence becomes useful computation: no spike means no event to process. The cost is visible in the accuracy columns, where STDP-based performance falls below the more accuracy-oriented models. That makes the STDP result a genuine tradeoff rather than a simple victory. It gives the strongest energy result, and that strength is attached to a specific accuracy cost.

**Question.** Does lower spike count automatically mean a better model?

**Answer.** Lower spike count usually helps energy efficiency, but model quality also depends on the accuracy reached with those spikes. A very sparse model that misses too many labels solves the energy problem while weakening the prediction task. The comparison works because spike count, energy, and accuracy are read together: the best choice is the model whose prediction quality and cost match the intended use.

The mental model is a budget. Accuracy is the benefit purchased by computation. Energy is the price paid for each prediction. Spike count is one reason the price changes across spiking models, because sparse event-driven activity avoids many operations that dense continuous computation performs. The ANN buys the strongest raw accuracy with the highest energy bill. The converted SNN cuts that bill substantially while still carrying some cost from many rate-coded spikes. The surrogate gradient SNN finds a balanced region, keeping accuracy close to the ANN while reducing energy strongly. The STDP-based SNN spends the least energy by using the sparsest activity, while accepting a lower accuracy result. Reading the results together means asking which model gives the right amount of correctness for the energy budget available.
