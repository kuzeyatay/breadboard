## Latency Results

Imagine a small edge device watching a stream of events and needing to decide quickly whether a hand gesture has occurred. The device cannot wait forever for more evidence, because a late decision can be useless even if the final label is correct. Latency measures this waiting time: it is the time between presenting one input sample and receiving the model's decision. In a spiking model, latency is closely tied to the simulation window, the span of simulated time during which spikes are allowed to accumulate before the model commits to an output. A longer window gives the model more spike evidence, but it also delays the answer.

A conventional ANN-style CNN processes values in a dense, continuous way. Once the input is available, the model runs through its layers and produces an output, with reported latency typically in the 20-50 ms range depending on model complexity and hardware configuration. A spiking model changes the timing problem because information arrives as discrete spike events. A spike is a brief signal at a particular moment, and the model's decision depends on how enough of these events build up over time. If the useful spikes arrive early, the model can decide quickly. If the model needs many spikes spread across a longer simulation window, the decision is delayed.

The latency comparison places four strategies beside one another: ANN CNN, Converted SNN, Surrogate Gradient SNN, and STDP-based SNN. The comparison is useful because these names describe different ways of getting a model to produce decisions, and those choices affect how quickly evidence appears. A converted SNN begins with an ANN-like model and translates its activations into spiking behavior. That conversion can preserve much of the original model's usefulness, but the spiking version may need a longer window so that spike rates approximate the continuous activation values it came from. In practice, that means a converted SNN can carry a delay around 20 ms when it waits for enough spikes to represent those rates reliably.

The table makes the comparison concrete by putting the response delays side by side. The ANN CNN sits in the tens-of-milliseconds range, reported around 20-50 ms. The converted SNN is shown around 20 ms, which places it faster than many ANN cases but slower than the most direct low-latency spiking strategy in this comparison. The surrogate-gradient SNN is reported around 10 ms, making it the clearest low-latency option among the listed strategies. The STDP-based SNN is slightly slower during early inference because its spike behavior adapts gradually, but under optimized conditions it stabilizes below about 15 ms. Reading the table this way turns latency into a model-selection clue: the fastest method is not simply "any SNN," but the SNN whose training strategy produces useful spikes early enough for a quick decision.

Surrogate-gradient training gives the spiking model a direct way to learn spike patterns that support the task. The difficulty with spikes is that they are discrete events, while ordinary gradient training works most naturally with smooth changes. A surrogate-gradient method uses an approximate learning signal so the model can still be trained toward better decisions. For latency, the practical effect is that the model learns when to spike, not only whether to spike. If the decisive spikes occur early in the simulation window, the model can reach a reliable output around 10 ms. A useful analogy is listening for the first few notes of a familiar tune: if the notes are distinctive, you do not need the entire song to recognize it. A surrogate-trained SNN aims to make the early spike pattern distinctive enough for the model to decide quickly.

A converted SNN has a different timing burden. Since it often represents ANN activations through spike rates, it may need time to count enough spikes for those rates to become meaningful. Suppose one output pathway is supposed to represent a stronger activation than another. In a rate-coded spiking version, the stronger pathway should emit more spikes over the window. If the window is too short, the observed spike counts may be noisy or incomplete. Extending the window makes the rate estimate more stable, but that extension is paid for as response delay. This is the simulation-window cost: the model buys a more faithful spike-based approximation of continuous activations by spending more time before deciding.

The graph turns the table's numbers into a shape that is easier to compare at a glance. The surrogate-gradient SNN appears as the shortest latency among the four model categories, matching the reported low-latency response of about 10 ms. The STDP-based SNN sits above it but remains below the converted SNN under optimized conditions, showing that gradual spike adaptation can still support fast inference once the spike pattern stabilizes. The converted SNN's taller bar reflects the delay added by a longer simulation window. The ANN CNN bar gives the familiar dense-computation baseline, with latency that can range higher depending on the specific model and hardware setup. The visual pattern says that latency is shaped by how the model obtains its evidence: learned early spike timing, accumulated rate-coded spikes, or dense continuous computation.

STDP-based SNNs follow another route. STDP stands for spike-timing dependent plasticity, a learning rule where timing relationships between spikes influence how connections change. For latency interpretation, the central point is that an STDP-based model may need a short early phase in which spike activity settles into a useful pattern. That makes it slightly slower than the surrogate-gradient SNN in early inference, yet it can still stabilize to sub-15 ms latency when optimized. A beginner might expect a spike-timing method to always be the fastest because it is built around timing. The better mental picture is that using spike timing for learning and producing the earliest possible decision are related but separate goals. STDP can create sparse and efficient spike behavior, while surrogate-gradient training is especially effective here at shaping spikes for quick task-level decisions.

**Question.** If SNNs use brief spike events, why can a converted SNN still have higher latency than a surrogate-gradient SNN?

**Answer.** A converted SNN often needs a longer simulation window because it is trying to approximate ANN activation values through spike activity, especially through spike rates. The model may need enough time for the number of spikes to represent those values reliably. A surrogate-gradient SNN is trained directly as a spiking model, so it can learn spike patterns that support an earlier decision, producing reported latency around 10 ms.

Latency comparison only becomes meaningful when it is read beside the cost of getting the decision. The cost here is not only computation in the ordinary sense; it is also waiting time inside the simulation window. A method that needs more simulated milliseconds before its spikes become informative has a larger response delay. A method that forms a decisive spike pattern early can stop sooner or produce a useful output sooner. That is why the same label "SNN" does not guarantee the same latency. Converted SNNs, surrogate-gradient SNNs, and STDP-based SNNs all use spikes, but they organize spike evidence differently.

**Question.** Does the lowest latency always mean the best model choice?

**Answer.** The lowest latency identifies the fastest response in this comparison, which is the surrogate-gradient SNN at about 10 ms. Model choice still depends on what the application needs from the decision. If the task requires quick, accurate responses, the surrogate-gradient result is especially attractive. If the design values a different tradeoff, such as using a converted model for compatibility with an ANN workflow or using an STDP-based model where adaptive spike behavior is suitable, a slightly longer response delay may be acceptable.

The clean mental model is to think of latency as the time needed for evidence to become decision-ready. An ANN CNN waits for dense layer computations to finish. A converted SNN waits long enough for spike activity to approximate the activation strengths it inherited from an ANN. A surrogate-gradient SNN learns spike timing that can make the answer visible early. An STDP-based SNN lets timing-shaped spike behavior settle and can still reach low latency once optimized. The comparison reveals that fast SNN inference comes from the way spikes are trained and used, not from spikes alone.
