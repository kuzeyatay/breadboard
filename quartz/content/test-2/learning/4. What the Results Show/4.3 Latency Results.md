After comparing models by how well they classify and how much activity they spend, the next result asks a different practical question: how long does a model make the world wait before it decides? Imagine a small drone flying through a hallway. Its camera or event sensor receives a stream of changing signals, and the model must decide whether the shape ahead is open space, a wall, or a moving person. A very accurate decision that arrives too late can still lead to a collision. Latency is the time between receiving an input and producing a usable output. In real-time inference, the model is judged by whether that time is short enough for the surrounding system to act while the situation is still current.

A millisecond is one thousandth of a second, so latency numbers such as 10 ms or 20 ms sound small at first. They become meaningful when the task repeats continuously. If a robot updates its decision many times per second, every extra millisecond reduces the time left for control, communication, and physical movement. A 10 ms inference leaves much more room in the control loop than a 50 ms inference. The latency result therefore changes how SNN performance should be interpreted: a model can be attractive because it reacts quickly, even when another model has slightly better raw accuracy or a simpler training story.

The latency comparison places four model types side by side: a conventional ANN/CNN, a converted SNN, a surrogate-gradient SNN, and an STDP-based SNN. An ANN/CNN processes continuous-valued activations through layers. Its decision time depends on the number of operations, the model size, and the hardware running those operations. In the reported benchmark range, CNN-based ANNs typically require about 20-50 ms per sample. That means the ANN can be strong at recognizing patterns, but its continuous computation can make real-time deployment harder when the device has tight timing limits.

The table makes latency feel like a concrete engineering quantity rather than a vague claim of speed. Each row names a model family, and the latency column gives the approximate decision time in milliseconds. The ANN/CNN row sits in the slower range, about 20-50 ms. The converted SNN row is around 20 ms. The surrogate-gradient SNN row is around 10 ms. The STDP-based SNN row stabilizes below about 15 ms under optimized conditions. Reading the table this way shows that "SNN" is a broad category: the training method and coding strategy strongly affect how soon the network can decide.

A converted SNN starts from an already trained ANN and translates its activations into spikes. The conversion is useful because it borrows the ANN's learned structure, but the resulting SNN often needs a longer simulation window to estimate what the original continuous activation would have been. A simulation window is the time interval over which the spiking network is allowed to emit and collect spikes before the output is trusted. If the network uses rate coding, a stronger signal is represented by more spikes over time. Waiting for enough spikes gives a better estimate, but waiting also adds latency. That is why converted SNNs can remain competitive while still showing delays around 20 ms.

A surrogate-gradient SNN is trained directly as a spiking model while using an approximation that allows gradient-based learning despite the sharp, discrete nature of spikes. The word "surrogate" means that the training process uses a smooth stand-in for the spike's abrupt threshold during learning. The deployed network still communicates through spikes, but the training has shaped when those spikes should occur. Because the model learns spike timing patterns directly, it can produce useful responses in a shorter window. The result around 10 ms shows why this training method is often attractive when accuracy and reaction time must be balanced together.

The graph turns the table into a visual ranking of response speed. Lower bars mean shorter decision time. The surrogate-gradient SNN appears near the low end, around 10 ms, which visually separates it from the ANN/CNN range and from the converted SNN's longer delay. The STDP-based SNN also lands in the low-latency region once optimized, with sub-15 ms behavior, although its early inference can be slightly slower because its spike patterns adapt gradually. The converted SNN sits higher because it may need more time steps to approximate ANN-like activation levels. The ANN/CNN sits in the broad 20-50 ms region, showing that strong conventional models can demand more waiting time depending on complexity and hardware.

STDP-based SNNs learn through spike-timing dependent plasticity, where the relative timing of spikes influences how connections change. If one neuron tends to spike just before another, the connection between them can be strengthened; if the timing relationship is weaker or reversed, the connection can change differently. This kind of learning is local and biologically inspired, and it can lead to sparse activity. For latency, the result is slightly nuanced: early responses may need time to settle because the network's useful spike pattern emerges through gradual adaptation, but optimized STDP systems can stabilize below about 15 ms. In a real deployment, that means STDP can be fast enough for many responsive systems while also offering advantages in sparse spiking behavior.

**Question.** If surrogate-gradient SNNs and STDP-based SNNs are both in the low-millisecond range, why does the training method still matter for latency?

**Answer.** The training method shapes how quickly useful spikes appear. A surrogate-gradient SNN is directly trained to produce effective spiking responses, so it can often reach a decision in about 10 ms while keeping accuracy competitive. An STDP-based SNN can also become fast, reaching sub-15 ms under optimized conditions, but its early inference behavior may be slightly slower because its spike responses adapt more gradually. The two models may both be fast enough for real-time use, yet they reach that speed through different mechanisms.

Latency-sensitive deployment means choosing a model with the timing demands of the application in mind. A gesture-recognition system on neuromorphic hardware can tolerate only a small delay if it is meant to respond naturally to a person's movement. An edge device watching an event-based sensor may need to classify each sample in 5-10 ms so that downstream control stays smooth. A monitoring device with looser timing may accept 20 ms if the model is easier to train or provides another benefit. The latency result therefore adds a selection rule: the best model is the one whose decision time fits the loop it must serve.

A beginner may wonder whether lower latency automatically means the model has "thought less" and must therefore be less reliable. The better mental picture is that a spiking model can encode useful evidence in the timing of events, so an early spike pattern can already carry enough information for a decision. A network that has learned good temporal structure can decide quickly because the first meaningful spikes are informative. A network that depends on counting many spikes over a longer window waits because its evidence accumulates more slowly.

**Question.** Why can a converted SNN be slower than a directly trained SNN even though both use spikes?

**Answer.** A converted SNN often represents ANN-like activation values through spike rates, so it may need to observe spikes over a longer simulation window before the output is stable. A directly trained surrogate-gradient SNN learns spiking behavior as part of training, so it can place useful spikes earlier and reach a decision sooner. The difference comes from how information is encoded over time, not simply from the presence of spikes.

The latency results change the interpretation of SNN performance by adding time as a first-class part of model quality. Accuracy says whether the final decision is usually right. Energy and spike activity say how much computational effort the model spends. Latency says whether the answer arrives while it can still guide action. In that mental model, surrogate-gradient SNNs stand out when a system needs a fast response with competitive accuracy, STDP-based SNNs become compelling when sparse adaptive spiking can still meet the timing budget, converted SNNs offer a bridge from ANN models with some added delay, and ANN/CNN baselines remind us that familiar high-performing models may carry a reaction-time cost. A real-time SNN is best understood as a decision system whose value depends on when its answer becomes available, not only on what answer it eventually gives.
