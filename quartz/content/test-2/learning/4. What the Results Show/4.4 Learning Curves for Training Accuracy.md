### Learning Curves for Training Accuracy

After watching loss fall across epochs, the natural question is what that falling loss buys for the learner: does the network actually make more correct predictions as training continues? Imagine training three spiking systems on the same recognition task. At the start, each system is mostly guessing. After every pass through the training data, called an **epoch**, you pause and ask a simple question: "Out of all the training examples shown, what fraction did the model classify correctly?" That fraction is **training accuracy**. If 100 examples are checked and 82 are classified correctly, the training accuracy is 82%. A **learning curve** plots that accuracy after each epoch, so the curve becomes a time record of how the model's usable skill grows during training.

Loss and accuracy answer different parts of the same story. Loss is sensitive to how confident or mistaken the model's internal outputs are, so it can keep changing even when the number of correct predictions changes slowly. Accuracy counts the practical outcome: correct or incorrect. A model can reduce loss because its correct answers become more confident, while its accuracy stays nearly flat for a few epochs. A model can also jump in accuracy when many examples cross the decision boundary from wrong to right, even if the loss curve looks less dramatic. Reading accuracy beside loss gives a fuller picture of convergence: the network is converging when its training process is settling into predictions that are both lower-loss and increasingly correct.

For spiking neural networks, this comparison is especially useful because the training methods learn in different ways. A **converted SNN** begins from a conventional neural network and translates its activity into spikes, often using spike rates over a simulation window to approximate the original activations. A **surrogate-gradient SNN** is trained directly with a workaround that lets gradient-based learning adjust spike-producing units even though spikes themselves are discrete events. An **STDP-based SNN** adjusts connections using spike timing relationships, so learning grows from local timing patterns rather than a direct global error signal. These differences shape the accuracy trajectory: how quickly the curve rises, how smoothly it rises, and where it levels off.

The graph turns training accuracy into a visible trajectory across epochs. The horizontal axis is the number of epochs, so moving left to right means watching the same method after more training. The vertical axis is training accuracy, so moving upward means the method is classifying a larger share of the training examples correctly. The shape of each line matters as much as its endpoint. A steep early rise means the method learns useful decision patterns quickly. A flatter line means each additional epoch adds less correct classification than before. A curve that levels off has reached a practical ceiling under the shown training setup: more epochs in the displayed range add little extra training accuracy.

The surrogate-gradient curve rises fastest and reaches the highest accuracy among the SNN methods shown. That shape matches the role of direct supervised optimization: the model receives a training signal aimed at improving task performance, and the surrogate gradient supplies a usable path for adjusting parameters despite the spike event's abrupt on-or-off nature. In practical terms, the curve says that by around the later part of the 20-epoch window, this method has already captured much of what it can learn from the training setup. Its accuracy trajectory complements the earlier loss behavior: a steep loss decline becomes meaningful because it is accompanied by a strong rise in correct classifications.

The converted SNN curve follows a more moderate path. Its accuracy improves over epochs and reaches a competitive level, but the rise is less direct than the surrogate-gradient trajectory. A useful way to picture this is to imagine translating a fluent sentence into a compressed code: much of the original meaning survives, but the translation has constraints. Converted SNNs inherit useful structure from an ANN, yet they must express that structure through spike activity, often over longer windows and with more spikes. The accuracy curve shows that conversion can preserve substantial performance, while the pace and ceiling of improvement reflect the cost of representing continuous activations through spike-based activity.

The STDP-based curve grows more slowly and settles lower. STDP, or spike-timing dependent plasticity, strengthens or weakens connections based on the relative timing of spikes. If one neuron consistently spikes just before another, their connection can become stronger; if the timing relationship is different, the connection can weaken. This local timing rule is powerful for forming efficient spike patterns, but it does not aim every update directly at the classification target in the same way a supervised gradient method does. The accuracy curve therefore climbs gradually and reaches a lower final training accuracy in the displayed comparison. That lower ceiling connects to the broader performance tradeoff: STDP-based SNNs can use very few spikes and very low energy, but the training accuracy curve shows the performance side of that bargain.

**Question.** If the surrogate-gradient SNN already has the lowest loss, why look at training accuracy too?

**Answer.** Loss tells how well the model's internal outputs match the training objective, including confidence and error size. Training accuracy tells how often those outputs produce the correct class. A falling loss becomes more convincing when the accuracy curve also rises quickly and reaches a high level. In the surrogate-gradient case, the two views reinforce each other: the model is reducing its training error signal and turning that improvement into correct predictions over the 20-epoch training window.

A beginner might expect the highest final accuracy to automatically identify the best method. The accuracy curve gives only one dimension of the decision. SNN methods are often compared across accuracy, energy consumption, spike count, latency, and convergence behavior. A curve that reaches a high final accuracy may require more spikes, longer simulation windows, or greater computational cost. A curve that reaches a lower final accuracy may still be valuable when the application values energy efficiency and sparse activity. The learning curve only becomes decision-ready when read beside its cost: how many epochs, spikes, milliseconds, and millijoules are needed to obtain that accuracy.

The phrase **final accuracy** means the accuracy at the end of the plotted training window, here the value reached by the last epoch shown. It is tempting to treat that number as the whole result, but the path to that number carries practical information. Suppose two methods both end near the same training accuracy after 20 epochs. If one reaches most of that accuracy by epoch 8 and the other only approaches it near epoch 20, the faster method offers better **learning speed** under the same training budget. Learning speed is the rate at which accuracy improves over epochs. On the graph, it appears as the steepness of the curve: a steeper upward slope means more accuracy gained per epoch.

**Question.** What does it mean when an accuracy curve rises early and then becomes almost flat?

**Answer.** The model has gained most of the training accuracy available under that setup. Early epochs taught it patterns that improved many predictions, and later epochs changed fewer incorrect predictions into correct ones. The flat region is a practical plateau: additional training in the shown range gives only small accuracy gains, so judging the method requires looking at whether that plateau is high enough and whether the cost of reaching it is acceptable.

The safest mental model is to read an accuracy learning curve as a growth trace of usable skill. The starting point shows how the method behaves before much adaptation. The slope shows how quickly training turns examples into correct predictions. The final height shows the accuracy ceiling reached within the plotted epochs. The comparison among converted, surrogate-gradient, and STDP-based SNNs shows three different balances: conversion preserves competitive performance through spike-based representation, surrogate-gradient training learns quickly and reaches the strongest training accuracy, and STDP-based learning favors sparse, efficient adaptation with a slower and lower accuracy trajectory. Loss tells whether the training objective is being optimized; accuracy tells whether that optimization is becoming correct behavior. Together, they let you see convergence as both a numerical settling process and a practical improvement in what the spiking network can do.
