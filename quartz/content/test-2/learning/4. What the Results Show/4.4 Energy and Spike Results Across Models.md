### Energy and Spike Results Across Models

After seeing how inference means running a trained model on one input, it helps to ask what each inference costs. Imagine a small camera sensor on a battery-powered device watching a doorway. Most frames may contain nothing new, but a conventional artificial neural network still pushes continuous numerical values through every layer each time it makes a prediction. That prediction may be accurate, yet the device pays for it in energy every time the model runs. A spiking neural network changes the accounting: activity happens through discrete spike events, so a quiet input can lead to less internal activity. Once inference is viewed this way, accuracy alone gives an incomplete picture. Two models can both classify well, while one spends far more energy and generates far more internal events to reach its answer.

Energy per inference is the amount of energy a model uses to process one input and produce one output. If a model uses 200 millijoules per inference, then each single prediction costs about 200 mJ. If another model uses 5 millijoules per inference, then each prediction costs about 5 mJ. The unit "millijoule" measures energy, and the phrase "per inference" makes the number comparable across models because each value refers to one completed prediction. This metric lets you ask a practical deployment question: how expensive is one answer from this model?

Spike count per inference measures how many spike events are generated while processing one input. A spike is a discrete event, so spike count gives a direct way to compare how active different spiking systems are during prediction. A high spike count means many events must be communicated and processed. A low spike count means the model reaches its answer with sparser activity. In event-driven computing, sparse activity is valuable because silence can reduce work. For example, if one model produces about 20,000 spikes for one prediction and another produces about 4,000, the second model is using only one-fifth as many spike events for that inference. When energy and spike count fall together, the model is doing less event-based work and using less power to produce each answer.

The model comparison becomes clearer when energy consumption and spike count are placed side by side.

![Comparison of energy consumption and spike count per inference across neural network models](/test-2/assets/source-visuals/2510-27379v1-page-9-graph-g1-comparison-of-energy-consumption-and-spike-count.png)

*Comparison of energy consumption and spike count per inference across neural network models* *(p. 9)*

The graph shows a steady movement from dense, high-energy computation toward sparse, low-energy computation. The ANN sits at the high end, around 200 mJ per inference, because it relies on continuous-valued operations rather than spike-based activity. The converted SNN drops energy sharply to about 20 mJ per inference, which already shows how moving into a spiking format can reduce energy use. Its spike count remains high at roughly 20,000 spikes per inference, so the converted model still performs a large amount of event activity. This makes sense for a converted SNN that uses rate-coded spike propagation: information is represented by many spikes over time, so energy improves, but spike traffic stays relatively large.

The surrogate gradient SNN lowers both measures further, using about 15 mJ per inference and around 12,000 spikes. Surrogate gradient training directly learns spiking patterns, so it can shape the model toward more efficient event use while preserving strong performance. The result is a balanced profile: lower energy than the converted SNN, fewer spikes, and competitive accuracy compared with conventional models. For a device that needs real-time predictions while still saving power, this middle position is useful because it reduces cost without pushing the model to the sparsest possible behavior.

The STDP-based SNN occupies the lowest-energy, lowest-spike corner of the comparison. It uses about 5 mJ per inference and generates roughly 4,000 spikes. Spike-Timing Dependent Plasticity, or STDP, is a learning rule based on the timing relationship between spikes. In this setting, its sparse, event-driven activity leads to the smallest energy and spike count values among the compared models. That makes STDP-based SNNs attractive for ultra-low-power tasks, especially when the application can tolerate lower classification accuracy or slower convergence in exchange for minimal energy use. A simple way to picture this is a hallway sensor that only needs to detect broad changes over long periods: it benefits more from conserving battery than from squeezing out the last point of accuracy on every classification.

The same comparison can be read in a deployment-oriented way through the energy efficiency summary.

![SNN energy efficiency summary by model](/test-2/assets/source-visuals/2510-27379v1-page-9-table-t1-snn-energy-efficiency-summary-by-model.png)

*SNN energy efficiency summary by model* *(p. 9)*

The table organizes the result by model, energy consumption, and energy efficiency. The "model" column separates the design choices: ANN, converted SNN, surrogate gradient SNN, and STDP-based SNN. The "energy consumption" column gives the per-inference cost, so it answers how much energy one prediction uses. The "energy efficiency" column translates that cost into a practical judgment about suitability. ANNs have the highest energy use, so they are least attractive when power is the limiting constraint. Converted SNNs improve energy consumption substantially, but their relatively high spike count means they still carry a heavier event-processing load. Surrogate gradient SNNs sit in a strong middle range, combining competitive performance with reduced energy. STDP-based SNNs receive the strongest efficiency interpretation because they require the least energy per prediction and produce the fewest spikes.

**Question.** If the converted SNN uses much less energy than the ANN, why is its high spike count still worth noticing?

**Answer.** Energy per inference tells you the total energy cost of one prediction, while spike count per inference tells you how much event activity happened inside the model during that prediction. The converted SNN improves energy dramatically, from about 200 mJ to about 20 mJ, so it is much more efficient than the ANN. Its roughly 20,000 spikes still matter because high spike traffic can create communication and processing demands in event-driven systems. The model saves energy overall, but it reaches that saving with many spike events.

Low-power model selection means choosing a model based on the full cost of making predictions, rather than choosing only the highest-accuracy option. Accuracy answers, "How often is the prediction right?" Energy per inference answers, "How much power does one prediction spend?" Spike count per inference answers, "How much event activity does the prediction require?" These three questions can point to different choices. An ANN may dominate raw accuracy, but it also sits at the highest energy level in this comparison. A surrogate gradient SNN can be the better fit when the application needs strong accuracy with a meaningful reduction in energy. An STDP-based SNN can be the better fit when the application prizes long battery life, sparse activity, and ultra-low-power operation.

A beginner confusion often comes from assuming that fewer spikes automatically means a better model in every setting. Fewer spikes are valuable when energy and event traffic are the main constraints, but a model still has to meet the task's performance needs. The STDP-based SNN reaches the lowest energy and spike count, yet it is associated with reduced accuracy and slower convergence compared with stronger supervised approaches. The right interpretation is practical rather than absolute: the best model depends on which cost the deployment can afford.

**Question.** Why can an STDP-based SNN be a good choice even if another model has higher accuracy?

**Answer.** An STDP-based SNN can be a good choice when the deployment is energy-constrained. At about 5 mJ per inference and roughly 4,000 spikes, it uses much less energy and produces much less spike activity than the other compared models. If the task can tolerate some reduction in accuracy, the large energy savings can make STDP-based SNNs more suitable for ultra-low-power settings.

The mental model is to treat each inference as a small transaction. Accuracy tells you whether the transaction produced the right answer. Energy per inference tells you how much power the transaction spent. Spike count per inference tells you how much event activity the model used while spending that power. Dense continuous computation pays a high cost for every answer. Spiking computation can reduce that cost by communicating through events, and the sparsest STDP-based models show how far that reduction can go. When results are read through all three lenses together, the models stop looking like a simple accuracy ranking and start looking like deployment choices: ANN for raw accuracy, surrogate gradient SNN for a balanced accuracy-efficiency tradeoff, and STDP-based SNN for the lowest-energy path when ultra-low-power operation is the main goal.
