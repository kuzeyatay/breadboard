### Convergence Results Across Training Strategies

After seeing how spiking neural networks can trade accuracy, latency, energy, and spike count against one another, training speed gives us one more way to judge whether a method is practical. Imagine training three SNNs for the same recognition task. At the start, each model makes many mistakes because its internal parameters have poor settings. During each epoch, the model gets another pass through the training data and adjusts itself. If the training loss falls quickly, the model is learning useful internal patterns with fewer passes. If the loss falls slowly or settles high, the model needs more training time or reaches a less precise solution. Convergence is the moment when the curve has mostly stopped improving, so the remaining training epochs add little.

Training loss is useful because it turns "how wrong is the model?" into a single number that can be tracked over time. A high loss means the model's predictions disagree strongly with the desired answers. A low loss means the model's predictions are closer to the target behavior. When comparing training strategies, the shape of the loss curve matters as much as the final value. A steep early drop means the method finds better parameters quickly. A flat or gently declining curve means learning is more gradual.

The clearest way to read the convergence behavior is to follow the loss curves from left to right. The surrogate-gradient SNN drops fastest and stabilizes around the 20th epoch, which means its training rule is giving it useful direction early in learning. The converted SNN improves more gradually, because it inherits behavior from an ANN and then must express that behavior through spikes over simulation time. The STDP-based SNN declines more slowly and tends to plateau at a higher loss, which fits its role as a local, spike-timing-driven learning method rather than a direct accuracy-optimizing method.

*Convergence behavior of SNN models training loss across epochs* *(p. 10)*

In this loss graph, the steepness of each curve is the visual clue. The surrogate-gradient curve falls sharply, so each early epoch buys a large improvement. By about epoch 20, the curve has flattened, which suggests that the model has already reached most of the improvement available under that training setup. The converted SNN curve also moves downward, but its improvement is less direct because rate-coded spike propagation often needs longer simulation windows to approximate the continuous activations it came from. The STDP-based curve improves more slowly and levels off higher, showing a training path that is gentler and less tightly aimed at minimizing classification loss.

A beginner might expect the lowest-energy method to also be the best training method, but the curves show why model choice depends on the goal. STDP-based learning can produce sparse spiking and very low energy use, yet its training loss does not fall as quickly as the surrogate-gradient method. That pattern means STDP can be attractive when the system values low spike count, low power, and local adaptation, while surrogate-gradient training is better when the model must reach strong performance quickly.

The training-loss table gives a second way to check the same interpretation. A graph makes the trend easy to see, while a table lets you compare the actual loss values at specific epochs. If the surrogate-gradient row reaches a low value by the later epochs while the STDP row remains higher, the table confirms that the faster visual drop is also a numerical advantage. If the converted SNN sits between them, it supports the idea that conversion gives competitive performance but with a slower or less efficient path than direct surrogate-gradient training.

![Training loss across epochs for converted, surrogate gradient, and STDP-based SNN models](/test-2/assets/source-visuals/2510-27379v1-page-10-table-t1-training-loss-across-epochs-for-converted-surro.png)

*Training loss across epochs for converted, surrogate gradient, and STDP-based SNN models* *(p. 10)*

The table should be read across each row as a training story. The epochs mark repeated learning passes. The loss values show how far the model still is from the desired prediction behavior. The surrogate-gradient SNN reaches its stable low-loss region quickly, which supports the claim that it converges by about the 20th epoch. The converted SNN reduces loss too, but its path reflects the cost of translating an ANN-style representation into spikes. The STDP-based SNN changes more slowly, which matches its spike-timing rule: it adjusts connections from local timing relationships rather than from a global error signal designed to push classification loss down rapidly.

Training accuracy gives the same comparison from the opposite direction. Loss asks, "How wrong is the model?" Accuracy asks, "How often is the model right?" During successful training, loss usually moves downward while accuracy moves upward. The accuracy curve is especially helpful because learners often care about the practical question: after a certain number of epochs, which method is already useful?

![Training accuracy learning curves across converted, surrogate-gradient, and STDP-based SNNs](/test-2/assets/source-visuals/2510-27379v1-page-11-graph-g1-training-accuracy-learning-curves-across-convert.png)

*Training accuracy learning curves across converted, surrogate-gradient, and STDP-based SNNs* *(p. 11)*

The accuracy curves should be compared by their rise, not only by where they finish. A curve that rises quickly means the model becomes useful earlier in training. The surrogate-gradient SNN climbs rapidly, matching the fast loss reduction seen before. This pairing is important: the model is not merely reducing a numerical training objective; it is also turning that optimization into better prediction performance. The converted SNN can reach competitive accuracy, but its improvement tends to be less immediate. The STDP-based SNN rises more slowly and may settle at a lower accuracy, which fits its strength in sparse, low-energy operation rather than maximum supervised classification performance.

**Question.** If surrogate-gradient training reaches strong accuracy quickly, why would anyone choose STDP-based training?

**Answer.** STDP-based training can be the better fit when energy use, spike count, and local adaptation matter more than fastest supervised convergence. It can use far fewer spikes and much less energy per inference, around 5 mJ in the reported comparison, while surrogate-gradient SNNs use more energy and spikes in exchange for faster convergence and stronger accuracy. The slower curve is a cost, but the low-power behavior is the benefit.

The optimization tradeoff becomes clearer when training speed is placed beside operating cost. Surrogate-gradient SNNs are the fast learners in this comparison: their loss falls rapidly, their accuracy rises quickly, and they stabilize by about epoch 20. That makes them well suited for settings where a system needs competitive accuracy and quick training behavior. Converted SNNs are useful when an existing ANN-style model needs to be moved into a spiking form, though the conversion can carry extra spike activity and longer simulation needs. STDP-based SNNs give up some convergence speed and classification accuracy, but they gain very sparse activity and low energy consumption.

**Question.** Does "fast convergence" mean the model is always the best choice?

**Answer.** Fast convergence means the model reaches a stable training state in fewer epochs. It does not by itself decide the best model. A fast-converging model can still use more spikes or more energy than another method. The right choice depends on the task: fast supervised learning favors surrogate-gradient SNNs, while ultra-low-power or unsupervised adaptation can favor STDP-based SNNs.

A useful mental model is to picture each training strategy as paying for improvement with a different currency. Surrogate-gradient training pays with a more direct optimization signal, so it buys rapid loss reduction and quick accuracy gains. ANN-to-SNN conversion pays with inherited ANN behavior, then spends extra spike activity and simulation time to express that behavior in events. STDP pays with patience: it learns from spike timing more gradually, and in return it can operate with very sparse activity and low energy. The convergence curves show the speed of learning, the accuracy curves show when the model becomes useful, and the loss table checks the trend with concrete values. Together, they show that training speed only becomes meaningful when read beside the cost of achieving it.
