## Reading Latency Results

Imagine a small robot moving through a hallway while its camera watches for a hand gesture, a doorway, or an obstacle. The robot does not have the luxury of thinking for a full second after each visual change. A late decision can mean turning after it has already passed the doorway or braking after it has already reached the obstacle. In this setting, accuracy alone gives an incomplete picture. A model that recognizes the right pattern too slowly can still be a poor real-time system. Latency names this waiting time: the delay between receiving an input and producing a usable decision.

Latency is usually measured in milliseconds, abbreviated **ms**. One millisecond is one thousandth of a second, so 10 ms is 0.010 seconds and 50 ms is 0.050 seconds. Those numbers look tiny on paper, but real-time systems often repeat decisions many times per second. A model with 10 ms latency can, in principle, respond within one hundredth of a second for each sample. A model with 50 ms latency takes five times as long for the same decision. When the input stream is continuous, that difference changes how quickly the system can react to new events.

The latency comparison below places four model types side by side: an ANN/CNN, a converted SNN, a surrogate-gradient SNN, and an STDP-based SNN. Read the bars as decision-time costs. Shorter bars mean the model reaches an inference result sooner.

![Latency comparison in milliseconds across ANN and SNN models](/test-2/assets/source-visuals/2510-27379v1-page-8-graph-g1-latency-comparison-in-milliseconds-across-ann-an.png)

*Latency comparison in milliseconds across ANN and SNN models* *(p. 8)*

The shape of the comparison is the main lesson. The ANN/CNN sits in the higher-latency range, around the 20-50 ms scale depending on complexity and hardware. That range can still be fast for many ordinary applications, but it gives less room for tight real-time loops. The converted SNN is also around the 20 ms region because it often needs a longer simulation window. A simulation window is the period over which the spiking system is allowed to emit spikes before the output is read. If the model needs many time steps to approximate the activation values of the original ANN, the decision arrives later.

A converted SNN begins from a trained artificial neural network and translates its continuous activations into spike-based behavior. This translation is useful because it can preserve much of the ANN's learned structure, but the converted model often represents information through spike rates. A spike rate means "how many spikes occurred during a time interval." If a neuron needs time to accumulate enough spikes for the rate to become clear, the model cannot always decide immediately. A rough analogy is counting claps to estimate how enthusiastic a crowd is: one clap tells you something happened, but several moments of clapping give a more stable estimate. That waiting period is the simulation-window cost.

A surrogate-gradient SNN takes a more direct route. It trains the spiking model itself using a method that can adjust the network even though spikes are discrete events. A spike is sudden, more like an on/off pulse than a smooth number, so ordinary gradient training needs a workaround. The surrogate gradient supplies a trainable approximation during learning, allowing the network to shape spike timing and spike patterns for the task. The result shown here is a low-latency SNN near 10 ms while remaining close to ANN-level accuracy. That makes the surrogate-gradient model especially strong when the goal is not simply to save energy, but to respond quickly while still recognizing patterns reliably.

The STDP-based SNN also belongs to the low-latency side of the comparison, with optimized behavior stabilizing below about 15 ms. STDP stands for **Spike-Timing Dependent Plasticity**, a learning rule in which the relative timing of spikes influences how connections change. If one neuron tends to spike just before another, the connection can be strengthened; if the timing relationship is different, the connection can weaken. This makes STDP naturally tied to temporal structure. In the latency comparison, it performs faster than the ANN/CNN range and faster than the converted SNN's typical simulation-window burden, though it is described as slightly slower in early inference phases because its spike adaptation develops gradually.

The table should be read row by row as a practical model-selection guide. The ANN/CNN row represents the familiar dense-computation baseline, with latency commonly in the 20-50 ms range. The converted SNN row shows that changing an ANN into a spiking model can reduce some costs, but the need to run over a longer spike window can keep latency around 20 ms. The surrogate-gradient SNN row is the strongest low-latency accuracy candidate here, reaching about 10 ms. The STDP-based SNN row falls below about 15 ms under optimized conditions, making it attractive when sparse, adaptive spiking behavior is more important than matching the highest supervised accuracy.

A beginner confusion often appears around the word "fast." A model can be fast in different ways. It can use little energy, produce few spikes, converge quickly during training, or respond with low inference latency. The latency table is about the last meaning: how long the model takes to produce a decision after input arrives. A model with excellent energy savings still needs to be checked for decision delay, because an ultra-low-power system that waits too long may fail in a live control loop. In the same way, a highly accurate model needs a latency check before it can be called suitable for real-time use.

**Question.** If the converted SNN is spike-based, why is it not the fastest model in the latency comparison?

**Answer.** A converted SNN often uses spikes to approximate the continuous activation values of an already trained ANN. To make that approximation reliable, it may need to observe spikes across a longer simulation window. The model is spike-based, but the decision can be delayed while enough spikes accumulate to represent the original ANN-like signal. That is why its latency can sit around 20 ms instead of near the 10 ms surrogate-gradient result.

The strongest real-time interpretation comes from comparing latency with the purpose of the system. For a batch image classifier running on stored data, 20-50 ms may be acceptable. For neuromorphic vision, robotics, or edge AI reacting to incoming events, every millisecond reduces the gap between sensing and action. A surrogate-gradient SNN near 10 ms offers a useful balance because it combines low decision time with accuracy close to the ANN baseline. An STDP-based SNN below about 15 ms also fits real-time use, especially when sparse activity and low power are central design goals. The ANN/CNN baseline can still be accurate, but its higher latency range gives SNNs a real advantage when the environment changes quickly.

**Question.** Does the lowest latency automatically mean the best model?

**Answer.** Low latency is valuable only beside its cost. A real system also cares about accuracy, energy use, spike count, and training behavior. The surrogate-gradient SNN is appealing because it combines low latency with competitive accuracy. The STDP-based SNN is appealing when sparse activity and energy savings matter strongly. The converted SNN can be useful when preserving an ANN's behavior is the priority, even if the simulation window adds delay.

The mental model is to picture each architecture as answering the same question under a different timing rule. The ANN/CNN processes dense numerical activity and may take tens of milliseconds before the answer is ready. The converted SNN speaks in spikes, but often waits long enough for spike rates to stand in for the old continuous activations. The surrogate-gradient SNN learns to make spiking decisions directly, so it can reach a strong answer in a short window of about 10 ms. The STDP-based SNN adapts through spike timing and can settle into low-latency behavior below about 15 ms. Reading the latency results means asking how quickly each model can turn input into action, then matching that delay to the demands of the real-time system.
