## Performance Summary Across Models

After looking separately at accuracy, latency, energy use, spike count, and convergence, the full comparison asks a more practical question: if an SNN must run in a real application, which model should be chosen? Imagine a small edge device that must recognize events from a sensor while running on limited battery power. A model with the highest accuracy may look attractive at first, but the device also has to respond quickly, avoid wasting energy, and keep its spike activity sparse enough for efficient neuromorphic hardware. A performance summary exists because model choice depends on all of these pressures at once.

A multi-model comparison begins by placing several candidate systems in the same frame. Here the comparison includes a conventional ANN/CNN baseline, a converted SNN, a surrogate-gradient SNN, and an STDP-based SNN. The ANN/CNN represents the familiar dense-computation approach, where numerical activations are processed continuously through the network. The SNN models represent event-based computation, where activity is carried by spikes. The converted SNN starts from an ANN-like trained model and translates its behavior into spikes. The surrogate-gradient SNN trains spike-based behavior with gradient-style optimization adapted for the difficulty of spikes. The STDP-based SNN uses spike timing as the learning signal, so learning follows the relative timing between presynaptic and postsynaptic spikes.

The table gives the first integrated reading because it places the models beside the reported quantities that make selection meaningful: model type, accuracy, latency, energy consumption, spike count, and convergence behavior.

*SNN performance summary across models* *(p. 7)*

Read the table across each row as a profile rather than as a scoreboard. The model name tells you which training strategy and computational style produced the results. Accuracy tells you how often the model makes the correct prediction. Latency tells you how long the model needs before producing its decision. Energy consumption tells you the cost per inference, measured in millijoules, where one inference means one completed prediction. Spike count tells you how many spike events the SNN generates during that inference. Convergence describes how quickly training settles into useful performance. These columns belong together because an SNN that saves energy by producing fewer spikes may also change accuracy or training behavior, and an SNN that responds quickly may require a different training method than one optimized mainly for sparse activity.

The ANN/CNN baseline anchors the comparison. It tends to dominate in raw accuracy, but it pays for that strength with dense continuous-valued operations. Its reported energy consumption is about 200 mJ per inference, much higher than the SNN alternatives. That number gives scale to the rest of the comparison: when an SNN uses 20 mJ, 15 mJ, or 5 mJ per inference, the difference is not a small adjustment; it is a shift in the operating cost of the model.

A simple percentage calculation makes the size of the energy reduction concrete. Energy saving can be computed as

Energy saving percentage = ((baseline energy - new energy) / baseline energy) x 100.

Here, baseline energy is the ANN/CNN energy per inference, and new energy is the energy used by the SNN being compared. If the ANN/CNN uses 200 mJ and the STDP-based SNN uses 5 mJ, then the saving is ((200 - 5) / 200) x 100 = 97.5%. This tells you that the STDP-based SNN uses only a small fraction of the baseline energy for each prediction. The calculation does not say that STDP is best for every use; it says that energy is where STDP becomes especially strong.

The graph makes the energy-and-spike side of the comparison easier to feel visually.

![Performance analysis and energy consumption of SNNs versus ANN](/test-2/assets/source-visuals/2510-27379v1-page-7-graph-g1-performance-analysis-and-energy-consumption-of-s.png)

*Performance analysis and energy consumption of SNNs versus ANN* *(p. 7)*

The tallest energy value belongs to the ANN/CNN, at roughly 200 mJ per inference. The converted SNN drops energy to about 20 mJ, but its spike count remains high at roughly 20,000 spikes per inference. That shape matches its strategy: conversion can preserve competitive performance, but rate-coded spike propagation often needs many spikes and longer simulation windows. The surrogate-gradient SNN moves further toward efficiency, using about 15 mJ and about 12,000 spikes while keeping accuracy close to the ANN range, around 97.8% in the reported comparison. The STDP-based SNN sits at the sparse end, around 5 mJ and about 4,000 spikes, which makes it especially attractive when energy and spike economy matter more than squeezing out the last bit of accuracy.

**Question.** If the STDP-based SNN uses the least energy, why would anyone choose a surrogate-gradient SNN?

**Answer.** A surrogate-gradient SNN gives a stronger balance when accuracy and response time still matter heavily. It keeps accuracy close to the ANN baseline, reaches low latency around 10 ms, and converges faster, with useful behavior by about the 20th epoch. STDP uses less energy and produces fewer spikes, but its accuracy is slightly lower and its convergence is slower. For a battery-powered sensor that can tolerate a small accuracy drop, STDP may fit well. For a real-time classifier that still needs high accuracy, the surrogate-gradient SNN may be the better match.

The converted SNN occupies a middle position with its own tradeoff. It can achieve competitive performance because it inherits structure from an ANN, but it often needs more spikes and longer simulation windows. A beginner might think conversion should automatically give the best of both worlds: ANN-like accuracy and SNN-like efficiency. The reported pattern gives a more precise picture. Conversion can reduce energy dramatically compared with the ANN/CNN baseline, but its spike count remains higher than the other SNN approaches. Since spike events are part of the computation cost, a converted SNN can be less efficient than its energy number alone might suggest.

**Question.** Should accuracy be treated as the main number, with energy and latency used only as secondary checks?

**Answer.** Accuracy should be read beside the costs that produce it. A high-accuracy model that uses much more energy may be poorly matched to an edge device. A low-energy model that converges slowly may be awkward when training speed matters. A low-latency model may be preferred in real-time settings even if another model saves a little more energy. The comparison becomes useful because it reports accuracy, latency, energy consumption, spike count, and convergence together.

This is how performance summary turns into model selection. If the application values raw predictive performance above all else, the ANN/CNN baseline remains a strong reference. If the application needs an SNN with high accuracy and fast response, the surrogate-gradient SNN becomes attractive because it balances accuracy near the ANN level with low latency and moderate energy. If the application needs an SNN derived from an already trained ANN, the converted SNN can be useful, while its higher spike count and longer simulation requirements must be accounted for. If the application is energy-constrained and can accept a slight accuracy reduction with slower convergence, the STDP-based SNN becomes compelling because its spike activity and energy use are the smallest.

The mental model is a control panel rather than a ladder. Turning the priority toward accuracy favors one model; turning it toward energy favors another; turning it toward latency or convergence changes the choice again. The comparison works because every model is evaluated beside the costs it actually reports. An SNN is therefore best understood as a design choice shaped by the task: spikes create opportunities for sparse, energy-aware computation, and the right training strategy determines which balance of accuracy, speed, energy, spike count, and convergence the system delivers.
