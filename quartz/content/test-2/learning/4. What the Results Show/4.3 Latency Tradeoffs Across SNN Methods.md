## Latency Tradeoffs Across SNN Methods

Once a spiking network has learned to classify inputs through conversion, surrogate-gradient training, or STDP, the next practical question is how long it must listen before it can answer. Imagine a hand-gesture sensor feeding events into a small robot. A fast response lets the robot turn, stop, or grasp while the gesture is still unfolding. A slow response can still be accurate on a benchmark, but the robot may act after the useful moment has passed. Latency measures this response time: the time from presenting an input to producing a usable output decision, usually reported in milliseconds per sample.

A conventional ANN or CNN processes an input through layers of continuous numerical activations. Once the input is available and the hardware runs the operations, the network produces an output after a forward pass. An SNN has an extra temporal ingredient: spikes arrive over time, and the network may need several time steps before the output neuron has enough evidence to decide. The total duration allowed for those time steps is the **simulation window**. If the window is 20 ms, the model is allowed to accumulate spikes for 20 milliseconds before its output is read. A shorter window can produce quicker inference, but only if the network has learned to place useful information early in the spike train.

Converted SNNs make the simulation window especially visible. In ANN-to-SNN conversion, a trained ANN is translated into a spiking version by making spike rates approximate the ANN's continuous activations. A high ANN activation becomes many spikes over time; a low activation becomes fewer spikes. The decision becomes reliable after the spike counts have had enough time to represent those rates. For example, if two output classes have similar early spike counts, a converted SNN may need additional milliseconds before one class clearly wins. That waiting time is the cost of representing a static activation value through repeated events.

The table places the four response-time patterns side by side. The ANN/CNN baseline sits in the tens-of-milliseconds range, around 20-50 ms depending on model and hardware. Converted SNNs are reported around 20 ms, which is faster than many CNN cases but still shaped by the need for a long enough simulation window to approximate activation rates. Surrogate-gradient SNNs reach about 10 ms, showing that direct training can make spikes useful earlier. STDP-based SNNs can stabilize below 15 ms under optimized conditions, though their early inference can be slower because their spike patterns adapt gradually.

Surrogate-gradient training reaches low latency through a different route. Instead of converting a continuous network after training, the spiking network is trained directly. The difficulty is that a spike is a hard event: it fires or it does not fire, so ordinary gradient descent cannot smoothly follow the exact spike function. A **surrogate gradient** uses a smooth approximation during learning so the model can adjust its weights toward useful spike timing. The learned result can place decisive spikes earlier, because the training process rewards spike patterns that solve the task within the temporal dynamics of the SNN itself.

A simple way to picture this is to compare two students answering from evidence. The converted SNN is like a student tallying votes: it waits until enough marks accumulate to estimate the original ANN's activation levels. The surrogate-trained SNN is like a student trained to recognize the most diagnostic clue as soon as it appears. Both can reach the right answer, but the second route can reach it with fewer moments of waiting.

**Question.** If converted SNNs use spikes, why can they still need more time than surrogate-gradient SNNs?

**Answer.** Converted SNNs often use spike rates to imitate the continuous activations of an ANN. A rate is measured over a window of time, so the network may need enough spikes before the output reflects the original activation pattern reliably. Surrogate-gradient SNNs are trained directly with spiking behavior in mind, so they can learn to make informative spikes arrive earlier instead of waiting for a rate estimate to become clear.

Latency by itself can hide the reason a model is fast. A 10 ms result means the output arrives quickly, but it does not reveal whether the model used many spikes in that short time, how much energy the hardware consumed, or how much accuracy was preserved. A model can answer quickly by firing many spikes at once, which may reduce the energy advantage that made spiking attractive. Another model can use very few spikes and save energy, but may need a slightly longer settling period. Reading latency alongside training method helps reveal the mechanism behind the number.

STDP-based SNNs show this clearly. STDP, or spike-timing dependent plasticity, adjusts connections based on the relative timing of spikes. If a presynaptic neuron tends to fire just before a postsynaptic neuron, their connection can strengthen; if the timing is less useful, the connection can weaken. This local timing rule can produce sparse, efficient spiking patterns, but its early responses may adapt more gradually than directly supervised surrogate-gradient training. Under optimized conditions, STDP-based models can still reach sub-15 ms latency, so they remain relevant for fast systems, especially when low spike count and low energy are also valuable.

**Question.** Should the lowest latency model always be chosen for a real-time edge device?

**Answer.** The lowest latency model is attractive when immediate response is the main constraint, but deployment also depends on accuracy, spike count, energy per inference, and hardware support. A surrogate-gradient SNN may offer a strong balance with about 10 ms latency and competitive accuracy. An STDP-based SNN may be slightly slower in early inference while using fewer spikes and less energy. A converted SNN may be simpler to obtain from an existing ANN but can require a longer simulation window.

The mental model is to treat latency as the visible response time produced by a hidden temporal strategy. Converted SNNs often wait long enough for spike rates to stand in for ANN activations. Surrogate-gradient SNNs learn spike timing directly, so they can place decision-making evidence early and reach low-latency inference. STDP-based SNNs build timing-sensitive, sparse responses that can become fast after adaptation. A response-time comparison tells you who answers quickly; the training route explains why that speed appears and what tradeoffs may be hidden behind it.
