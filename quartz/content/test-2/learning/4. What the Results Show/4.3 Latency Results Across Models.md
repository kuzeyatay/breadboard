### Latency Results Across Models

After seeing that spiking networks can trade accuracy, energy, and spike activity against one another, the remaining question is how quickly each model can reach a decision. Imagine a small robot moving through a hallway. Its camera sees a hand gesture, an obstacle edge, or a sudden motion cue. A useful model has to classify that input quickly enough for the robot to react while the event is still relevant. Latency is the time between receiving an input sample and producing the model's decision. In this setting, latency is decision time measured in milliseconds, so a lower number means the system can respond sooner.

A conventional ANN-style CNN processes information through dense numerical operations. Each layer transforms arrays of continuous values, and the model produces its answer after the required computation finishes. A spiking model works with events over time, so its decision can depend on when enough spikes have arrived during a simulation window. A simulation window is the span of simulated time the SNN is allowed to observe before deciding. If the window is long, the model has more time to accumulate spike evidence, but the decision arrives later. If the window is short, the model must form a confident answer from fewer spike events. Low-latency inference means the model reaches a usable decision with a short decision time, often because its training has shaped spike timing so that informative spikes arrive early.

The latency comparison places four model types side by side: an ANN CNN, a converted SNN, a surrogate gradient SNN, and an STDP-based SNN. The ANN CNN provides the familiar dense-computation reference point, typically around 20-50 ms depending on model complexity and hardware. The converted SNN starts from an ANN and translates its activations into spiking behavior. Because this conversion often relies on rate-coded spike propagation, it may need a longer simulation window so spike rates can approximate the original ANN activations. That pushes its latency to about 20 ms in the comparison. A surrogate gradient SNN is trained directly as a spiking model using a learning method that makes spike-based decisions trainable despite the sharp, event-like nature of spikes. Its latency is about 10 ms, showing that direct spiking training can support fast responses while remaining competitive in accuracy. An STDP-based SNN learns through spike-timing dependent plasticity, where timing relationships between spikes shape the connections. It can stabilize to sub-15 ms latency under optimized conditions, though it may be slightly slower during early inference because its spike adaptation unfolds gradually.

A beginner confusion often comes from treating "spiking" as automatically faster. Spikes are discrete events, but an SNN still needs enough events to support a decision. A converted SNN may wait longer because it is trying to represent an ANN's continuous activation level through spike rates. For example, if a high activation is represented by many spikes across a time window, the model may need to wait long enough to count or accumulate that rate reliably. A directly trained surrogate gradient SNN can learn patterns where earlier spikes carry more useful evidence, so the model can decide sooner.

**Question.** If a converted SNN is already a spiking model, why can it be slower than a surrogate gradient SNN?

**Answer.** A converted SNN often inherits the logic of the ANN it came from. The ANN used continuous activation values, and the converted SNN may approximate those values through spike rates. Estimating a rate can require observing spikes over a longer simulation window, so the model waits longer before the decision is reliable. A surrogate gradient SNN is trained directly around spike-based behavior, so useful decision evidence can appear earlier in the spike sequence.

For latency-sensitive deployment, the best model is the one whose decision time fits the environment's response deadline while still meeting accuracy and energy needs. A gesture-recognition interface, an event-camera perception system, or a real-time robotics controller benefits when inference happens in only a few milliseconds. In benchmark settings with event-based datasets such as DVS Gesture and SHD, SNNs can reach inference latencies as low as 5-10 ms per sample on neuromorphic platforms. That range is especially meaningful because event-based inputs already arrive as changes over time, so a model that can respond quickly to those events preserves the timing advantage of the sensor.

**Question.** Does the lowest latency automatically make one model the best choice?

**Answer.** Low latency is strongest when fast reaction is the binding requirement. The surrogate gradient SNN is compelling for fast decisions because it reaches about 10 ms while remaining competitive in accuracy. The STDP-based SNN may be chosen when sparse spikes and low energy are especially valuable, even if its early inference behavior is slightly slower. The converted SNN can still be useful when preserving ANN-like behavior is attractive, but its longer simulation window makes it less ideal for the fastest response settings.

The mental model is to picture each model as needing a different amount of evidence before it can answer. The ANN CNN completes dense computations over continuous values. The converted SNN waits long enough for spike rates to stand in for those values. The surrogate gradient SNN has learned spiking patterns that make early evidence informative, so it reaches a decision quickly. The STDP-based SNN adapts through spike timing and can settle into low-latency behavior under optimized conditions. Latency results guide real-time choice because they translate model design into the time a system must wait before acting.
