## Spike Count as an Energy Clue

Once accuracy, latency, and convergence have been placed side by side, the remaining question is what a network spends each time it makes a decision. During inference, a model receives an input, passes activity through its layers, and produces an output such as a class label. A conventional artificial neural network keeps many numerical activations moving through arithmetic operations. A spiking neural network can make the same kind of decision with discrete events: a neuron stays quiet until its internal state reaches a threshold, then it emits a spike. That silence is useful. If fewer neurons need to spike, less switching activity is required, and the hardware has less work to do.

Energy per inference means the amount of energy used to process one input example and produce one output. If a gesture sample, sound sample, or image-like event stream enters the model, energy per inference asks: how many millijoules did this one decision cost? The unit millijoule, written mJ, is one thousandth of a joule. A lower value means the model can run longer on a battery, produce less heat, and fit better into small edge devices such as wearable sensors or neuromorphic chips.

Spike count gives a second view of the same cost. Total spike count per inference means the number of spike events generated while the model processes one input. A spike is a brief event, so a spike-based machine can often avoid constant dense computation. Each spike still has a cost: it must be routed, registered, and used to update downstream neurons. A model that fires 20,000 spikes for one decision is asking the system to handle far more events than a model that fires 4,000 spikes. The spike count becomes an energy clue because each event tends to contribute to the total activity the hardware must support.

The figure makes the relationship visible by placing energy consumption and spike count next to each model. The ANN CNN sits at the high-energy end, around 200 mJ per inference, because its computation is based on continuous-valued operations rather than sparse spike events. The converted SNN reduces energy sharply to about 20 mJ, yet it still produces roughly 20,000 spikes per inference. That combination tells us something subtle: converting an ANN into spikes can save energy, but rate-coded spike propagation can still require many events to represent information. The surrogate gradient SNN lowers both values further, reaching about 15 mJ and about 12,000 spikes. The STDP-based SNN is the sparsest case in this comparison, around 4,000 spikes and about 5 mJ per inference.

That pattern is the reason spike-count efficiency is useful. Spike-count efficiency means getting the needed decision-making behavior with fewer spike events. Imagine two people sending the same message by tapping on a table. One person taps continuously for a long time; the other sends a few carefully timed taps. If the listener can still understand the message, the second method uses less effort. STDP, or spike-timing dependent plasticity, encourages learning through the timing relationship between spikes. When spikes are organized by timing, the network can become selective about when it fires. A smaller number of well-placed spikes can carry useful information without filling the whole computation with activity.

The energy-efficiency table gives a compact comparison of the same tradeoff across model families.

The model column separates the approaches: ANN CNN, converted SNN, surrogate gradient SNN, and STDP-based SNN. The energy consumption column gives the approximate cost of one inference. The energy efficiency column turns those numbers into a practical ranking. ANN CNNs are powerful but expensive in this comparison, with the highest energy use. Converted SNNs gain a large reduction by using spikes, though their spike count remains high. Surrogate gradient SNNs occupy a middle position: they keep strong performance while using less energy and fewer spikes than converted SNNs. STDP-based SNNs reach the lowest energy consumption, about 5 mJ per inference, and the lowest spike count, about 4,000 spikes per inference.

The low-energy tradeoff appears when this result is read together with convergence behavior. Convergence describes how training settles over time. A method that converges quickly reduces its loss rapidly and stabilizes in fewer epochs. Surrogate gradient training tends to reach stable behavior faster, with strong accuracy and low latency. STDP-based learning converges more slowly and may settle at lower classification accuracy. Its advantage appears in a different part of the design space: it can produce very sparse activity and very low energy use.

A beginner confusion often comes from treating "lowest energy" as the same as "best model." Energy is one goal among several. If a medical alarm, robot controller, or gesture recognizer needs the highest possible classification accuracy under strict timing, a surrogate gradient SNN may be the better fit. If a tiny always-on sensor must run for a long time with very little power, an STDP-based SNN becomes attractive because each inference costs very little energy. The model choice depends on the constraint that dominates the application.

**Question.** If the STDP-based SNN uses the least energy, why would anyone choose a surrogate gradient SNN instead?

**Answer.** A surrogate gradient SNN can offer a stronger balance of accuracy, latency, and training behavior. It uses more energy than the STDP-based SNN in this comparison, about 15 mJ rather than about 5 mJ per inference, but it also tends to converge faster and maintain competitive accuracy. When a task needs reliable classification quickly, that balance can outweigh the extra energy cost.

The numbers also show why spike count should be interpreted carefully. A lower spike count usually points toward lower energy, and the figure follows that pattern: the STDP-based SNN has both the fewest spikes and the lowest energy. Yet spike count alone does not tell the whole story. A network can fire very few spikes and still miss useful distinctions in the input. Another network can fire more spikes and gain accuracy or faster stabilization. Spike count is a clue about efficiency, while accuracy, latency, and convergence explain what the model gives in return.

Energy per inference turns the result into a practical engineering question. Suppose an edge device must classify many inputs over a long deployment. At 200 mJ per inference, an ANN CNN spends forty times as much energy as a 5 mJ STDP-based SNN for each decision. Even without calculating a full battery budget, the direction is clear: repeated inference makes small per-decision savings accumulate quickly. A model that saves 195 mJ on one decision saves far more over thousands or millions of decisions.

**Question.** Does a lower spike count mean the network is always learning better?

**Answer.** Lower spike count means the network is using fewer spike events during inference, which usually helps energy efficiency. Learning quality depends on whether the trained network reaches useful accuracy and stable behavior. STDP can produce sparse, low-energy activity, but it may converge more slowly and achieve lower classification accuracy than methods trained with surrogate gradients.

The mental model is to see each inference as a budget. Accuracy asks how correct the decision is. Latency asks how quickly the decision arrives. Convergence asks how training settles. Energy asks how much the decision costs. Spike count reveals one major source of that cost because spikes are the events the system must process. STDP earns its place by spending very few spikes and very little energy, even though it asks for patience during training and may accept some accuracy loss. That is the low-energy tradeoff: slower convergence can be worthwhile when the main goal is sparse, efficient, always-on computation.
