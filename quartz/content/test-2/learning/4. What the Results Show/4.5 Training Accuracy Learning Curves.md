After watching the loss curves, the natural question is whether the models are only reducing a training penalty or actually becoming better at choosing the correct class. Training accuracy answers that question directly. Imagine training a spiking neural network to recognize a gesture from event-camera spikes. At the beginning, the model's internal spike patterns are poorly tuned, so many inputs push the output neurons toward the wrong class. After each pass through the training set, the model has another chance to adjust how input spikes influence later neurons. If those adjustments are useful, a growing fraction of examples will be classified correctly. A training accuracy curve tracks that fraction across repeated passes through the data, so it turns learning into a visible path rather than a single final score.

An epoch is one complete pass through the training data. If a model is trained for 20 epochs, it sees the whole training set 20 times, with its parameters updated along the way. Training accuracy at each epoch means: "After this much training, what percentage of training examples does the model classify correctly?" A curve that rises quickly means the model begins making reliable predictions early. A curve that rises slowly means the model needs more repeated exposure before its spike patterns become useful for classification. A curve that flattens means additional epochs are bringing smaller gains, so the model is approaching its current practical limit under that training method.

Accuracy curves complement loss curves because loss and accuracy answer related but different questions. Loss measures how strongly the model is being penalized for its mistakes and uncertain predictions. Accuracy measures how often the final decision is correct. A model can reduce loss before accuracy changes much, because it may become less confidently wrong while still choosing the wrong class. Then, once enough examples cross the decision boundary, accuracy can climb. For a learner, the accuracy curve is often easier to read as behavior: it shows when the network begins getting examples right often enough to be useful.

The graph compares three training paths over the same 20-epoch window: converted SNNs, surrogate-gradient SNNs, and STDP-based SNNs. Read the horizontal axis as training time measured in epochs, and read the vertical axis as how reliably each model predicts the right label on the training data. The most useful feature of the graph is the shape of each curve. A steep early rise means the model quickly turns training experience into correct predictions. A gentle rise means learning is happening more gradually. A plateau means the model has reached a region where more of the same training brings only modest improvement.

The surrogate-gradient curve is the clearest case of fast target attainment. Surrogate-gradient training is used because spikes create a practical problem for ordinary gradient learning: a spike is a sharp event, so the direct mathematical signal needed for smooth adjustment is difficult to use. The surrogate-gradient method supplies a smooth training signal around the spike event so the network can still learn which internal connections should strengthen or weaken. In the accuracy curve, that training advantage appears as a strong rise toward high accuracy within the 20 epochs. By the twentieth epoch, the model has effectively converged in this comparison: its training accuracy has stabilized near its useful level, so the learner should interpret the curve as evidence of quick and reliable supervised learning.

A converted SNN follows a different route. It begins from a conventional artificial neural network whose learned activations are translated into spiking behavior. This gives the spiking model a strong starting structure, so it can achieve competitive accuracy. Its curve should be read as a model that benefits from prior ANN-style training but may need longer spike-based simulation windows and more spike activity to represent values through firing rates. In the 20-epoch comparison, the converted SNN's accuracy rise shows useful performance, while its overall interpretation depends on the extra cost of producing enough spikes for that representation. If two models reach similar training accuracy, the one that needs fewer spikes, lower latency, or less energy may be the better practical learner.

The STDP-based curve represents a more local and biologically inspired learning rule. Spike-Timing Dependent Plasticity adjusts a connection based on the relative timing of spikes: if one neuron tends to fire just before another, their connection can be strengthened; if the timing relation is less useful, it can weaken. This rule can create efficient sparse spike activity, which is valuable when energy use is the main constraint. In the training accuracy curve, though, STDP-based learning rises more slowly and tends to settle at a lower level than the supervised methods. The shape tells the learner that local timing adaptation can produce useful structure, but it usually takes longer to become accurate for classification tasks that demand precise labels.

**Question.** If the loss curve already showed learning, why do we need the training accuracy curve?

**Answer.** The loss curve shows whether the training penalty is shrinking, while the training accuracy curve shows when the model begins making correct predictions reliably. Loss can improve even while many final class choices remain wrong, because the model may be becoming less uncertain or less severely mistaken. Accuracy translates the training process into the learner's practical question: after each epoch, how often does the model choose the right answer?

A common beginner confusion is to treat the highest curve as the complete result. Training accuracy is powerful, but it earns its meaning only beside cost. Spiking neural networks are often valued because they can reduce energy use, spike count, and latency by computing through discrete events. A model that reaches high training accuracy after 20 epochs may still be expensive if it needs many spikes or long simulation windows. A model that learns more slowly may still be attractive when it uses very sparse activity and very low energy. The learning curve tells how quickly correctness appears; the cost measurements tell what the correctness requires.

This is why the 20-epoch comparison should be read as a convergence story rather than a race with one universal winner. Surrogate-gradient SNNs show the strongest balance for tasks that need accurate decisions quickly: their curve rises efficiently and stabilizes by the twentieth epoch, aligning with fast convergence and competitive accuracy. Converted SNNs show that ANN knowledge can be carried into a spiking format, giving solid accuracy with tradeoffs tied to spike-based representation. STDP-based SNNs show slower accuracy growth, while offering the kind of sparse activity that can be valuable in energy-constrained settings.

**Question.** Does convergence by the twentieth epoch mean the model has learned perfectly?

**Answer.** Convergence means the curve has largely stabilized under the given training setup. It means additional epochs are expected to produce smaller accuracy gains, so the model has approached its practical training level for that method and configuration. It does mean perfect learning. The final accuracy still has to be judged against the task goal, and the model's usefulness still depends on latency, spike count, and energy cost.

The mental model is a set of three learning paths climbing toward reliable prediction. Loss curves show the pressure easing as the models adjust; accuracy curves show correct decisions becoming more frequent. Surrogate-gradient learning climbs quickly because it gives spike-based networks a usable training signal for supervised adjustment. Conversion starts from a useful ANN-shaped solution and translates it into spikes, often reaching competitive accuracy with extra representational cost. STDP adapts through local spike timing, learning more gradually while preserving the energy-friendly character of sparse spiking. Over 20 epochs, the accuracy curves reveal when each method becomes dependable, and the broader performance picture reveals what each method spends to get there.
