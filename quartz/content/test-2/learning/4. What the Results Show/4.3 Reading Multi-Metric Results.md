### Reading Multi-Metric Results

Imagine choosing an SNN for a small camera at the edge of a farm field. The camera watches crops all day, sends alerts when pests appear, and runs from a limited battery. Earlier metrics gave us separate lenses: accuracy tells how often the model is right, latency tells how quickly it responds, energy tells how much power each inference consumes, spike count tells how much event activity the network produces, and convergence time tells how quickly training settles into useful behavior. A real deployment forces these lenses to be read together, because the useful model is the one whose strengths match the constraint of the situation.

A single accuracy number feels tempting because it is easy to compare. If one model reaches about 98% accuracy and another reaches about 95.5%, the larger number seems like the obvious winner. In an SNN, that reading is too narrow for practical selection. A model can be highly accurate while producing many spikes, taking longer to simulate, or consuming more energy than the device can afford. Multi-metric evaluation means reading each result beside the cost paid to achieve it. Accuracy answers "How often does the model choose correctly?" The other metrics answer "How expensive is that correctness in time, energy, spikes, and training effort?"

The clearest way to read a results table is to begin with the deployment constraint. A deployment constraint is the practical limit that matters most for the use case. A medical alarm may place the strongest weight on low latency, because a correct warning that arrives too late loses value. A remote sensor may place the strongest weight on energy, because replacing or recharging batteries is expensive. A training pipeline may care about convergence time, because a method that becomes useful by the 20th epoch can be easier to tune and deploy than one that improves slowly. The same SNN result can look excellent or weak depending on which constraint governs the task.

Surrogate-gradient SNNs give a good example of a balanced result. They can closely approach ANN-level accuracy, with reported accuracy around 97.8% and an accuracy gap within about 1-2% of conventional neural networks. They also reach low latency, around 10 milliseconds, and show faster convergence by about the 20th epoch. Energy use remains far below a conventional ANN, with a reported value around 15 millijoules per inference. The spike count is moderate, around 12,000 spikes per inference. Read together, these numbers describe a method suited to applications that want high accuracy and fast responses while still gaining much of the energy advantage of spiking computation.

A converted SNN tells a different story. Conversion starts from a trained conventional network and expresses its activity through spikes. That often preserves competitive accuracy, which makes conversion attractive when accuracy is the main concern. The cost appears in the temporal side of the result: converted SNNs can require longer simulation windows and higher spike rates. A reported converted SNN energy value of about 20 millijoules per inference is far below an ANN value near 200 millijoules, yet its spike count around 20,000 spikes per inference is higher than the surrogate-gradient case. The interpretation is that conversion can keep performance strong, while the network may need more spike activity and more simulated time to express the computation.

STDP-based SNNs shift the tradeoff again. Spike-Timing Dependent Plasticity learns from the timing relationship between spikes, so the method naturally favors sparse event activity. The reported energy can be as low as about 5 millijoules per inference, with a spike count around 4,000 spikes. Those numbers are powerful for energy-constrained settings because fewer spikes usually mean fewer event-driven operations. The cost appears in accuracy and convergence: reported accuracy is around 95.5%, and convergence is slower than the surrogate-gradient case. For a battery-powered sensor that can tolerate a small reduction in accuracy, this result can be highly attractive. For a low-latency, high-accuracy application, the same result may be less suitable.

**Question.** If STDP uses the least energy, should it always be chosen for edge devices?
**Answer.** STDP is strongest when the energy budget dominates the design. If the device must respond extremely quickly or maintain the highest possible accuracy, a surrogate-gradient SNN may be a better fit because it combines about 97.8% accuracy, roughly 10 ms latency, and moderate energy use. The choice follows the constraint: ultra-low-power settings favor STDP, while low-latency accuracy targets favor surrogate-gradient training.

Spike count deserves special attention because it links the computational behavior of an SNN to its energy profile. A spike is an event, and event-driven systems spend much of their work when events occur. More spikes usually mean more communication and more computation. That is why the approximate sequence from ANN energy near 200 mJ, to converted SNN energy near 20 mJ, to surrogate-gradient SNN energy near 15 mJ, to STDP energy near 5 mJ is meaningful beside the spike counts. The lower-energy SNNs are doing less event work per inference. The numbers also show why energy should be read with spike count, because energy savings are easier to understand when the activity level is visible.

Latency and convergence can be confused because both involve time, but they answer different practical questions. Latency is the time needed to produce an answer during inference. If a surrogate-gradient SNN responds in about 10 milliseconds, that describes runtime behavior after training. Convergence time describes training behavior: how quickly the learning process reaches useful performance. A method that converges by the 20th epoch reduces training effort and can make experimentation faster. A method with low inference energy and slower convergence may still be valuable when training happens once and deployment lasts a long time. The correct reading depends on where time is costly: during training, during inference, or both.

**Question.** How can a model with lower accuracy still be the better model?
**Answer.** A lower-accuracy model can be better when the saved energy, reduced spike count, or deployment fit matters more than the last few percentage points of correctness. An STDP-based SNN around 95.5% accuracy with about 5 mJ per inference may serve a long-running sensor better than a more accurate model that spends three or four times as much energy. The result is judged against the job the model must perform.

A useful mental model is to picture each SNN method as occupying a position in a five-dimensional space. Accuracy pulls toward correctness. Latency pulls toward quick response. Energy pulls toward battery life and sustainable computation. Spike count pulls toward sparse event activity. Convergence pulls toward easier training and faster optimization. Surrogate-gradient training sits near the center of the practical balance: high accuracy, low latency, moderate energy, and fast convergence. Conversion sits near accuracy preservation, with higher spike activity and longer simulation needs. STDP sits near sparse, ultra-low-energy operation, with a cost in accuracy and training speed. Multi-metric reading turns scattered numbers into a design decision: choose the method whose costs are acceptable for the constraint that actually governs the deployment.
