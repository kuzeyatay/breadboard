### Latency Tradeoffs

After comparing training paradigms and simulation windows, latency becomes the practical test of whether a spiking model can act quickly enough for the world it is sensing. Imagine a hand-gesture sensor controlling a robot arm. The sensor receives motion as brief changes over time, the model must classify the gesture, and the robot must respond while the movement is still happening. If the model waits too long to collect spikes before deciding, its answer may be accurate but late. In a real-time setting, late recognition can behave like wrong recognition, because the useful moment for action has already passed.

Latency is the delay between the arrival of an input and the model's decision. In a spiking neural network, this delay is tied to the **simulation window**, the span of time over which the network is allowed to produce and process spikes before the output is read. A longer window gives the network more spike events to work with. More events can make a spike-rate estimate steadier, much like judging rainfall from a full minute of drops is steadier than judging it from one second. The cost is time: if the model needs a longer window before its output becomes reliable, the decision arrives later.

This is where the training paradigm changes the latency story. A converted SNN begins from an artificial neural network and translates its continuous activations into spike activity. That conversion often uses firing rates to approximate the original activation values. A high activation is represented by many spikes over time, and a lower activation is represented by fewer spikes. Rate information becomes clearer when the model observes a longer window, so converted SNNs can need more time before their spike counts faithfully approximate the original ANN behavior. The model can be competitive, but its decision process often depends on accumulating enough spikes.

A surrogate-gradient SNN is trained directly as a spiking model while using a training trick that makes spike-based learning possible with gradient descent. Spikes are discrete events, so their exact threshold crossing is hard to differentiate during learning. A surrogate gradient replaces that hard step during training with a smooth approximation, allowing the network to learn spike timings and patterns that support accurate decisions. Because the model learns as a spiking system from the beginning, it can learn to make useful decisions from shorter spike histories. That is why surrogate-gradient SNNs are strongly associated with low-latency performance, including latencies around 10 ms.

The latency graph makes the model-choice pressure visible. The ANN CNN sits in a higher latency range, around 20-50 ms depending on complexity and hardware. Converted SNNs improve on that range but remain around 20 ms when they need a longer simulation window to approximate ANN activation rates. Surrogate-gradient SNNs are shown near 10 ms, which places them among the fastest options while still preserving competitive accuracy. STDP-based SNNs can also reach low latency under optimized conditions, with sub-15 ms behavior, but their early inference can be slower because their spike adaptation develops gradually.

A beginner confusion often comes from thinking that an SNN is automatically low-latency because spikes are sparse. Sparse activity helps reduce computation and energy, but latency depends on when the model has enough evidence to decide. A network that fires few spikes may still wait across a longer time window if its decision rule needs accumulated evidence. A network with more spikes can sometimes decide sooner if its learned timing patterns quickly separate one class from another. Low latency comes from the whole inference process: spike timing, training method, simulation window, hardware, and the confidence needed for the task.

Read the table as a decision guide. The "model type" column separates the approaches: ANN CNN, converted SNN, surrogate-gradient SNN, and STDP-based SNN. The "latency in milliseconds" column tells you how long each approach tends to take per inference. The standout number for low-latency SNN design is the surrogate-gradient SNN value near 10 ms. Converted SNNs sit closer to 20 ms, reflecting the extra time often needed for rate-coded spike propagation. ANN CNNs can require 20-50 ms, which can be acceptable for offline classification but restrictive for fast control. STDP-based SNNs can reach below 15 ms in optimized settings, while also being known for very low energy use.

Latency-sensitive inference means choosing and evaluating the model around decision delay, rather than treating delay as an afterthought. A vision system sorting saved images can tolerate tens of milliseconds because the image is already captured. A hearing aid, a drone obstacle detector, or a gesture-controlled interface has a tighter timing budget because the input stream keeps changing. If a drone sees an obstacle and the model responds after the safe maneuver window has passed, the classification label becomes less useful. In these cases, a model with slightly lower accuracy but faster response may be the better engineering choice.

The decision-time tradeoff is the central idea: giving a spiking model more time can improve the evidence available for a decision, while demanding a faster answer can force the model to act from fewer spikes. Converted SNNs often benefit from longer windows because their spike rates need time to represent the ANN activations they imitate. Surrogate-gradient SNNs are attractive for low-latency targets because direct spiking training can shape early spike patterns into useful evidence. STDP-based SNNs bring a different advantage, especially low energy, but their gradual adaptation can make them less naturally aligned with the fastest early decisions.

**Question.** If converted SNNs can be accurate, why might they be a weaker choice for a real-time task than surrogate-gradient SNNs?

**Answer.** Converted SNNs often rely on spike rates to approximate the activations of the ANN they came from. Those rates become more reliable over a longer simulation window, so the model may need more time before deciding. Surrogate-gradient SNNs are trained directly with spiking behavior, so they can learn spike patterns that support decisions within shorter windows, with reported latency around 10 ms.

Latency-sensitive inference also has a cost beyond speed. A very short simulation window gives the model fewer spikes to inspect, which can reduce the evidence behind a decision. Pushing for the fastest possible answer can therefore pressure accuracy, stability, or robustness. The best model is the one whose timing fits the application while keeping the other performance demands within acceptable limits. For a battery-powered sensor, energy may dominate. For a safety-critical controller, decision delay may dominate. For a general classifier, accuracy may dominate. The latency result shows why there is no single best SNN design for every situation.

**Question.** Does a 10 ms latency mean the model is always better than a 20 ms model?

**Answer.** A 10 ms model is better when the task genuinely needs that faster response and its accuracy, energy use, and stability are acceptable. A 20 ms model can be suitable when the application has a wider timing budget or when the longer window improves reliability. Latency only becomes decisive when the environment demands decisions within a tight time limit.

A useful mental model is to picture each SNN approach as choosing how long it listens before speaking. A converted SNN often listens long enough to estimate activation-like firing rates. A surrogate-gradient SNN learns to make earlier spike events carry decision-ready information. An STDP-based SNN listens and adapts through local spike timing patterns, which can save energy while shaping responses more gradually. Low-latency performance belongs most clearly to surrogate-gradient SNNs because they combine direct spiking training with short decision windows, making them a strong fit when milliseconds determine whether an answer arrives in time.
