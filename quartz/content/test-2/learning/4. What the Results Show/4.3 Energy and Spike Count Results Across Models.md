### Energy and Spike Count Results Across Models

After seeing how spike count measures the amount of event activity in a spiking network, energy becomes easier to reason about: every spike is a small request for computation. Imagine a small camera sensor on a battery-powered device. Most of the time, the scene may be quiet, with only a hand gesture, a moving object, or a sudden change needing attention. A system that keeps pushing continuous numerical values through every layer spends energy even when the useful information is sparse. A spiking system can spend less because it communicates through events: when a neuron fires, downstream synapses are activated; when it stays silent, much of that activity can be skipped.

Energy per inference means the amount of energy used to process one input and produce one decision. The unit used here is the millijoule, written mJ. One inference is one completed pass from input to output, such as classifying one event-based gesture sample. This measure gives spike count a practical meaning. A model may be accurate, but if each decision costs too much energy, it becomes hard to use in edge devices, portable sensors, or neuromorphic systems where power is limited. Energy per inference only becomes meaningful beside its cost: the energy number has to be read together with the model's spike count, learning method, and performance tradeoffs.

The comparison across model types shows a clear pattern. The ANN CNN uses continuous-valued operations and has the highest reported energy use, about 200 mJ per inference. The converted SNN lowers energy to about 20 mJ, but it still produces about 20,000 spikes per inference because rate-coded spike propagation can require many spikes to approximate the behavior of the original continuous network. The surrogate gradient SNN reduces both values further, reaching about 15 mJ and about 12,000 spikes by learning spiking patterns directly with gradient-based training. The STDP-based SNN reaches the lowest reported energy, about 5 mJ per inference, with the sparsest activity at about 4,000 spikes.

The table is useful because it puts the two quantities beside each other rather than treating energy as a standalone score. The ANN CNN sits at the high-energy end because continuous activity keeps computation dense. The converted SNN shows that changing the representation to spikes can sharply reduce energy, but its large spike count reveals that many events are still being generated. The surrogate gradient SNN improves the pattern by using training to shape when spikes occur. The STDP-based SNN is the most sparse case in this comparison: fewer spikes mean fewer event-driven operations, and that helps explain the very low 5 mJ result.

Spike count and energy are linked, but they are separate measurements. Spike count tells how many spike events were generated during inference. Energy consumption tells how much power the whole inference required under the evaluation assumptions. A lower spike count usually pushes energy downward because fewer spikes trigger fewer synaptic operations. At the same time, total energy also depends on the cost of each synaptic operation and the hardware assumptions behind the measurement. A useful mental picture is a room with lights controlled by motion sensors. Fewer movements usually mean fewer lights turn on, but the final electricity use also depends on how efficient each light is. In the same way, fewer spikes reduce computational activity, while the hardware and synapse costs determine the final energy number.

*Comparison of energy consumption and spike count per inference across neural network models* *(p. 9)*

The graph makes the relationship visible as a downward movement from dense activity to sparse activity. The ANN CNN appears at the high end of energy use. The converted SNN drops energy substantially, but its spike count remains comparatively high. The surrogate gradient SNN moves further down in both energy and spike count. The STDP-based SNN lands at the lowest end of both scales, showing the strongest pairing of sparse spiking and low energy in this set of results. That shape explains why STDP-based SNNs are attractive when the design goal is ultra-low-power operation rather than the fastest training behavior.

STDP, or spike-timing dependent plasticity, adjusts connections using the timing relationship between spikes. In this result comparison, that timing-based local learning is associated with very sparse network activity. A beginner might expect the "best" model to be the one with the most computation, because more activity can feel like more effort. Spiking results encourage a different intuition: when the input and task allow it, a well-timed small number of events can carry enough information for a useful decision. The STDP-based result is an example of this sparse style, where the network fires less often and spends less energy per inference.

**Question.** If the STDP-based SNN has the fewest spikes, does that automatically explain all of its low energy use?

**Answer.** It explains a major part of the result because fewer spikes usually mean fewer synaptic events and less computation. The full energy value also depends on how expensive each synaptic operation is and what hardware assumptions are used. Spike count is therefore a strong clue about energy, while energy per inference is the complete reported cost of processing one input.

The converted SNN result helps sharpen the interpretation. It uses spikes and consumes far less energy than the ANN CNN, but its spike count remains high compared with the other SNN approaches. That happens because conversion often represents continuous activation strength through spike rates. A stronger activation may require more spikes over a simulation window, so the network can still generate many events. The model gains energy efficiency from event-based computation, while its rate-coded activity keeps spike count higher than the surrogate gradient and STDP-based cases.

The surrogate gradient SNN occupies a middle position in this energy-and-spike view. It is trained to work directly with spiking behavior, so it can use fewer spikes than the converted SNN while also reducing energy per inference. Its result shows that spike efficiency can be learned: the network can shape activity patterns so that information is carried by fewer events. The STDP-based SNN pushes this sparsity further in the reported comparison, reaching the lowest spike count and lowest energy value.

**Question.** Why would anyone choose the STDP-based SNN if another model converges faster?

**Answer.** A model choice depends on the operating goal. When the main constraint is battery life, heat, or always-on sensing, the reported 5 mJ per inference and roughly 4,000 spikes make the STDP-based SNN appealing. Faster convergence can be valuable during training, but low-power suitability is about how cheaply the trained system can process each input.

The results connect into one practical mental model: energy follows activity, and spike count is the visible trace of that activity. Dense continuous computation produces high energy use. Spike-based computation reduces energy by allowing silence to save work. Converted SNNs show a large energy drop while still producing many spikes. Surrogate-trained SNNs reduce the activity further by learning more efficient spike patterns. STDP-based SNNs, in this comparison, give the clearest example of sparse event-driven inference: few spikes, low energy per decision, and strong suitability for settings where every millijoule matters.
