### Convergence and Learning Curves Across Training Strategies

After comparing SNNs by accuracy, latency, energy use, and spike count, the next question is how each training strategy gets to its result. Imagine training three spiking models for the same task and checking them after every pass through the training data. One model may become useful quickly, another may improve steadily but need more time, and another may save energy while learning more slowly. A final accuracy number tells where the model ends up, but an epoch-by-epoch view shows the path it took to get there.

A **training epoch** is one full pass through the training data. After each epoch, the model can be measured by **training loss**, which is a score that becomes smaller when the model's predictions fit the training examples better. If loss falls rapidly, the model is adjusting its internal parameters in a useful direction. If loss falls slowly, the model is learning, but each epoch brings a smaller improvement. If loss flattens early, the model has reached a plateau where additional epochs produce limited progress.

A **loss curve** plots that loss value against the epoch number. The horizontal direction represents training time in epochs, and the vertical direction represents how much error remains. Reading the curve from left to right is like watching the model learn in slow motion. A steep downward slope means the model is correcting many errors quickly. A gentle downward slope means the model is improving at a slower pace. A nearly flat line means the model has mostly stopped gaining from more training under that strategy.

![Convergence behavior of SNN models training loss across epochs](/test-2/assets/source-visuals/2510-27379v1-page-10-graph-g1-convergence-behavior-of-snn-models-training-loss.png)

*Convergence behavior of SNN models training loss across epochs* *(p. 10)*

The loss curves show three different learning behaviors. The surrogate-gradient SNN has the steepest and most consistent loss decrease, so it reaches a low-loss region quickly. That shape means the training rule is giving the model a useful signal for how to change its spiking behavior from one epoch to the next. The converted SNN also reduces loss, but its curve descends more moderately, so it converges more gradually. The STDP-based SNN descends slowly and then levels off earlier, showing that its unsupervised spike-timing rule improves the model but reaches its useful training limit sooner.

The word **convergence** describes this settling process. A model has converged when additional epochs bring only small changes in the measured result. Convergence time is therefore the amount of training needed before the curve becomes mostly stable. Fast convergence means the model reaches its useful performance level in fewer epochs. Slow convergence means the model needs more epochs to approach its stable behavior, or it may stabilize at a less accurate level.

The table gives the same idea in a more exact form. A graph helps the eye recognize shape, while a table lets the learner check the actual loss values that create that shape. The rows follow epoch number, and the columns compare the converted SNN loss, surrogate-gradient SNN loss, and STDP-based SNN loss. Reading across a row compares the three strategies at the same training moment. Reading down a column follows one strategy through time.

![Training loss across epochs for converted, surrogate gradient, and STDP-based SNN models](/test-2/assets/source-visuals/2510-27379v1-page-10-table-t1-training-loss-across-epochs-for-converted-surro.png)

*Training loss across epochs for converted, surrogate gradient, and STDP-based SNN models* *(p. 10)*

The tabulated losses support the visual interpretation of convergence. The surrogate-gradient column drops most strongly and most steadily over the 20 epochs, matching the steep curve. The converted SNN column shows a smaller but continued reduction, matching moderate convergence. The STDP-based column changes less after its early progress, matching the plateau seen in the loss graph. Together, the table and curve teach the same reading habit: the comparison comes from how values change epoch by epoch, rather than from a single final number alone.

**Question.** If two SNNs reach similar final loss values, why should we still compare their loss curves across epochs?
**Answer.** Similar final loss can hide different training dynamics. One model may reach that level quickly and then stabilize, while another may need many more epochs to arrive there. The curve reveals convergence time, so it shows how efficiently the training strategy turns repeated exposure to data into improved behavior.

Loss tells how much error remains during training, but a learner also needs to know how often the model is correct. That is the role of **training accuracy**, the percentage of training examples classified correctly at each epoch. An **accuracy learning curve** plots accuracy against epoch number. Here the desired direction is upward: a rising curve means the model is becoming correct on more examples as training proceeds.

![Learning curves showing training accuracy versus epochs for surrogate-gradient, converted, and STDP-based SNNs](/test-2/assets/source-visuals/2510-27379v1-page-11-graph-g1-learning-curves-showing-training-accuracy-versus.png)

*Learning curves showing training accuracy versus epochs for surrogate-gradient, converted, and STDP-based SNNs* *(p. 11)*

The accuracy curves complete the picture started by the loss curves. The surrogate-gradient SNN rises quickly and approaches useful accuracy by around the 20th epoch, which matches its steep loss reduction. The converted SNN reaches competitive performance, but its path is slower because conversion relies on representing prior ANN-style activations through spike activity over simulation time. The STDP-based SNN improves more gradually and remains lower in accuracy, consistent with its slower loss convergence and early plateau.

A common beginner confusion is to treat "best" as whichever curve is highest at the end. In SNN results, the curve has to be read as a path through training. Surrogate-gradient training is strong when the goal is high accuracy with fast convergence and low latency. Converted SNNs can be competitive, but they often need more spike activity or longer simulation windows to express their learned behavior. STDP-based SNNs trade some accuracy and convergence speed for very sparse spiking and low energy use, which can be attractive when power limits dominate the design goal.

**Question.** Why can the STDP-based SNN be useful even when its accuracy curve rises more slowly?
**Answer.** Its slower rise means it converges less quickly and reaches a lower training-accuracy region, but its sparse spiking behavior supports very low energy use. For tasks where power consumption is the main constraint, that tradeoff can still make the STDP-based strategy valuable.

The mental model is a set of three moving traces. Loss curves show error draining away; accuracy curves show correct predictions accumulating. Surrogate-gradient training drains error fastest and raises accuracy quickly. Conversion follows a steadier middle path. STDP learns through local spike-timing adjustments, so its curve moves more slowly and stabilizes earlier. Reading these curves together turns training results into a story of learning speed, final usefulness, and the tradeoffs each SNN strategy brings into a neuromorphic system.
