## Training Loss and Accuracy Curves

After comparing SNN methods by final performance, energy use, latency, and spike activity, the next step is to watch learning unfold over time. Imagine training three students for the same task. If all you know is their final exam score, you can rank them at the end, but you cannot tell who learned steadily, who improved early and then stalled, or who needed many rounds of practice to become useful. Training curves give that missing time dimension. For a spiking neural network, each epoch is one full pass through the training data, and the curve records how the model changes after each pass.

A learning curve is a plot of a training metric across epochs. When the metric is training loss, the curve is called a loss curve. Training loss is a penalty score: lower loss means the model's outputs are better aligned with the desired answers. A useful loss curve usually slopes downward because the model is making fewer or smaller mistakes as training proceeds. The path of that curve is the training trajectory, meaning the route the model takes while learning, not just the place it reaches at the end.

Consider three SNN training approaches: a converted SNN, a surrogate-gradient SNN, and an STDP-based SNN. A converted SNN begins from an artificial neural network and translates its behavior into spikes. A surrogate-gradient SNN learns through a gradient-based method adapted for spiking activity, so it can adjust spiking patterns directly during training. An STDP-based SNN uses spike-timing dependent plasticity, where connection strengths change according to the relative timing of spikes. These approaches can all improve, but their curves reveal different kinds of improvement.

The loss table makes the comparison concrete because it shows the same story epoch by epoch. The columns separate the three training methods, while the rows show how their loss values change as training progresses. Reading down a column is like watching one model practice repeatedly. Reading across a row compares the models at the same moment in training.

![Training loss across epochs for converted, surrogate gradient, and STDP-based SNN models](/test-2/assets/source-visuals/2510-27379v1-page-10-table-t1-training-loss-across-epochs-for-converted-surro.png)

*Training loss across epochs for converted, surrogate gradient, and STDP-based SNN models* *(p. 10)*

The surrogate-gradient column shows the sharpest and most consistent decline. That means each training pass is producing a relatively strong improvement in the model's ability to match the target outputs. The converted SNN improves more moderately, so it is learning, but its steps are smaller. The STDP-based SNN decreases more slowly and tends to flatten earlier at a higher loss value. That flattening is called slow convergence when the model needs more epochs to approach stability, or reaches a stable region while still carrying more error than the other methods.

The same information becomes easier to interpret when drawn as a curve. A loss curve turns the table into a shape. A steep downward slope means rapid improvement. A shallow slope means gradual improvement. A curve that becomes nearly horizontal has reached a plateau, where extra epochs produce smaller gains.

![Convergence behavior of SNN models training loss across epochs](/test-2/assets/source-visuals/2510-27379v1-page-10-graph-g1-convergence-behavior-of-snn-models-training-loss.png)

*Convergence behavior of SNN models training loss across epochs* *(p. 10)*

In this loss graph, the surrogate-gradient SNN follows the most favorable training trajectory: its loss falls quickly and then stabilizes by the twentieth epoch. The converted SNN follows a middle path, reducing loss without matching the speed of the surrogate-gradient method. The STDP-based SNN has a slower descent and settles at a higher loss. The visual shape carries the interpretation. A final loss value tells where the method ended; the curve tells how much training effort was needed to get there and whether progress was still happening near the end.

**Question.** If two models finish with similar loss, why should we care about the shape of the loss curve?

**Answer.** The shape tells how quickly each model became useful. A model that reaches low loss early can need fewer training epochs, while a model that only reaches similar loss late has a longer training trajectory. The final value answers "where did it end?" The curve answers "how did it get there?"

Accuracy curves tell the same learning story from the opposite direction. Training accuracy measures the fraction of training examples the model classifies correctly. While loss should generally go down, accuracy should generally go up. A rising accuracy curve means the model is turning training experience into correct decisions. The speed of that rise shows how quickly the model becomes practically useful.

![Training accuracy learning curves over 20 epochs for surrogate gradient, converted, and STDP-based SNNs](/test-2/assets/source-visuals/2510-27379v1-page-11-graph-g1-training-accuracy-learning-curves-over-20-epochs.png)

*Training accuracy learning curves over 20 epochs for surrogate gradient, converted, and STDP-based SNNs* *(p. 11)*

The surrogate-gradient SNN approaches strong accuracy quickly, matching the loss behavior: its errors shrink rapidly, and its correct classifications rise rapidly. The converted SNN also improves, but its curve climbs more gradually. The STDP-based SNN rises more slowly and reaches a lower accuracy region, which fits its higher plateau in the loss curve. The two graphs are therefore two views of one training process. Loss describes how much penalty remains; accuracy describes how often the model is correct.

A common beginner confusion is to treat "efficient" as if it always means "learns fastest." In SNN results, efficiency can refer to energy use and spike activity, while learning speed refers to how many epochs are needed for the model to improve. STDP-based learning can be highly efficient in spike use and energy, yet its training curve can still show slow convergence. Surrogate-gradient learning can require more structured training, yet it often reaches useful accuracy faster. The curves separate these ideas so they do not get blended into one vague score.

**Question.** Does the most energy-efficient SNN automatically have the best training curve?

**Answer.** Energy efficiency and training speed describe different properties. An STDP-based SNN can use very sparse spiking activity and low energy while still converging slowly. A surrogate-gradient SNN can reach high accuracy more quickly even if it does not have the sparsest spike pattern. The training curve shows speed of improvement; energy measurements show cost of computation.

The mental model is to see training as a path through time. Each epoch gives the model another chance to adjust. The loss curve shows whether errors are shrinking, the accuracy curve shows whether correct decisions are increasing, and the slope of each curve shows how quickly the model is learning. A surrogate-gradient SNN looks strong when fast convergence and high useful accuracy are the goal. A converted SNN occupies a moderate path. An STDP-based SNN shows how a method can be efficient in spiking behavior while learning more slowly. Training curves therefore turn a single final score into a story of learning speed, stability, and practical tradeoff.
