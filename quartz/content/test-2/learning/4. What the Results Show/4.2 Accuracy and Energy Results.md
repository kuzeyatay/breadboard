## Accuracy and Energy Results

Imagine choosing a neural network for a small battery-powered camera that must recognize objects throughout the day. If two models both recognize most images correctly, the better choice is not decided by accuracy alone. One model may burn far more energy each time it makes a prediction, while another may save power by doing less computation and sending fewer spike events. A practical comparison therefore asks two questions together: how often is the model right, and how much energy does it spend to be right?

Accuracy measures the fraction of predictions that match the correct labels. If a model classifies 978 out of 1000 images correctly, its accuracy is 97.8%. Energy consumption measures the cost of running one inference, meaning one forward pass that produces one answer. In ordinary artificial neural networks, computation is usually dense: many numerical activations are updated whether the input has changed a lot or only a little. In spiking neural networks, computation is event-driven: neurons communicate through spikes, and fewer spikes usually mean fewer operations on neuromorphic hardware. This is why accuracy alone can hide energy and latency cost. A model can score highly while requiring many operations, many spike events, or a longer simulation window before it settles on an answer.

The clearest way to read the reported results is to begin with the familiar reference point: the ANN. The ANN has the strongest raw accuracy, especially on the harder CIFAR-10 setting, where it reaches about 92%. That makes it a useful ceiling for comparison. The central question for SNNs is how close they can get to that accuracy while using much less energy. When an SNN gets close to ANN accuracy, it is showing near-ANN accuracy: it approaches the conventional model's prediction quality while keeping the event-driven advantages of spikes.

The table places that tradeoff into one view. The ANN column gives the high-accuracy baseline. Converted SNNs are built by taking a trained ANN and translating its activity into spikes, so they usually keep competitive accuracy but may require many spikes and longer simulation behavior to represent values through firing rates. Surrogate Gradient SNNs are trained more directly for spiking behavior using gradient-based learning, so they preserve much of the accuracy while reducing energy more effectively. STDP-based SNNs use spike-timing-dependent plasticity, a local learning rule where the timing between pre-synaptic and post-synaptic spikes shapes connection strength. Their reported strength is energy efficiency: they reach the lowest normalized energy consumption, though their accuracy is lower than the ANN and surrogate-trained model.

Normalized energy consumption makes the energy comparison easier to read because it expresses each model's energy relative to a common reference instead of forcing the learner to compare raw units across settings. If the ANN is treated as the high-energy baseline, then a normalized SNN value far below the ANN value means the SNN spends only a fraction of the energy per inference. This kind of comparison answers a practical deployment question: if two models are close enough in accuracy for the application, which one can run longer on the same battery or produce less heat on edge hardware?

A concrete interpretation helps. Suppose the ANN gives the best accuracy on CIFAR-10, around 92%, while a direct SNN method drops closer to 85% as the dataset becomes harder. That gap matters for applications where every percentage point of accuracy affects safety or reliability. Yet the energy side moves in the other direction: surrogate-trained SNNs can operate at less than 10% of ANN energy cost, and STDP-based SNNs can go even lower in energy. The decision becomes application-shaped. A cloud server classifying difficult images may prefer the ANN's extra accuracy. A wearable sensor, mobile robot, or always-on camera may prefer the SNN that is slightly less accurate but dramatically cheaper to run.

The tallest energy behavior belongs to the ANN, at roughly 200 mJ per inference. That high value fits the dense-computation picture: continuous-valued operations keep many units active. Converted SNNs reduce energy sharply to about 20 mJ, which is already about a tenfold reduction, yet they still generate a relatively high spike count of about 20,000 spikes. That spike count tells us why conversion is useful but limited: rate-coded spike propagation may need many events to represent what the ANN represented with continuous activations. Surrogate Gradient SNNs improve the balance, using about 15 mJ and about 12,000 spikes. The lower spike count means the model has learned a more efficient spiking pattern rather than simply imitating dense ANN activity through many events. STDP-based SNNs sit at the lowest energy point, around 5 mJ per inference, with about 4,000 spikes. That sparse activity is exactly the behavior desired when power is scarce.

**Question.** If the ANN has the highest accuracy, why would anyone choose an SNN?

**Answer.** An SNN can be preferable when the accuracy drop is small compared with the energy saved. A surrogate-trained SNN can approach ANN accuracy within about 1-2% while using far less energy per inference. For an edge device that runs continuously, saving energy can extend battery life and make real-time operation more feasible, so the best model is the one that satisfies the accuracy requirement at the lowest practical cost.

The accuracy-energy tradeoff means that improving one metric can come with pressure on another. Converted SNNs preserve more ANN-like behavior, but that preservation can require more spikes. STDP-based SNNs minimize spikes and energy, but their accuracy is lower. Surrogate Gradient SNNs occupy the middle of the tradeoff curve: they are close enough to ANN accuracy for many uses and much more energy-efficient than the ANN. This makes them a strong default candidate when both prediction quality and low power matter.

A common beginner confusion is to treat low energy as automatic once a network uses spikes. Spikes create the possibility of lower energy because computation happens through discrete events, but the actual energy depends on how many spikes are produced and how long the network must simulate before deciding. A converted SNN with many rate-coded spikes can consume more energy than a carefully trained spiking model. The mechanism, training method, and spike count all shape the final cost.

**Question.** Does normalized energy efficiency mean the most energy-efficient model is always the best model?

**Answer.** Normalized energy efficiency tells how cheaply a model runs relative to the comparison baseline, but model choice also depends on the required accuracy. STDP-based SNNs have the strongest energy result, around 5 mJ per inference in the reported comparison, while surrogate-trained SNNs offer a stronger accuracy-energy balance. The best choice changes when the task values maximum accuracy, low power, fast response, or unsupervised adaptation differently.

The mental model is a set of connected knobs rather than a single leaderboard. Accuracy tells how often the answer is correct. Energy tells how costly each answer is. Spike count helps explain that cost because fewer events usually mean less computation. Training method determines where the model lands on the tradeoff: ANN conversion keeps accuracy competitive but can carry spike overhead, surrogate learning finds a balanced spiking solution, and STDP favors sparse low-power behavior. Reading the results together shows why SNN evaluation must combine accuracy with energy: the strongest model is the one whose accuracy is high enough for the task while its energy use fits the device that must run it.
