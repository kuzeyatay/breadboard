### Accuracy and Energy Results

Imagine choosing a model for a small device that must recognize images while running on a limited battery. If the model is used only once, accuracy may seem like the whole story: the model that labels the most images correctly looks best. But an edge device does not make one prediction in isolation. It may classify camera frames again and again, and every inference consumes energy. A model that is slightly more accurate but burns much more energy can drain the device quickly, while a model that gives up a small amount of accuracy may run far longer. Benchmark results for spiking neural networks become meaningful only when accuracy and energy are read together.

Accuracy tells us the fraction of test examples a model classifies correctly. If a model reaches 98% accuracy on MNIST, it correctly labels about 98 out of every 100 handwritten digit images in the test set. Energy consumption tells us how much computational work the model spends to make each prediction, often reported as millijoules per inference. A lower energy number means each prediction is cheaper to run. Normalized energy consumption makes this comparison easier by expressing energy relative to a reference model, usually the conventional artificial neural network. If the ANN is treated as the 1x baseline, then a spiking model using 0.1x normalized energy uses one tenth as much energy per inference.

The reason spiking models can reduce energy begins with how they represent activity. A conventional ANN passes continuous numerical activations through layers whether the signal is large, small, changing, or nearly unchanged. A spiking neural network communicates through discrete spike events. When fewer spikes are produced, fewer event-driven computations are needed. In a practical result table, spike-based efficiency shows up indirectly as lower energy consumption. The model is spending less because it is doing less work at times when there is little useful signal to send.

The rows separate model types, while the accuracy column reports predictive correctness and the normalized energy column reports cost relative to the ANN-style baseline. Reading across a row gives the character of one model; reading down a column compares models on one metric. The useful interpretation comes from doing both at once.

The table's structure teaches a careful habit: an accuracy number earns its meaning beside its energy number. An ANN-style model can sit near the top on raw accuracy, especially on familiar image benchmarks, but it also represents the high-energy reference point. Surrogate-gradient SNNs keep accuracy close to the ANN range while cutting energy substantially. STDP-based SNNs move even further toward low energy, reaching the smallest consumption, while accepting a modest loss in accuracy. Converted SNNs occupy another middle position: they inherit useful accuracy from an ANN-like starting point, but their rate-coded spike propagation can produce many spikes, so their energy savings are smaller than the sparsest spiking approaches.

MNIST is the easiest place to see why accuracy alone can mislead. MNIST consists of simple handwritten digit images, so many models can score highly. If two models both perform well on MNIST, a tiny accuracy gap may matter less than a large energy gap. Suppose one model classifies 98 out of 100 digits correctly but uses the full energy baseline, while another classifies around 96 or 97 out of 100 and uses only a small fraction of the energy. The second model may be the better engineering choice for a battery-powered sensor, because the lost predictions are few while the energy savings repeat on every inference. The MNIST comparison only matters beside its cost because the benchmark is easy enough for several models to look strong on correctness.

CIFAR-10 makes the comparison sharper. CIFAR-10 images are more complex than MNIST digits, so accuracy gaps widen as the task becomes harder. Conventional ANNs remain strong on CIFAR-10, with reported performance around 92%, while direct SNN approaches can drop to around 85%. That wider gap changes the decision. On a harder benchmark, the cost of choosing a lower-energy spiking model includes more missed classifications. At the same time, the energy savings can become even more visible, with surrogate-trained SNNs operating at less than 10% of ANN energy costs. The result is a tradeoff rather than a single winner: the best model depends on how much accuracy the application needs and how tightly energy is constrained.

The graph below makes that tradeoff visible by putting MNIST, CIFAR-10, and energy consumption into the same comparison. The accuracy values invite one kind of reading: which model predicts best on each benchmark? The energy bars invite another: which model spends the least per inference? The shape of the comparison pushes the learner to hold both questions together. A tall accuracy bar paired with high energy means strong prediction at high cost. A lower energy bar paired with slightly reduced accuracy means a model is exchanging some correctness for efficiency.

The graph also helps separate two kinds of benchmark thinking. On MNIST, close accuracy values can hide large differences in computational cost. On CIFAR-10, the accuracy separation becomes more visible, so the learner must ask whether the energy savings compensate for the performance drop. The energy portion then completes the picture: SNNs can reduce energy by a large margin, especially when their spike activity is sparse. In reported comparisons, ANNs consume about 200 mJ per inference, converted SNNs about 20 mJ, surrogate-gradient SNNs about 15 mJ, and STDP-based SNNs about 5 mJ. Those numbers mean that the lowest-energy spiking approach can spend only a small fraction of the ANN's energy for each prediction.

Spike count gives a concrete reason for these energy differences. A converted SNN may still generate many spikes, around 20,000 per inference, because rate coding represents values through repeated spiking activity. A surrogate-gradient SNN can reduce that activity, around 12,000 spikes, while maintaining strong accuracy. An STDP-based SNN can be even sparser, around 4,000 spikes, and this sparse activity aligns with its very low energy consumption. A useful analogy is sending messages over a network: if one system sends long streams of updates and another sends only the events that matter, the second system can save bandwidth and power. In SNNs, fewer spikes often mean fewer operations, and fewer operations usually mean lower energy.

**Question.** If an ANN has the highest accuracy, should it always be chosen?

**Answer.** The choice depends on the job. If every percentage point of accuracy is essential and energy is plentiful, the ANN may be appropriate. If the model must run repeatedly on a low-power device, a spiking model with slightly lower accuracy and much lower energy can be the better fit. The result should be read as accuracy per practical cost, not accuracy alone.

A common beginner confusion is to treat normalized energy efficiency as another accuracy score, where "higher model quality" simply means a larger number. Energy works in the opposite direction when it is reported as consumption: lower consumption is better because each inference costs less. If energy is normalized to an ANN baseline, the baseline represents the reference cost, and smaller fractions mean larger savings. A model using 0.1x the baseline energy is not performing at 10% quality; it is spending 10% of the energy.

**Question.** Why does the MNIST result need an energy reading if the accuracy already looks high?

**Answer.** MNIST is a relatively simple benchmark, so several models can achieve strong accuracy. When correctness numbers are close, energy separates models that look similar at first glance. A small drop in MNIST accuracy may be acceptable if it comes with a large reduction in per-inference energy, especially for devices that make many predictions over time.

A strong reading of these results follows a simple mental model. Accuracy answers, "How often is the prediction right?" Energy answers, "How expensive is each prediction?" Benchmark difficulty changes how severe the accuracy tradeoff becomes: MNIST keeps many models close, while CIFAR-10 exposes larger gaps. Spiking activity explains why energy can fall: fewer spikes usually mean fewer event-driven operations. The practical decision comes from placing these pieces on the same scale of judgment, so a model is evaluated as a complete operating system for inference rather than as a single accuracy number.
