### Latency Results Across Models

After seeing that spiking models can trade accuracy, energy use, spike count, and convergence behavior in different ways, latency asks a more immediate question: how long does the model need before it can make a decision? Imagine a small camera-based sensor at the edge of a road watching for a hand gesture, a moving vehicle, or a sudden obstacle. The useful answer is the one that arrives while the event is still actionable. A model that eventually reaches the right class after a long internal simulation may still feel too slow for a low-latency decision system.

Latency measures inference time: the time between presenting an input and receiving the model's output. In a conventional ANN or CNN, information usually flows through layers as continuous numerical activations. Each layer computes values, passes them forward, and the final layer produces a class score. In a spiking neural network, information arrives as spikes over time. The model may need to observe a short stream of spike events before the output neuron's activity becomes confident enough to count as a decision. That time window is where the latency tradeoff appears.

The latency comparison places four model categories beside one another: an ANN/CNN baseline, a converted SNN, a surrogate-gradient SNN, and an STDP-based SNN. The baseline ANN/CNN typically sits in the 20-50 millisecond range depending on model and hardware. The converted SNN is reported around 20 milliseconds. The surrogate-gradient SNN reaches about 10 milliseconds. The STDP-based SNN stabilizes under optimized conditions below about 15 milliseconds. Those numbers make the ordering clear: surrogate-gradient SNNs are the strongest fit among these choices when the goal is a fast decision while keeping accuracy competitive.

The table is useful because it turns "fast" into a direct comparison. The ANN/CNN row represents the familiar continuous-activation approach, with latency in the tens of milliseconds. The converted SNN row shows that converting an ANN into a spiking form can reduce or control latency, but the decision still depends on collecting enough spikes across a simulation window. The surrogate-gradient row is the lowest-latency result at about 10 milliseconds, which means the network has learned spike patterns that become informative quickly. The STDP-based row sits close behind, with sub-15 millisecond behavior under optimized conditions, but its learning rule adapts through spike timing in a way that can make early inference slightly slower.

A simulation window is the span of time over which a spiking model is allowed to accumulate spikes before deciding. A short window asks the model to decide from fewer events. A longer window gives the model more evidence, but it also increases latency. Converted SNNs are especially exposed to this penalty because they often begin as ANNs whose continuous activations are approximated by spike rates. A high activation becomes many spikes over time; a low activation becomes fewer spikes. To estimate those rates reliably, the converted model may need to wait long enough for the spike count to represent the original activation. That waiting time is the simulation-window penalty.

A simple analogy is measuring rainfall with a bucket. If rain is heavy, the bucket fills quickly and the measurement becomes obvious. If rain is light, a very short glance at the bucket may give a noisy estimate. A converted SNN using rate-coded spikes faces a similar issue: it may need enough time to count spikes before the original ANN-like signal is represented clearly. Surrogate-gradient SNNs are trained directly in the spiking setting, so they can learn to make earlier spikes carry more useful decision information. Instead of treating spikes mainly as a slow estimate of a continuous value, the timing and pattern of spikes become part of the learned computation.

**Question.** If converted SNNs come from trained ANNs, why are they not automatically the fastest spiking option?

**Answer.** A converted SNN inherits useful structure from an ANN, but it often represents the ANN's continuous activations through spike rates. Rate information becomes clearer after observing spikes for some amount of time. If the model needs a longer simulation window to estimate those rates, the inference time increases. The converted SNN can still be competitive, especially for accuracy, but its latency can rise because the model is waiting for enough spike evidence.

Latency comparisons also need to be read beside their cost. A model can be made faster by cutting the observation window too aggressively, but then the decision may become less reliable. A model can be made more reliable by waiting longer, but then it loses the advantage of quick response. The useful result is the balance: surrogate-gradient SNNs reach about 10 milliseconds while remaining tied to competitive accuracy, which makes them attractive for low-latency inference. STDP-based SNNs are attractive when very low energy and sparse spiking dominate the design goal, while converted SNNs are attractive when preserving ANN-like performance is valuable and the application can tolerate a longer decision window.

**Question.** Does the lowest latency always mean the best model?

**Answer.** The lowest latency identifies the fastest decision path, but model choice also depends on accuracy, energy use, spike count, and training behavior. For low-latency decisions in these results, the surrogate-gradient SNN is the best fit because it combines the shortest reported inference time with competitive performance. For an ultra-low-power setting, an STDP-based model may still be preferred, even if its latency is slightly higher.

The mental model is a race between evidence and decision. An ANN/CNN processes dense numerical values through layers. A converted SNN tries to reproduce those values through spike rates, so it may wait for a long enough spike count. An STDP-based SNN adapts through local spike timing and can run with sparse activity, though its early responses may take longer to settle. A surrogate-gradient SNN is trained directly to use spikes for the task, so useful evidence appears quickly in the spike pattern. When the design target is low-latency inference, that direct training advantage is what makes the surrogate-gradient SNN the strongest choice in the latency results.
