## Energy and Spike Count Results

Imagine a small device making repeated decisions from sensor input while running on a limited battery. Each decision is one inference: the model receives an input, processes it, and produces an output. If the model performs this work by constantly updating many numerical values, then even a simple decision can require many operations. If the model performs the work only when spikes occur, then quiet moments cost very little. Energy use begins to follow activity: fewer spike events usually mean fewer synaptic operations, and fewer synaptic operations usually mean less energy spent per inference.

A spike count is the number of spikes generated while the model processes one input. In a spiking neural network, a spike is a discrete event sent from one neuron to another. When a spike arrives at a synapse, the receiving neuron updates its internal state. That update is a synaptic operation. The connection between spike count and energy comes from this chain: spikes trigger synaptic operations, synaptic operations require computation, and computation consumes energy. A model that reaches a useful answer with fewer spikes has a direct path to lower-power inference because it asks the hardware to do less event-driven work.

This is why energy results should be read beside spike counts rather than alone. Energy consumption tells us how much cost one inference has, often measured in millijoules. Spike count tells us how much spiking activity produced that inference. When both numbers fall together, the result is easier to interpret: the model is saving energy because its computation is sparser. When energy falls but spike count remains high, the model may still be more efficient than a dense artificial neural network, but it is carrying more event traffic through time.

The comparison across model types shows this activity-energy coupling clearly.

![Comparison of energy consumption and spike count per inference across models](/test-2/assets/source-visuals/2510-27379v1-page-9-graph-g1-comparison-of-energy-consumption-and-spike-count.png)

*Comparison of energy consumption and spike count per inference across models* *(p. 9)*

The graph places four models side by side: an ANN/CNN, a converted SNN, a surrogate gradient SNN, and an STDP-based SNN. The ANN/CNN has the largest energy cost, about 200 mJ per inference. That high value fits its style of computation: continuous-valued activations are propagated through the network, so the model performs dense numerical work even though no spike events are being counted in the same way as in an SNN. The converted SNN drops energy sharply to about 20 mJ per inference, which is roughly one tenth of the ANN/CNN value. Its spike count remains high, around 20,000 spikes per inference, because conversion often represents information through firing rates over a simulation window. In plain terms, the converted model saves energy by moving into spike-based computation, but it still needs many spike events to approximate the behavior of the original network.

The surrogate gradient SNN improves the balance further. Its energy is about 15 mJ per inference, and its spike count is about 12,000 spikes. Surrogate training adjusts spiking patterns directly, so the model can learn a more efficient use of events than a converted model that inherits much of its structure from a non-spiking network. The graph's downward movement from converted SNN to surrogate gradient SNN shows a practical gain: fewer spikes are doing the job, and the energy cost falls with them.

The STDP-based SNN sits at the lowest end of both measurements: about 5 mJ per inference and around 4,000 spikes. Spike-Timing Dependent Plasticity, or STDP, is a learning rule that changes synaptic strength based on the timing relationship between spikes. When the learning process favors sparse, timing-based activity, the trained model can operate with fewer spike events. In the graph, this appears as the smallest spike count and the smallest energy bar. A useful way to read that result is to imagine every spike as a tiny message that may cause downstream work. The STDP-based model sends far fewer messages, so the whole inference can be much cheaper to run.

**Question.** If the STDP-based SNN uses the least energy, does that automatically make it the best model?

**Answer.** It makes it the strongest choice when low-power inference is the main goal, but model choice also depends on accuracy, latency, and convergence behavior. The same results that show about 5 mJ per inference and about 4,000 spikes also indicate a tradeoff: STDP-based SNNs can have lower accuracy and slower convergence than other options. A battery-limited or always-on system may value the energy savings more than the lost accuracy, while a task that demands maximum accuracy may prefer another model.

The table is useful because it keeps the cost beside the label "energy efficiency." A label such as "high efficiency" only becomes meaningful when it is tied to reported energy values and model behavior. The ANN/CNN row represents the high-energy baseline, with about 200 mJ per inference. The converted SNN row shows a major drop to about 20 mJ, so it earns a stronger efficiency interpretation while still carrying a relatively large spike count. The surrogate gradient SNN row improves the balance again, with about 15 mJ per inference and fewer spikes than the converted SNN. The STDP-based SNN row gives the clearest low-power result, with about 5 mJ per inference and the sparsest activity.

This table also prevents a common mistake: treating "efficient" as one universal ranking that settles every deployment decision. Efficiency is a relationship between what a model spends and what it delivers. The STDP-based SNN spends the least energy per inference, so it becomes attractive in ultra-low-power settings. The surrogate gradient SNN spends more energy than the STDP-based model but can be a better balance when stronger accuracy and fast convergence matter. The converted SNN spends more energy and uses more spikes than the other SNNs shown, but it can still be attractive when an existing ANN is being converted into a spiking form. Each method optimizes a different deployment priority.

A beginner might wonder why training speed enters a discussion about energy per inference. Training and inference are different phases. Training is the process of adjusting the model so it learns useful behavior. Inference is the process of using the trained model to make one decision. A model that converges slowly during training can still be valuable if it runs very cheaply after training. STDP-based SNNs fit that pattern in these results: they may not be the fastest or most accurate option, but their low spike count makes each inference inexpensive. If a model will be trained once and then used many times on an energy-constrained device, the repeated inference cost can dominate the practical decision.

**Question.** Why does a lower spike count usually reduce energy in an SNN?

**Answer.** A spike causes communication and synaptic updates. When fewer spikes occur during one inference, fewer synaptic operations are needed. Since synaptic operations are the event-driven computations that consume energy, sparse spiking activity usually lowers energy consumption. The graph supports this pattern: the STDP-based SNN has the fewest spikes, around 4,000 per inference, and also the lowest energy use, about 5 mJ.

The mental model is a budget of events. An ANN/CNN spends heavily because dense continuous computation keeps many values active. A converted SNN spends much less because it uses spikes, but it may still send many events. A surrogate gradient SNN learns to use fewer events more effectively. An STDP-based SNN pushes activity down further, making it especially appealing when the system's main constraint is power. The model with the fewest spikes can be attractive because those missing spikes are missing operations, and missing operations become saved energy every time the model performs an inference.
