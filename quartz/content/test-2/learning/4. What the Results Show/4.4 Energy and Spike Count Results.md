## Energy and Spike Count Results

Imagine a small camera on a battery-powered sensor watching a hallway. Most of the time, the scene barely changes: the wall stays still, the floor stays still, and only an occasional person passes through. A conventional artificial neural network still pushes dense numerical activations through many layers for each inference, even when the useful signal is small. A spiking neural network gives the computation a different shape: activity appears as discrete spike events, and quiet parts of the network can remain quiet. The cost of inference then depends strongly on how many spikes the model produces, because each spike can trigger synaptic operations downstream.

Energy per inference means the amount of energy used to process one input and produce one output. In the reported comparison, energy is measured in millijoules, written as mJ. One millijoule is one-thousandth of a joule, so a smaller mJ value means less energy is spent for a single decision. Spike count means the number of spike events generated during that inference. If a model produces many spikes, it sends many event messages through the network. If it produces fewer spikes while still preserving the useful information, it can often reduce the amount of work the hardware must perform.

That link between spikes and cost gives the main mental handle for reading the result: a spike is both a signal and a small request for computation. When a neuron fires, connected neurons may need to update their internal state. In a sparse-event system, silence saves work because inactive neurons do not keep broadcasting changing values. Lower spike count can therefore support lower energy per inference when spike transmission and synaptic-operation costs dominate the comparison.

The table organizes energy consumption by model type. The artificial neural network, represented by an ANN/CNN baseline, sits at the high-energy end with about 200 mJ per inference. That high value fits the way dense networks compute: each layer transforms continuous-valued activations, so the model carries out many numerical operations regardless of whether the input contains a small event or a broad pattern. The converted SNN lowers energy substantially to about 20 mJ per inference, showing that replacing continuous activation flow with spikes can reduce cost. Surrogate gradient SNNs lower the reported energy further to about 15 mJ, and STDP-based SNNs reach the lowest reported value, about 5 mJ per inference.

The numbers are easier to interpret as ratios. Moving from 200 mJ for the ANN/CNN baseline to 20 mJ for the converted SNN divides the energy by 10. That means the converted SNN uses about one-tenth as much energy per inference as the ANN/CNN baseline in this comparison. Moving from 200 mJ to 5 mJ divides the energy by 40. The STDP-based SNN therefore uses about one-fortieth of the ANN/CNN energy per inference, which corresponds to a very large reduction in operating cost for a device that must make repeated decisions.

A beginner might expect every spiking model to have automatically tiny spike counts, but the training strategy shapes how many spikes are needed to represent the signal. ANN-to-SNN conversion often begins with a trained dense network and translates its activity into spike rates. A rate-coded signal represents a larger activation by producing more spikes over a simulation window. That can preserve useful behavior from the original network, but it can also create a spike-count burden: the network spends many events expressing values that were originally continuous.

The graph makes this burden visible by pairing energy consumption with spike count across the same model types.

The converted SNN appears with much lower energy than the ANN/CNN baseline, about 20 mJ rather than about 200 mJ, while still producing a relatively high spike count of roughly 20,000 spikes per inference. That combination teaches an important cost structure: converting to spikes can reduce energy, yet the resulting spike traffic may remain heavy if the model relies on rate-coded propagation. The model spends less energy than the dense baseline, but its event stream is still crowded.

Surrogate gradient SNNs sit in a middle region of the comparison. Their reported energy is about 15 mJ per inference, and their spike count is about 12,000 spikes. Surrogate gradient training adjusts spiking patterns directly using a gradient-based method adapted for spike events. In practical terms, the model learns when spikes are useful for the task instead of only inheriting a dense activation pattern and expressing it through spike rates. The result shown here is a lower spike count than the converted SNN and a modest additional reduction in energy.

STDP-based SNNs occupy the sparsest and lowest-energy corner of the result, with about 5 mJ per inference and about 4,000 spikes. Spike-Timing Dependent Plasticity, or STDP, changes connections using the relative timing of spikes. When presynaptic and postsynaptic activity occur in close temporal relation, the connection can be adjusted. This kind of learning naturally emphasizes local timing relationships and can lead to sparse activity. In the reported result, that sparsity appears directly as fewer spikes and indirectly as lower energy per inference.

A simple way to feel the difference is to imagine three people describing the same quiet hallway video. The converted SNN is like someone giving many short updates to approximate a continuous description: "slightly bright here, slightly dark there, still similar, still similar." The surrogate-trained SNN is more selective, sending fewer updates after learning which events matter for the decision. The STDP-based SNN is the most restrained in this comparison, sending a small number of timing-based signals when the activity pattern is meaningful.

**Question.** If the converted SNN uses much less energy than the ANN/CNN baseline, why should its spike count still concern us?

**Answer.** Its spike count matters because each spike can trigger communication and synaptic updates. The converted SNN reduces energy from about 200 mJ to about 20 mJ per inference, so it is much cheaper than the ANN/CNN baseline. Its roughly 20,000 spikes per inference still create a heavy event load, which can limit how far the energy savings go on hardware where spike routing and synaptic operations are major costs.

The phrase low-power inference means making each decision with very little energy. The results show two routes toward that goal. One route is to move away from continuous-valued dense computation, which explains the large drop from the ANN/CNN baseline to spiking models. Another route is to reduce unnecessary spike traffic inside the spiking model itself, which explains why surrogate gradient and STDP-based SNNs improve on the converted SNN in the reported spike and energy values.

Energy efficiency should be read together with the training strategy. ANN/CNN models give strong accuracy but carry high energy use in this comparison. Converted SNNs inherit useful behavior from dense models and greatly reduce energy, while paying a spike-count cost from rate-coded activity. Surrogate gradient SNNs learn spiking behavior more directly, producing fewer spikes and lower energy than the converted model. STDP-based SNNs show the strongest sparse-event advantage, reaching the lowest reported energy and spike count, while accepting a performance tradeoff described elsewhere in the results.

**Question.** Does the lowest spike count automatically mean the best model choice?

**Answer.** The lowest spike count means the model is attractive when energy and sparse activity are the main priorities. In this comparison, the STDP-based SNN has about 4,000 spikes and about 5 mJ per inference, so it is the strongest low-power option. Model choice still depends on the goal of the system: a battery-limited unsupervised setting may favor that sparse STDP behavior, while a task that demands closer accuracy to a dense model may favor a surrogate-trained SNN despite its higher spike count.

The result becomes clear when energy and spikes are read as one chain. Dense continuous computation spends energy broadly. Spiking computation spends energy through events. A converted SNN already benefits from changing the signal format, but rate-coded activity can leave many spikes in the system. A surrogate-trained SNN learns a more economical event pattern. An STDP-based SNN produces the sparsest activity in the comparison, and that sparse-event behavior supports the lowest energy per inference. The practical mental model is that every spike is useful only when it carries enough information to justify the work it causes; the most energy-efficient SNNs are the ones that make silence carry part of the computation.
