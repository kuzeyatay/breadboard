### Accuracy and Energy Results Across Models

Imagine choosing a neural network for a small device that must recognize images all day from a limited battery. Accuracy tells you how often the model gives the right answer, so it feels natural to begin there. If two models see the same handwritten digit or object image, the model with higher accuracy makes fewer mistakes. A deployment choice, however, also depends on how much energy each inference consumes. An inference is one complete pass from input to output: the device receives an image, the model processes it, and the model produces a prediction. A model that is slightly more accurate but uses far more energy may drain the device quickly, heat the hardware, or require a larger power supply. A model that is slightly less accurate but much cheaper to run may become the better fit for an always-on sensor or edge device.

That is why accuracy and energy need to be read together. An artificial neural network, or ANN, processes continuous-valued activations through dense operations. In the comparison here, the ANN reaches the strongest raw accuracy, but it also consumes about 200 mJ per inference. A spiking neural network, or SNN, communicates through discrete spike events. Silence carries practical value because no spike means no event needs to be processed at that moment. This event-driven style allows SNNs to reduce computation when activity is sparse. The result is a performance-energy tradeoff: each model sits at a different point on a map whose axes are correctness and cost.

The model rows separate the ANN CNN, the converted SNN, the surrogate-gradient SNN, and the STDP-based SNN. The MNIST accuracy column reports performance on a simpler handwritten-digit recognition task. The CIFAR-10 accuracy column reports performance on a more varied natural-image classification task. Reading both columns prevents a model from being judged by a single dataset. A method can look very strong on MNIST because the input structure is comparatively simple, then show a larger gap on CIFAR-10 because the images contain richer variation in color, texture, and object shape. Dataset-specific accuracy comparison means asking, "Which task is this model being asked to solve?" before turning a number into a general claim about model quality.

The normalized energy consumption column gives the cost side of the comparison on a common scale. Normalization means the energy values are expressed relative to a reference so that the reader can compare models by proportion rather than raw units alone. If the ANN is treated as the high-energy baseline, then a converted SNN at about 20 mJ per inference uses roughly one tenth of the ANN's 200 mJ. A surrogate-gradient SNN at about 15 mJ uses even less, and an STDP-based SNN at about 5 mJ is lower still. The practical interpretation is simple: a lower normalized energy value means each prediction costs less energy relative to the baseline. That number does not replace accuracy; it sits beside accuracy so the deployment choice can account for both.

A converted SNN begins with a trained ANN and translates its activity into spikes. This helps preserve much of the original model's performance, but rate-coded spike propagation can generate many spikes. In the reported comparison, the converted SNN lowers energy from about 200 mJ to about 20 mJ per inference, yet still produces roughly 20,000 spikes. A useful analogy is replacing a steady stream of water with timed droplets, but still using many droplets to approximate the original flow. The cost drops sharply compared with the dense ANN, but the spike count remains relatively high because the model uses many events to represent information.

A surrogate-gradient SNN is trained more directly to work with spiking activity. The challenge is that spikes are discrete events, which makes ordinary gradient-based learning difficult at the exact spike point. Surrogate training uses a smoother learning signal during training so the model can adjust its spiking patterns effectively. In the comparison, this model reaches about 97.8% accuracy, consumes about 15 mJ per inference, and produces about 12,000 spikes. That combination places it near the middle of the tradeoff map: close to ANN-level accuracy, lower energy than the converted SNN, and fewer spikes than the converted SNN. For a system that needs strong recognition performance with large energy savings, this is a balanced point.

An STDP-based SNN uses spike-timing dependent plasticity, where learning depends on the timing relationship between spikes. Its activity is especially sparse in the reported results, with about 4,000 spikes and about 5 mJ per inference. Its accuracy is lower, about 95.5%, and its convergence is slower than the surrogate-gradient model. That makes it attractive when energy is the tightest constraint and a small drop in accuracy is acceptable. Think of a tiny battery-powered sensor that only needs to make reliable enough decisions over long periods: spending less energy on every inference can be more valuable than squeezing out the last few percentage points of accuracy.

*Performance analysis and energy consumption of SNN models compared with ANN* *(p. 7)*

The tall energy value for the ANN shows the cost of continuous dense computation. Moving from ANN to converted SNN, then to surrogate-gradient SNN, then to STDP-based SNN, the energy values fall sharply: about 200 mJ, 20 mJ, 15 mJ, and 5 mJ per inference. The spike counts follow the same general direction among the SNN models, from about 20,000 spikes for the converted SNN to about 12,000 for the surrogate-gradient SNN and about 4,000 for the STDP-based SNN. The shape of this comparison teaches a useful reading habit: lower spike activity tends to support lower energy use, because fewer events need to be processed.

**Question.** If the ANN has the highest raw accuracy, should it automatically be chosen?

**Answer.** The ANN is the natural choice when maximum accuracy is the only priority and energy cost is acceptable. For energy-limited settings, the SNN models become strong candidates because they reduce energy dramatically. The surrogate-gradient SNN is especially useful when the goal is to stay close to ANN accuracy with much lower energy, and the STDP-based SNN is useful when minimal energy use is the dominant requirement.

A common beginner confusion is to treat the table as a leaderboard where the best model must occupy the top position in every column. The better mental picture is a set of application profiles. A high-accuracy application with moderate energy limits points toward the surrogate-gradient SNN. A task that can tolerate slightly lower accuracy in exchange for extreme efficiency points toward the STDP-based SNN. A system that already has an ANN and wants a spiking version with competitive performance may point toward conversion, accepting the higher spike count that comes with rate-coded propagation.

**Question.** Why compare MNIST and CIFAR-10 instead of using one accuracy number?

**Answer.** A single dataset can hide how a model behaves under different levels of visual difficulty. MNIST gives a cleaner digit-recognition setting, and CIFAR-10 gives a more complex image-recognition setting. Reading both accuracy columns shows whether a model's performance is stable across tasks or mainly strong in the easier setting.

The full result is a mental model for comparison rather than a single ranking. Accuracy measures correctness on a dataset. Energy consumption measures the cost of each prediction. Normalized energy places that cost on a shared scale. Spike count helps explain why energy changes across SNN models, because fewer spike events usually mean less event-driven computation. When these pieces are read together, model selection becomes a question of fit: choose the point on the performance-energy map that matches the device, dataset, and acceptable error level.
