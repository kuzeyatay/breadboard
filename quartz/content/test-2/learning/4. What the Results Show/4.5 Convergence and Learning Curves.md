## Convergence and Learning Curves

By this point, you already know that an SNN can be judged by more than its final accuracy. A model that eventually reaches a good score after many training passes behaves very differently from one that improves quickly and then stabilizes. Imagine training three spiking models on the same classification task and checking them after each epoch, where one epoch means one full pass through the training data. At the start, each model makes many mistakes because its internal parameters have not yet been shaped by experience. During training, the useful question becomes: how fast does each model turn repeated exposure into better predictions?

A training loss curve answers that question from the "error pressure" side. Loss is a number that measures how poorly the model's current outputs match the desired answers during training. A high loss means the model is still far from the target behavior; a lower loss means its predictions are becoming more compatible with the labels or learning objective. When loss is plotted against epochs, the shape of the curve shows the pace of improvement. A steep downward curve means the model is reducing its errors quickly. A flatter curve means training is producing smaller gains per epoch. A curve that stops changing much has reached a plateau, meaning extra epochs are no longer changing the model very much.

The epoch-level loss table makes this idea concrete because the curve is grounded in actual reported values rather than a vague impression of a line. Each row corresponds to an epoch, and each model column shows the loss measured at that point for the converted SNN, the surrogate-gradient SNN, and the STDP-based SNN. Reading down a column is like watching one model learn over time. Reading across a row compares the three models at the same training moment.

![Training loss across epochs for converted, surrogate gradient, and STDP-based SNN models](/test-2/assets/source-visuals/2510-27379v1-page-10-table-t1-training-loss-across-epochs-for-converted-surro.png)

*Training loss across epochs for converted, surrogate gradient, and STDP-based SNN models* *(p. 10)*

The table supports a careful way of thinking: a training loss curve is only meaningful when you connect its downward movement to the cost paid in epochs. If two models end with similar loss, the one that reaches that region earlier has converged faster. If one model still has noticeably higher loss after the same number of epochs, it is learning more slowly under this training setup. The surrogate-gradient SNN shows the strongest pattern of steady loss reduction across the 20-epoch window, so its training process is doing more useful error correction per epoch. The converted SNN improves more moderately. The STDP-based SNN improves more slowly and tends to settle at a higher loss, which means its learning rule is producing a weaker match to the supervised classification target in this comparison.

The graph of training loss turns the table into a shape your eyes can compare quickly. The vertical axis represents loss, and the horizontal axis represents epochs. Each model becomes a line. The most informative feature is the slope: how sharply the line drops as training proceeds.

![Convergence behavior of SNN models training loss across epochs](/test-2/assets/source-visuals/2510-27379v1-page-10-graph-g1-convergence-behavior-of-snn-models-training-loss.png)

*Convergence behavior of SNN models training loss across epochs* *(p. 10)*

The surrogate-gradient curve falls most rapidly and stabilizes by around the 20th epoch, which shows fast convergence. The converted SNN curve descends as well, but with a gentler drop, so it reaches improved behavior more gradually. The STDP-based curve descends slowly and flattens earlier at a higher loss level, so its training has less room-to-room correction after the early epochs. A useful mental picture is a ball rolling downhill: the surrogate-gradient model finds a steeper, more direct path toward lower error, the converted model moves down a milder slope, and the STDP-based model reaches a shallow area before getting as low as the others.

A training accuracy curve answers the same learning-speed question from the "correct prediction" side. Training accuracy measures the fraction of training examples classified correctly. As epochs increase, a learning model should usually show rising accuracy because its parameters are becoming better aligned with the task. When loss falls and accuracy rises at the same time, the two curves are telling a consistent story: the model is making fewer costly errors and producing more correct outputs.

![Learning curves showing training accuracy versus epochs for converted, surrogate-gradient, and STDP-based SNNs](/test-2/assets/source-visuals/2510-27379v1-page-11-graph-g1-learning-curves-showing-training-accuracy-versus.png)

*Learning curves showing training accuracy versus epochs for converted, surrogate-gradient, and STDP-based SNNs* *(p. 11)*

The accuracy curves should be read beside the loss curves rather than in isolation. The surrogate-gradient SNN rises quickly in accuracy while its loss drops quickly, so both views agree that it learns rapidly. The converted SNN also gains accuracy, but its slower loss decline matches a more gradual learning curve. The STDP-based SNN improves, yet its slower accuracy growth matches the earlier plateau seen in loss. This paired reading is stronger than looking at final accuracy alone, because convergence behavior is about the path through training, not just the endpoint.

A beginner might wonder why loss and accuracy are both needed if accuracy already says how often the model is correct. Accuracy counts correct decisions, while loss measures how strongly wrong or uncertain the model's outputs are during training. Two models can have similar accuracy at some epoch while one has lower loss, meaning its correct answers may be more stable or its mistakes may be less severe under the training objective. Loss is the training signal that guides improvement; accuracy is the task-level result that shows how often the current model succeeds.

**Question.** If the surrogate-gradient SNN has the fastest falling loss curve, does that automatically mean it is always the best SNN choice?

**Answer.** It means it converges fastest in this training comparison and reaches strong accuracy quickly. Model choice still depends on the goal being optimized. For a task where rapid supervised learning and high accuracy are central, the surrogate-gradient curve is favorable. For a task that values a different tradeoff, another method may be acceptable even with slower convergence.

**Question.** How can a learner tell from a curve that a model has mostly converged?

**Answer.** A model has mostly converged when its curve changes only slightly across later epochs. On a loss curve, this appears as a line that has stopped dropping much. On an accuracy curve, it appears as a line that has stopped rising much. The epoch where this flattening begins gives a practical estimate of convergence time.

The clean mental model is to treat training as a sequence of measured changes. Each epoch gives the model another chance to adjust. The loss curve shows how quickly error pressure is being reduced. The accuracy curve shows how quickly those adjustments become correct predictions. When loss drops steeply and accuracy rises steeply, learning is fast. When both curves flatten early before reaching the stronger region, learning has slowed or plateaued. Convergence is the moment when additional epochs bring only small changes, and comparing that moment across SNN methods reveals which learning rule turns training time into useful performance most efficiently.
