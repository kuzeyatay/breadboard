### Accuracy and Energy Tradeoffs

Once accuracy, normalized energy consumption, and training paradigm are on the table, model choice becomes a practical design problem. Imagine a small vision system mounted on a battery-powered device. It must classify what it sees, but every inference costs energy. A conventional artificial neural network can push accuracy very high by using continuous numerical activations at every layer, yet those continuous operations keep the hardware busy even when the final decision feels simple. A spiking neural network changes the cost structure: activity arrives as discrete spike events, so fewer spikes usually mean less switching activity and lower energy use. The tradeoff appears when the most accurate model and the most efficient model are different choices.

The comparison is easiest to read by keeping two questions together: how often does the model classify correctly, and how much energy does one classification consume? Accuracy alone answers only the correctness side. Normalized energy consumption answers the cost side by putting models onto a shared scale, so a lower value means the model spends less energy per inference relative to the comparison baseline. A training paradigm then explains why the model lands where it does: ANN training optimizes continuous activations directly, ANN-to-SNN conversion translates a trained ANN into spikes, surrogate-gradient training learns spiking behavior with a gradient-based workaround, and STDP adjusts synapses from spike timing patterns.

The table invites a row-by-row reading rather than a hunt for the largest accuracy number. The ANN row represents the strong accuracy reference point, especially because continuous-valued CNN-style computation is highly effective on image benchmarks. The converted SNN row usually stays close to that reference because it begins with a trained ANN and then approximates its activations using spike rates. That conversion preserves much of the learned decision structure, but representing a continuous activation as a spike rate often requires many spikes across a simulation window. The surrogate-gradient SNN row sits in the balanced region: it learns spiking patterns directly and can reach near-ANN accuracy, meaning its accuracy falls only slightly below the ANN reference, roughly within the 1-2% range reported for surrogate-trained models. The STDP-based SNN row shows the strongest energy advantage, with the lowest normalized energy consumption, while accepting a lower accuracy level than the ANN and surrogate-gradient alternatives.

Near-ANN accuracy is useful language because it names a practical threshold rather than an exact tie. If an ANN reaches the top accuracy in a benchmark and a surrogate-gradient SNN trails by only a small margin, the learner should read that as a small correctness cost paid for a large energy reduction. For example, a mobile classifier that loses about one or two correct predictions per hundred difficult cases may still be attractive if it cuts energy enough to run all day on a small battery. The decision depends on the application's tolerance for errors and its energy budget.

**Question.** Why can a model with slightly lower accuracy still be the better engineering choice?

**Answer.** A deployed model spends resources every time it runs. If two models classify almost equally well but one uses far less energy per inference, the lower-energy model can support longer battery life, less heat, and more frequent sensing. The small accuracy loss becomes acceptable when the application values efficiency strongly enough, such as always-on edge sensing or neuromorphic hardware deployment.

The energy graph turns the same idea into a visual tradeoff. A dense ANN consumes about 200 mJ per inference because continuous-valued operations remain active throughout the computation. A converted SNN drops that cost to about 20 mJ, already a major reduction, but it still produces a relatively large spike count of roughly 20,000 spikes per inference because rate-coded propagation uses many spikes to approximate ANN activations. A surrogate-gradient SNN lowers the energy further to about 15 mJ and reduces activity to roughly 12,000 spikes by learning spiking patterns more directly. An STDP-based SNN reaches the most energy-frugal point at about 5 mJ and around 4,000 spikes, showing how sparse spike activity can translate into very low energy use.

The shape of this comparison teaches the tradeoff more clearly than any single number. The ANN stands high on energy because it performs dense computation. The converted SNN lands much lower, showing that spiking execution can reduce cost even when it inherits an ANN's structure. The surrogate-gradient SNN improves the balance by keeping accuracy close to the ANN while using a small fraction of the energy. The STDP-based SNN occupies the lowest-energy corner: it spends the least energy and emits the fewest spikes, while giving up some accuracy and settling more gradually than methods trained with gradient-based optimization. That makes STDP especially attractive for ultra-low-power or adaptive settings, where energy and local learning are more valuable than squeezing out the highest benchmark accuracy.

A common beginner confusion comes from treating spikes as automatically efficient. Spikes create the opportunity for efficiency because silent neurons can avoid unnecessary activity, but the amount of efficiency depends on how many spikes the model uses to compute one answer. A converted SNN may use spikes while still producing many of them, because it often needs spike rates over time to mimic continuous ANN activations. An STDP-based SNN can be much sparser because its learning rule strengthens or weakens connections from timing relationships between spikes, encouraging a pattern where fewer events carry the decision.

**Question.** Why does the converted SNN save energy if it still has a high spike count compared with other SNNs?

**Answer.** The converted SNN replaces many continuous ANN operations with event-based spike activity, so it can consume much less energy than the ANN baseline. Its spike count remains high because rate-coded spikes must approximate the original ANN's activation values across time. It gains efficiency from spiking computation, while surrogate-gradient and STDP-based SNNs gain more efficiency by learning or adapting sparser spike patterns.

The result is a map of model suitability rather than a single champion. The ANN is the accuracy anchor when raw correctness is the main priority and energy is available. The converted SNN is a bridge: it keeps much of the ANN's performance while reducing energy by an order of magnitude, at the cost of many spikes and longer spike-based approximation. The surrogate-gradient SNN is the balanced choice, reaching near-ANN accuracy with much lower energy, making it a strong candidate when both correctness and deployment cost matter. The STDP-based SNN is the lowest-energy model, using sparse activity to reach about 5 mJ per inference, and its advantage grows in settings where power is scarce and adaptation is valuable.

A good mental model is to picture each training paradigm as choosing a different point on an accuracy-energy plane. Moving toward the ANN corner buys accuracy with dense computation. Moving toward the STDP corner buys energy savings with sparse spike timing. Surrogate-gradient training sits near the middle of the useful frontier, preserving much of the ANN's accuracy while capturing much of the SNN's energy benefit. Model selection becomes the act of placing the application on that plane: medical or safety-critical recognition may lean toward maximum accuracy, always-on edge sensing may lean toward minimum energy, and many neuromorphic deployments will choose the balanced region where near-ANN accuracy and low energy meet.
