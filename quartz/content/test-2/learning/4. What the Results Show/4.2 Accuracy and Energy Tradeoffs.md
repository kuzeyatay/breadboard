### Accuracy and Energy Tradeoffs

After seeing how different training methods shape a spiking neural network, the natural question becomes practical: if two models can both recognize digits or images, which one should we actually choose? Imagine a small camera on a battery-powered drone. It needs to classify what it sees, but every inference costs energy. A conventional neural network can push many numerical activations through many layers and often achieve excellent accuracy, yet that dense computation drains power quickly. A spiking model changes the cost structure because information moves through discrete spike events. When fewer spikes are produced, less activity has to be processed, and the energy per inference can drop sharply.

Accuracy tells us how often the model gives the correct answer. If a model reaches 99.2% accuracy on MNIST, then out of 10,000 handwritten digit images, it would classify about 9,920 correctly on average. Energy consumption tells us how much computational work the model spends to make one prediction. In this comparison, energy is reported in millijoules per inference, where one inference means one complete forward pass used to classify one input. Normalized energy consumption then expresses energy on a shared scale so that models can be compared directly, even when their internal computations differ. Reading these numbers together is essential because a model with the highest accuracy may also be the most expensive to run.

The table should be read across each row, rather than column by column in isolation. MNIST accuracy measures performance on handwritten digit recognition, a relatively simple visual task. CIFAR-10 accuracy measures performance on small natural images, which is harder because the images contain color, background variation, and more complex object structure. Normalized energy consumption gives the efficiency side of the same model's behavior. A strong model ranking depends on the goal: if the task demands maximum classification accuracy, the ANN/CNN result is strongest, with about 99.2% on MNIST and 92.3% on CIFAR-10. If the task demands low energy use, the STDP-based SNN becomes attractive because its energy per inference is far lower, even though its accuracy is lower. A surrogate gradient SNN sits between these extremes: it keeps accuracy close to the ANN while using much less energy.

A benchmark comparison works like comparing vehicles on speed and fuel use at the same time. A race car may be fastest, but a delivery robot running all day on a small battery needs a different kind of excellence. In the same way, an ANN/CNN can win the raw accuracy benchmark while losing badly on energy. A converted SNN improves energy use by replacing continuous activity with spike-based activity, but its rate-coded spikes can still be numerous. A surrogate gradient SNN learns spiking patterns more directly, so it can reduce both energy and spike count while preserving much of the accuracy. An STDP-based SNN uses a local timing-based learning rule, and its sparse activity gives it the lowest energy use among the compared models.

The graph makes this tradeoff visible by placing energy consumption beside spike activity across the model types.

The tall ANN energy bar, around 200 mJ per inference, shows the cost of continuous-valued computation. The converted SNN drops to about 20 mJ, which is already an order-of-magnitude reduction, but it still produces roughly 20,000 spikes per inference. That spike count matters because each spike is an event the hardware must route and process. The surrogate gradient SNN lowers energy further to about 15 mJ and reduces spike count to roughly 12,000, showing a better balance between learned accuracy and event efficiency. The STDP-based SNN reaches about 5 mJ and roughly 4,000 spikes, giving the sparsest and lowest-energy behavior in the comparison.

The shape of the graph teaches an important model-selection habit: accuracy and energy can move in different directions. The ANN/CNN has the strongest accuracy, but it also has the largest energy cost. The STDP-based SNN has the smallest energy cost, but it gives up more accuracy. The surrogate gradient SNN becomes the compromise model because it stays close to ANN accuracy while greatly reducing energy. Reported accuracy values come from multiple independent runs with different random seeds, so the comparison is meant to reflect typical behavior rather than a single lucky run.

**Question.** If the ANN/CNN has the best accuracy, why would anyone choose a spiking model instead?

**Answer.** A spiking model becomes useful when energy is part of the task requirement. On a server with abundant power, a small accuracy gain may be worth the cost. On a wearable sensor, drone, robot, or edge device, spending 200 mJ per inference can be too expensive. A surrogate gradient SNN can keep accuracy close to the ANN while using far less energy, and an STDP-based SNN can be chosen when ultra-low-power operation matters more than maximum accuracy.

To compare models fairly, think of each model as a point in a space with at least three coordinates: MNIST accuracy, CIFAR-10 accuracy, and normalized energy consumption. A model that performs well on MNIST but poorly on CIFAR-10 may be learning patterns that work for simple digits but struggle on richer images. A model with low energy but much lower accuracy may fit applications where rough classification is acceptable, but it may be unsuitable when mistakes are costly. A model with slightly lower accuracy and dramatically lower energy can be the best choice when predictions happen continuously over long periods.

Spike count helps explain why energy drops. A conventional network computes dense numerical activations layer after layer. A spiking network can remain silent when no event is needed. Each spike is like a brief message: "something crossed a threshold at this time." Fewer messages mean less communication and less computation. The STDP-based SNN produces the fewest spikes, so its energy is lowest. The converted SNN produces more spikes because rate coding often represents intensity through how frequently neurons fire, so many spikes may be needed to approximate the original ANN's activity. The surrogate gradient SNN learns with the spiking behavior in mind, which helps it avoid some of that extra event traffic.

**Question.** Does lower spike count always mean a better model?

**Answer.** Lower spike count usually supports lower energy use, but model quality still depends on accuracy for the target task. A model that sends very few spikes may save energy while missing important patterns in the input. The strongest choice is the model whose spike activity is sparse enough to save energy while still accurate enough for the application. That is why the table and graph belong together: one shows classification performance, and the other shows the cost of producing that performance.

The mental model is a three-way balance. Accuracy measures how reliably the model answers. Energy measures how much each answer costs. Spike count explains a major reason for that cost in spiking systems. ANN/CNN models occupy the high-accuracy, high-energy corner. STDP-based SNNs occupy the low-energy, lower-accuracy corner. Surrogate gradient SNNs occupy the practical middle ground, where much of the accuracy is preserved while energy falls sharply. A performance table for spiking neural networks only becomes meaningful when every row is read as a combined statement about what the model gets right, what it spends, and what kind of deployment it is suited for.
