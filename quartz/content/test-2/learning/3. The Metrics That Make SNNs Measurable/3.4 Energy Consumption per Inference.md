### Energy Consumption per Inference

Imagine the network has already learned to recognize a pattern, and now one new input arrives: one image, one sensor reading, or one short slice of activity. The network performs an **inference**, meaning it uses its learned connections to produce an output for that input. In earlier ideas, a spike was the event a neuron sends when its activity crosses a threshold, and a synapse was the connection through which one neuron influences another. Energy consumption per inference asks a practical question: how much energy does the network spend to process one input from start to finish?

A dense artificial network spends energy by repeatedly moving and combining many continuous numerical values. Even when most of the input contains little useful change, the network still carries out large arrays of operations. A spiking network uses a different accounting style. Silence carries meaning. If a neuron does not spike, many downstream computations are skipped. When a neuron does spike, the network must spend energy to communicate that event and update the synapses reached by that event. Energy therefore follows activity: more spikes usually mean more synaptic updates, and more synaptic updates mean more energy spent during inference.

To make that measurable, separate the cost into two parts. One part comes from producing and routing spike events. The other part comes from the synaptic operations triggered by those events. A useful energy accounting model is:
$$
E_{\text{total}} = E_{\text{spikes}} + E_{\text{synops}}
$$
Here, $E_{\text{total}}$ means the **total energy** consumed for one inference. $E_{\text{spikes}}$ means the energy associated with spike events themselves, such as generating or communicating discrete firing events. $E_{\text{synops}}$ means the energy associated with **synaptic operations**, the updates or accumulations that happen when spikes travel through synapses. This formula lets you compute energy consumption by treating inference as a collection of event costs rather than as one opaque number. When exact device-level constants are unavailable, the formula still gives the right measurement logic: count the events that create work, assign energy costs to those kinds of work, and add them.

A slightly more detailed version expresses the same idea as a count multiplied by a cost:
$$
E_{\text{total}} \approx N_{\text{spikes}}c_{\text{spike}} + N_{\text{synops}}c_{\text{synop}}
$$
In this expression, $N_{\text{spikes}}$ is the number of spikes produced during one inference, $c_{\text{spike}}$ is the average energy cost of one spike event, $N_{\text{synops}}$ is the number of synaptic operations caused by those spikes, and $c_{\text{synop}}$ is the average energy cost of one synaptic operation. The approximation sign matters because real energy depends on implementation details, but the structure is the central measurement idea. If two models have similar accuracy, the model that reaches its answer with fewer spikes and fewer synaptic operations can be cheaper to run.

A simple analogy is a room full of motion-sensitive lights. A conventional system keeps every light on and constantly updates the whole room. An event-driven system turns on a light only where motion is detected. The cost of using the room then depends on how many lights turn on and how often they trigger connected systems. In an SNN, spikes are like those motion events. Synapses are the connected systems that respond. Energy per inference measures how much work the whole event chain performs for one input.

**Question.** If an SNN produces fewer spikes, does it always consume less energy per inference?

**Answer.** Fewer spikes usually reduce energy because fewer events tend to trigger fewer synaptic operations. The full energy also depends on how many synapses each spike reaches and what each operation costs. A small number of spikes that fan out through many synapses can still cause substantial work, so spike count and synaptic operation count belong together in the energy estimate.

The comparison across models becomes clearer when energy per inference is read beside spike count per inference. The reported results compare four models: an ANN/CNN, a converted SNN, a surrogate gradient SNN, and an STDP-based SNN. The ANN/CNN sits at about **200 mJ per inference**, while the converted SNN drops to about **20 mJ**. The surrogate gradient SNN reaches about **15 mJ**, and the STDP-based SNN reaches about **5 mJ**. The spike counts move in the same general direction for the spiking models: the converted SNN produces about **20,000 spikes per inference**, the surrogate gradient SNN about **12,000 spikes**, and the STDP-based SNN about **4,000 spikes**.

The graph should be read as a paired story rather than two separate rankings. The converted SNN already spends far less energy than the ANN/CNN because it uses spike-based inference, but its spike count remains relatively high. That high activity fits the idea of rate-coded spike propagation: information is represented by many spikes over time, so the network still creates a large amount of event-driven work. The surrogate gradient SNN improves the balance. It uses learning to shape spiking patterns more directly, so it reaches lower energy and lower spike count together. The STDP-based SNN shows the sparsest activity, around 4,000 spikes, and the lowest energy, around 5 mJ per inference. That pairing is the cleanest example of the energy metric: sparse events lead to fewer triggered operations, and fewer triggered operations support lower inference cost.

A compact comparison table makes the same measurement logic visible:

| Model | Energy per inference | Spike count per inference | Interpretation |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| ANN/CNN | ~200 mJ | continuous-valued activity | High energy from dense continuous operations |
| Converted SNN | ~20 mJ | ~20,000 spikes | Lower energy, but still many spikes from rate-coded propagation |
| Surrogate gradient SNN | ~15 mJ | ~12,000 spikes | Lower activity through learned spiking patterns |
| STDP-based SNN | ~5 mJ | ~4,000 spikes | Lowest energy and sparsest activity, suited to ultra-low-power settings |

The table separates **model quality** from **deployment cost**. Accuracy tells whether the model gives the right answer often enough. Energy per inference tells how expensive each answer is to produce. A model can be attractive in one metric and less attractive in another. The ANN/CNN may dominate raw accuracy, while spiking models can reduce energy by about **90-97%** with reasonable performance tradeoffs. Surrogate-trained SNNs are useful when accuracy and low latency are important together. STDP-based SNNs are especially natural for ultra-low-power settings where sparse activity is valuable, even when there is some reduction in accuracy.

A beginner confusion often comes from treating "spike count" as the energy metric by itself. Spike count is a strong clue, but energy per inference is the fuller measurement. A spike becomes costly because it causes communication and synaptic work. If one model emits many spikes, it likely performs many event-driven operations. If another emits fewer spikes while preserving enough task performance, it can spend less energy. The energy metric turns that intuition into a number that can be compared across models.

**Question.** Why report energy per inference instead of total energy over an entire experiment?

**Answer.** Energy per inference gives a fair unit of comparison: one input, one run through the model, one output. Total experiment energy can grow simply because one model is tested on more inputs or run for more trials. Energy per inference asks how costly each individual decision is, which is the quantity needed when choosing a model for repeated use on edge or neuromorphic systems.

The mental model is an event ledger. During one inference, every spike records that a neuron became active enough to send a message. Every synaptic operation records that the message caused work at a connection. Total energy adds the costs of those records. Energy per inference then becomes the price of one decision. A spiking network becomes energy-efficient when it learns or organizes its activity so that useful information travels through relatively few events, and those events trigger only the needed synaptic work.
