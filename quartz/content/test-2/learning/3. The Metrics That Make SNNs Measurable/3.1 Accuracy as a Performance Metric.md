### Accuracy as a Performance Metric

Imagine an SNN has already taken an input image, turned its activity into spikes over time, and produced a label such as "3," "cat," or "gesture left." At that point, the first practical question is simple: did the network choose the right label? Classification gives the network a task with a correct answer, and accuracy turns many of those yes-or-no outcomes into one measurable number. Without such a number, model behavior stays anecdotal: one correct image here, one mistaken image there, with no clear way to compare training methods or decide whether a network is improving.

A single prediction is correct when the label produced by the model matches the true label for the input. If an image really belongs to the digit class "7" and the model outputs "7," that prediction counts as correct. If the same image is labeled "1," it counts as incorrect. Accuracy collects these outcomes across a whole test set and asks what fraction of all predictions were correct:
$$
\text{Accuracy} = \frac{\text{Number of correct predictions}}{\text{Total number of predictions}}
$$
Here, **accuracy** is the fraction of predictions that match the expected labels. The **number of correct predictions** is the count of inputs the model classified properly. The **total number of predictions** is the full count of inputs evaluated, including both correct and incorrect cases. The formula lets you compute one score for classification performance: if a model classifies 970 images correctly out of 1000 test images, then
$$
\text{Accuracy} = \frac{970}{1000} = 0.97
$$
An accuracy of $0.97$ is usually written as $97\%$, because multiplying by 100 converts the fraction into a percentage. This means that, over that test set, 97 out of every 100 predictions were correct on average.

Accuracy makes SNN behavior measurable because spikes themselves do not immediately tell you whether the network solved the task. A spike train may look sparse, fast, or biologically inspired, but classification performance asks whether the final decision is right. For a beginner, it helps to separate the internal activity from the external task. The internal activity is how the network computes; the predicted label is what the network outputs; accuracy measures how often that output agrees with the expected answer.

A useful analogy is a student taking a multiple-choice quiz. The student may use a fast strategy, a slow strategy, or a very energy-efficient strategy, but the quiz score first tells us how many answers were right. If the student answers 95 out of 100 questions correctly, the score is 95%. That score says something real about performance, but it does not tell us how long the student took, how much effort was spent, or whether the strategy would still work on harder questions. Accuracy plays the same role for a classifier: it measures correctness, then leaves other costs to other metrics.

**Question.** If two SNNs both reach 96% accuracy, have they performed equally well?

**Answer.** They have performed equally well only with respect to the fraction of correct classifications. They may still differ in latency, spike count, energy consumption, convergence behavior, or suitability for deployment. One SNN might reach 96% accuracy using many spikes over a long simulation window, while another might reach the same accuracy with fewer spikes and lower energy. Accuracy measures correctness, not the cost of producing that correctness.

This separation is especially important for spiking neural networks because SNNs are often valued for hardware-aware advantages. A conventional artificial neural network can process dense continuous values at every layer, while an SNN communicates through spike events. When fewer spikes are generated, less activity may need to be processed, which can reduce energy use on neuromorphic or edge hardware. Accuracy alone cannot show that benefit. It can say whether the classifier is right, but it cannot say whether the answer arrived quickly or efficiently.

The graph shows why a model comparison changes when energy is considered alongside accuracy. An ANN/CNN can appear strongest if the only goal is raw classification accuracy, but its energy consumption is much higher, around 200 mJ per inference. Converted SNNs reduce energy sharply to about 20 mJ, while still producing a relatively high spike count because rate-coded spike propagation keeps many events active. Surrogate-gradient SNNs sit in a balanced region: they retain strong accuracy while lowering energy to about 15 mJ and reducing spike activity compared with converted SNNs. STDP-based SNNs use the least energy, around 5 mJ per inference, with the sparsest spike count, while accepting some reduction in classification accuracy. The shape of the comparison teaches a practical lesson: the "best" model depends on whether the application values maximum correctness, low energy, sparse activity, or a compromise among them.

Accuracy separates model quality from deployment cost. Model quality begins with whether the network learns a useful input-output mapping: images to labels, gestures to commands, or sensor patterns to categories. Deployment cost asks what the model spends to produce that mapping in the real world. In an edge device, wearable sensor, drone, or robot, a slightly lower accuracy may be acceptable if energy use drops dramatically and the system can run longer on limited power. In a safety-critical classifier, the same accuracy reduction may be unacceptable. Accuracy gives the correctness side of the decision; energy, latency, spike count, and convergence complete the decision.

MNIST and CIFAR-10 represent different levels of classification difficulty. MNIST digit images are simpler, so high accuracy is easier to maintain. CIFAR-10 images are more complex, so performance differences widen as the task becomes harder. Reading the table across columns keeps the comparison honest. A model can look attractive on MNIST accuracy, then lose more ground on CIFAR-10, while still offering major energy savings. Normalized energy consumption gives a relative view of how expensive each model is to run, so the learner can compare correctness and cost on the same page. The table's lesson is that accuracy answers "How often is the model right?" while energy answers "How much does the model spend to be right?"

A common beginner confusion is to treat a high accuracy number as a complete ranking of models. The correct picture is more like choosing a vehicle. A race car may be fastest on a track, but a bicycle may be better for a short trip where fuel, cost, and simplicity matter. In the same way, a high-accuracy ANN may dominate if only the final label matters, while an SNN may be preferable when the system must operate with low power, sparse events, or fast response. The accuracy score is still essential; it prevents energy-efficient models from being praised when they fail the task. But once a model is accurate enough for the application, the other metrics decide whether it is practical.

**Question.** Why keep accuracy as a main metric if it hides energy and latency costs?

**Answer.** Accuracy remains the clearest measure of classification correctness. A low-energy model that often predicts the wrong class has limited value for a classification task. Accuracy establishes whether the model solves the problem, then energy and latency explain how efficiently it solves it. The metrics work together: accuracy checks usefulness, while cost metrics check deployability.

The mental model is a two-stage judgment. First, accuracy asks whether the SNN's spike-based computation leads to the right labels often enough. It converts many individual correct and incorrect predictions into a single classification-performance score. Then the remaining metrics ask what the network had to spend in time, spikes, energy, and training effort to reach that score. Accuracy gives the cleanest starting measurement of model quality, but SNN evaluation becomes meaningful when that correctness is read beside the costs that make the model usable in real hardware.
