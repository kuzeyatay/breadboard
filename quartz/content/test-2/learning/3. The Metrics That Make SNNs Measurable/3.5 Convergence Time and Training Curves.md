After measuring accuracy, loss, latency, energy, and spike counts separately, a new question appears: how quickly does an SNN become useful while it is being trained? Imagine training three SNNs for the same classification task. At the end, two of them may reach similar accuracy, but one may reach that level after only a handful of passes through the data while the other may need many more. If training is expensive, slow, or meant to adapt on an edge device, that difference changes how practical the model feels. Convergence time gives that difference a number.

Training happens in repeated passes called **epochs**. One epoch means the training procedure has gone through the training dataset once. After each epoch, the model has updated its internal parameters and can be evaluated again. If its predictions improve, accuracy rises. If its mistakes become less severe under the chosen objective, loss falls. These two measurements describe different sides of the same training process: accuracy tells how often the model is correct, while loss tells how the optimizer is moving through the learning problem.

To turn "learns quickly" into a measurable statement, we choose a **target accuracy**. The target accuracy is the accuracy level we decide is good enough for a particular comparison, such as 90%, 95%, or any other threshold suitable for the task. Then we ask: at which epoch does the model first reach that target? That first successful epoch is the model's **convergence time** for that target.

A compact way to write this is:
$$
T_{\text{conv}} = \min \{ e : A(e) \geq A_{\text{target}} \}
$$
Here, $T_{\text{conv}}$ is the convergence time, measured in epochs. The symbol $e$ stands for an epoch number. The function $A(e)$ means the model's accuracy measured after epoch $e$. The value $A_{\text{target}}$ is the target accuracy chosen before making the comparison. The expression says: look through the epochs, find every epoch where the model's accuracy is at least the target, and choose the smallest one. That smallest epoch is when the model first becomes good enough by the chosen standard.

For example, suppose an SNN has training accuracies of 70%, 82%, 89%, 93%, and 95% over five epochs, and the target accuracy is 90%. The model has not reached the target at epoch 3 because 89% is still below 90%. It reaches the target at epoch 4 because 93% is at least 90%. Its convergence time is therefore 4 epochs. If another SNN reaches 90% at epoch 8, the first one converges faster for that target, even if both eventually become accurate.

This metric describes training speed, not decision speed. A trained SNN may make an inference in milliseconds, while convergence time is counted across training epochs. Inference latency asks, "How long does one trained model take to produce an answer?" Convergence time asks, "How many rounds of training does the model need before its accuracy reaches the chosen level?" These are different clocks. One clock runs while the model is learning; the other runs after learning, when the model is being used.

**Question.** If an SNN has low inference latency, does that mean it also has fast convergence time?

**Answer.** No. Low inference latency means the trained model can make a decision quickly once it is already trained. Fast convergence time means the model reaches a chosen accuracy in fewer training epochs. A model can respond quickly after training but still require many epochs to train, so the two measurements must be read separately.

Accuracy alone can hide how training unfolded, so convergence is usually read together with curves. A **training loss curve** plots loss against epoch number. At the beginning of training, loss is often higher because the model's parameters are poorly adjusted. As training succeeds, the curve tends to move downward. The shape of that downward movement reveals **optimization behavior**, which means how easily the training method finds better parameter settings. A steep early drop suggests the model is quickly finding useful changes. A flat curve suggests training is struggling, moving slowly, or settling into a poorer solution.

In the comparison across converted SNNs, surrogate-gradient SNNs, and STDP-based SNNs, the loss behavior separates the methods. Surrogate-gradient training reduces loss more rapidly and stabilizes by about the 20th epoch. That shape means the optimizer is receiving a useful training signal and can adjust the SNN efficiently. Converted SNNs can perform competitively, but their training story is different because conversion begins from an ANN-like solution and then must be expressed through spikes and simulation windows. STDP-based SNNs tend to plateau at higher loss values and converge more slowly. That higher plateau means the training process has found a less accurate fit under the supervised classification objective, even though STDP can be attractive when low energy and unsupervised adaptation are the priority.

A table of loss values across epoch columns makes the curve concrete. Each row corresponds to one method: converted SNN, surrogate-gradient SNN, or STDP-based SNN. Each epoch column records the loss after that training pass. Reading across a row shows how one method changes over time. Reading down a column compares the methods at the same training stage. If the surrogate-gradient row drops faster across early epochs, that is the numeric version of a steeper loss curve. If the STDP row remains higher at later epochs, that is the tabular version of a higher plateau.

A **learning curve** usually tracks accuracy over epochs instead of loss. It answers the performance question more directly: how quickly does the model become correct often enough? A rising training accuracy curve means the model is turning training updates into better task performance. When the curve crosses the target accuracy line, the epoch of that crossing is the convergence time. If a surrogate-gradient SNN reaches a high accuracy by around the 20th epoch while another method is still climbing, the surrogate-gradient method has faster convergence for that target. If an STDP-based SNN rises more gradually and levels off lower, its convergence time is longer or may be undefined for a target it never reaches.

Loss and accuracy can move together, but they do not tell the same story. Loss is sensitive to how confident or costly the model's errors are under the training objective. Accuracy counts whether the final predicted class is right. A model can lower loss before accuracy changes much, especially when its predictions are becoming less wrong but have not yet crossed the boundary into correct classification. A model can also show an accuracy jump when many examples cross that boundary at once. Looking at both curves prevents a shallow reading of training progress.

**Question.** If one SNN has the lowest loss at epoch 20, is it automatically the best SNN overall?

**Answer.** It is the strongest on that training-loss measurement at that point, but "best" depends on the goal. Training loss describes optimization progress. Accuracy describes task performance. Latency describes decision timing. Energy and spike count describe resource use. A surrogate-gradient SNN may converge quickly and reach strong accuracy, while an STDP-based SNN may use less energy per inference but converge more slowly and settle at a higher loss.

Convergence time makes training behavior visible as a threshold-crossing event on a curve. The loss curve shows whether the training rule is steadily reducing the objective. The accuracy curve shows when that progress becomes useful task performance. Together, they let you see an SNN as a learning system unfolding over epochs rather than as a single final score. A fast-converging SNN is one whose curve reaches the chosen accuracy early; an efficient deployment SNN may also need low latency, low energy, and low spike count. The mental model is a set of linked timelines: during training, loss should fall and accuracy should rise; after training, latency and energy describe how the learned spiking system behaves when it makes decisions.
