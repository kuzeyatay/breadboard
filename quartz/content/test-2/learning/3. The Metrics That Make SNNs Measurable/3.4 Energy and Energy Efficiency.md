### Energy and Energy Efficiency

You have already measured how often an SNN fires, how many synaptic operations those spikes trigger, and how accurately the model answers a task. Now imagine choosing a model for a small battery-powered camera that classifies what it sees every few milliseconds. Accuracy tells you whether the answer is useful, spike count tells you how active the network was, and synaptic operations tell you how much communication happened between neurons. Energy is the metric that turns those behaviors into a deployment cost: how much physical work the device must spend to produce one inference.

A spiking network saves energy because it communicates through events. When a neuron stays silent, it avoids sending a signal and avoids activating many downstream synapses. When a neuron spikes, the spike has a cost, and the synaptic operations caused by that spike have their own cost. Total energy makes this behavior measurable by adding up the cost of producing spikes and the cost of processing the synaptic events those spikes create.

A simple energy model begins with two counts you already know how to interpret: the number of spikes and the number of synaptic operations. Each spike consumes some amount of energy to generate and transmit. Each synaptic operation consumes some amount of energy to apply the spike's effect to a connection. If a model fires many spikes, or if each spike fans out through many connections, its total energy rises.

The total energy can be written as:
$$
E_{\text{total}} = E_{\text{spike}} \cdot N_{\text{spikes}} + E_{\text{synop}} \cdot N_{\text{synops}}
$$
Here, $E_{\text{total}}$ is the total energy consumed for an inference, often measured in millijoules when comparing models. $E_{\text{spike}}$ is the energy cost of one spike. $N_{\text{spikes}}$ is the number of spikes generated during the inference. $E_{\text{synop}}$ is the energy cost of one synaptic operation. $N_{\text{synops}}$ is the number of synaptic operations performed while processing the inference. The formula lets you compute energy from the network's event activity rather than treating energy as a vague after-the-fact label.

For example, suppose one inference produces $4{,}000$ spikes, and each spike is cheap by itself. The network can still consume noticeable energy if those spikes trigger many synaptic operations. A sparse STDP-based SNN with around $4{,}000$ spikes per inference can reach about $5$ mJ per inference in the comparison, while a converted SNN with around $20{,}000$ spikes can use about $20$ mJ. The difference comes from the amount of event traffic the model creates. Fewer spikes usually mean fewer triggered operations, so the energy bill falls with the activity level.

This is why total energy separates model quality from deployment cost. Accuracy describes how often the model is right. Energy describes how expensive it is to obtain that answer. A conventional ANN in the comparison reaches high raw accuracy, but its continuous-valued operations are associated with about $200$ mJ per inference. A surrogate-gradient SNN uses about $15$ mJ per inference while keeping accuracy close to ANN-level performance, and an STDP-based SNN reaches about $5$ mJ with a modest accuracy tradeoff. These numbers show that two models can be close enough in usefulness for an application while being very different in the energy they require.

**Question.** If a model has high accuracy, why do we still need an energy metric?

**Answer.** Accuracy tells us the benefit of the model's output, but it does not tell us the cost of producing that output. A model that is slightly more accurate can be a poor choice for an edge device if it consumes much more energy per inference. Total energy gives the missing cost side of the comparison, so accuracy can be judged together with the physical resources needed to achieve it.

Once energy is measurable, the next step is to compare useful performance per unit energy. This is the role of normalized energy efficiency. It combines benefit and cost into one hardware-aware comparison: how much accuracy the model delivers for the energy it spends.

The metric can be written as:
$$
\eta_{\text{energy}} = \frac{A}{E_{\text{total}}}
$$
Here, $\eta_{\text{energy}}$ is normalized energy efficiency, $A$ is accuracy, and $E_{\text{total}}$ is the total energy consumption for the inference. If accuracy is written as a fraction, such as $0.978$ for $97.8\%$, then $\eta_{\text{energy}}$ measures accuracy fraction per unit of energy. If accuracy is written as a percentage, the numerical value changes, but the interpretation stays the same as long as all models use the same convention.

This ratio answers a practical question: for each unit of energy spent, how much correct performance does the model provide? A surrogate-gradient SNN with about $97.8\%$ accuracy and $15$ mJ per inference has strong accuracy with moderate energy use. An STDP-based SNN with about $95.5\%$ accuracy and $5$ mJ per inference gives less accuracy, but it spends much less energy to get there. Using the fraction convention, the surrogate model gives roughly $0.978 / 15 \approx 0.065$ accuracy units per mJ, while the STDP model gives roughly $0.955 / 5 \approx 0.191$ accuracy units per mJ. The STDP model therefore looks more energy-efficient under this metric, even though the surrogate model is more accurate in raw terms.

That interpretation is especially useful when comparing SNNs with ANNs. An ANN at about $200$ mJ per inference may have excellent accuracy, but its energy efficiency can be much lower because the denominator in the ratio is large. SNNs can reduce energy use by roughly $90\%$ to $97\%$ compared with ANNs in the comparison, so even a small reduction in accuracy can come with a large gain in efficiency. Normalized energy efficiency makes that tradeoff visible in a single number.

A beginner confusion often comes from treating low spike count and high energy efficiency as the same idea. Spike count is one contributor to energy, but the energy formula also includes the cost per spike, the number of synaptic operations, and the cost per synaptic operation. Two networks could fire the same number of spikes while using different energy if one spike pattern causes more synaptic operations or runs on hardware where each event costs more. Spike count is the activity trace; total energy is the cost computed from that trace.

**Question.** Can a model become less attractive even if its accuracy improves?

**Answer.** Yes. If accuracy rises only slightly while energy consumption rises a lot, normalized energy efficiency can fall. For example, a model that improves from $95.5\%$ to $97.8\%$ accuracy gains $2.3$ percentage points, but if its energy rises from $5$ mJ to $15$ mJ, it spends three times as much energy. Whether that tradeoff is worthwhile depends on the application's needs: a safety-critical task may prefer the higher accuracy, while a tiny battery-powered device may prefer the better efficiency.

Energy and energy efficiency turn SNN comparison into a practical design decision. Total energy converts spikes and synaptic operations into a measurable cost per inference. Normalized energy efficiency then connects that cost to the useful outcome, accuracy. A good mental model is to imagine every inference as a budgeted transaction: spikes and synaptic operations spend energy, accuracy is the value returned, and energy efficiency tells you how much value the model gives back for each unit it spends.
