### Accuracy

After seeing that spiking neural networks make decisions through timed spike events, the next problem is measurement: once the network gives an answer, how do we tell whether its behavior is good? Imagine a small vision system watching handwritten digits. An image of a `7` enters the system, spikes move through the network, and the final output points to one label. If the chosen label is `7`, the prediction is correct. If the chosen label is `1`, `2`, or any other digit, the prediction is incorrect. Before discussing whether the system used little energy, responded quickly, or produced few spikes, we need a simple way to count how often it gets the task right.

That count becomes the metric called **accuracy**. Accuracy measures the fraction of all predictions that are correct. It turns a pile of individual outcomes into one number that can be compared across models. If a network classifies 100 digit images and gives the correct label for 96 of them, its accuracy is 96%. The number does not describe how the network reached those answers; it describes the success rate of the answers themselves.

The formula is:
$$
\text{Accuracy} = \frac{\text{Number of Correct Predictions}}{\text{Total Number of Predictions}} \times 100\%
$$
Here, **correct predictions** means the cases where the model's chosen class matches the true class. **Total predictions** means every case the model attempted to classify. **Accuracy** is the resulting percentage of attempts that were correct. The multiplication by $100\%$ converts the fraction into a percentage, which makes it easier to read. For example, if an SNN classifies 1,000 inputs and 978 labels match the true answers, then:
$$
\text{Accuracy} = \frac{978}{1000} \times 100\% = 97.8\%
$$
This tells us that the network made the right classification for 97.8 out of every 100 examples on average. That number is especially useful when comparing different training strategies. A surrogate-gradient SNN reaching about 97.8% accuracy is behaving very close to a strong conventional neural network on the same classification task, while an STDP-based SNN at about 95.5% accuracy is making more mistakes but still solving most examples correctly. Accuracy makes this difference visible in a compact form.

Accuracy also separates **classification performance** from other kinds of performance. Classification performance asks, "How often does the model choose the right class?" It focuses on correctness of the output. In an SNN, that output may come from spike timing, spike counts, or activity accumulated over a simulation window, but once the network commits to a class, the accuracy calculation treats the answer like any other classifier's answer: right or wrong. This gives SNNs and artificial neural networks a shared measurement language. A dense ANN and a spiking network may compute very differently internally, yet both can be judged by how many labels they assign correctly.

A concrete way to think about this is a classroom quiz. If two students each answer 100 multiple-choice questions, and one answers 98 correctly while the other answers 95 correctly, the first student has higher accuracy. The score does not say who finished faster, who used less effort, or who had the more efficient strategy. It only measures correctness. That narrowness is useful because it keeps the question clean: how reliable are the final answers?

**Question.** If an SNN classifies 500 sensor readings and gets 460 of them right, what is its accuracy?

**Answer.** The number of correct predictions is 460, and the total number of predictions is 500. Using $\text{Accuracy} = \frac{\text{Correct Predictions}}{\text{Total Predictions}} \times 100\%$, the accuracy is $\frac{460}{500} \times 100\% = 92\%$. The SNN chose the correct class for 92 out of every 100 readings on average.

Once accuracy is measured, a second layer of evaluation becomes necessary for spiking systems. SNNs are often studied because they can use sparse spike events, short response times, and low energy per inference. A model that is slightly less accurate may still be better for a battery-powered sensor if it consumes far less energy or responds fast enough for real-time control. A model with the highest accuracy may be unsuitable if it needs a long simulation window, produces many spikes, or spends too much energy per decision.

This is where **deployment tradeoff** enters the picture. A deployment tradeoff is the practical exchange between correctness and the costs of using the model in the real world. For SNNs, common costs include latency, energy consumption, spike count, and convergence behavior during training. Accuracy answers the correctness question, while these other metrics answer the operating question: can the model run where we want to use it?

Consider three SNN design choices. A surrogate-gradient SNN can reach about 97.8% accuracy with latency around 10 milliseconds and moderate energy around 15 millijoules per inference. A converted SNN can remain competitive in accuracy, but may require more spikes and a longer simulation window. An STDP-based SNN may reach lower accuracy, around 95.5%, while using very low energy, around 5 millijoules per inference. Accuracy alone would rank the surrogate-gradient model above the STDP model for correctness, but a tiny always-on sensor may prefer the lower-energy behavior if the small accuracy drop is acceptable.

A beginner confusion often appears here: if accuracy is the score of correctness, it can feel like the model with the highest accuracy should always be chosen. The better mental picture is a set of dials rather than a single scoreboard. One dial measures correctness. Another measures time. Another measures energy. Another measures how many spikes the network emits. In a real edge device or neuromorphic chip, the chosen model must set all of these dials to acceptable positions for the application. A medical alarm, a robot reflex, and a low-power environmental sensor may each require a different balance.

**Question.** Why can two SNNs with similar accuracy still differ greatly in usefulness?

**Answer.** Similar accuracy means they make correct classifications at similar rates, but they may spend very different amounts of time and energy to reach those answers. One SNN may respond in about 10 milliseconds with moderate energy, while another may need a longer simulation window or many more spikes. For deployment, the useful model is the one whose accuracy, latency, energy use, and spike activity fit the task together.

Accuracy is therefore the starting measurement for SNN evaluation. It makes behavior measurable by turning correct and incorrect classifications into a percentage. It separates model quality on the classification task from the practical cost of running the model. Then the rest of the evaluation fills in the missing dimensions: how quickly the spikes lead to an answer, how much energy the inference consumes, how much spiking activity the network produces, and how training behavior develops. The clean mental model is to treat accuracy as the answer-quality axis. It tells us how often the SNN is right, while the deployment metrics tell us what the right answers cost.
