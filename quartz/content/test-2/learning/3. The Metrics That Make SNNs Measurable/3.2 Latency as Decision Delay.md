### Latency as Decision Delay

After accuracy, energy, and spike count, an SNN still needs one more measurement before it can be judged as a working decision system: how long it takes to answer. Imagine a small event-based camera watching for a hand gesture. The scene changes, spikes begin to flow into the network, and the system eventually chooses a class such as "swipe left" or "wave." The decision may be correct, and it may use very little energy, but a delayed answer can make the system feel slow or unsafe in a real-time setting. A gesture recognizer, hearing aid, robot sensor, or edge device needs a decision soon after the stimulus arrives, because the world keeps moving while the network computes.

Latency gives that delay a precise name. A stimulus is the input event or input pattern presented to the system. The stimulus time is the moment that input begins. A decision is the model's chosen output, such as the predicted class. The decision time is the moment when the model has enough output activity to commit to that choice. Latency is the time gap between those two moments: the waiting time from "the input started" to "the model answered."

The metric can be written as a simple time difference:
$$
\text{Latency} = t_{\text{decision}} - t_{\text{stimulus}}
$$
Here, $\text{Latency}$ is the decision delay, $t_{\text{decision}}$ is the time when the model produces its answer, and $t_{\text{stimulus}}$ is the time when the input is presented. When latency is reported in milliseconds, one millisecond means one thousandth of a second. This unit is useful because many SNN decisions happen over very short windows, such as 5 ms, 10 ms, 15 ms, or 20 ms.

A concrete example makes the formula feel less abstract. Suppose a spoken sound reaches an SNN at $t_{\text{stimulus}} = 100$ ms, and the network classifies it at $t_{\text{decision}} = 112$ ms. The latency is
$$
112\text{ ms} - 100\text{ ms} = 12\text{ ms}
$$
That 12 ms value tells you the time cost of the decision itself. The model may have used many or few spikes during that period, and it may have consumed more or less energy, but latency isolates the question: how long did the learner, robot, or device have to wait for the answer?

This is especially natural for SNNs because spikes already live in time. A conventional artificial neural network often processes an input through layers of continuous numerical activations, then reports an output after the computation finishes. An SNN processes discrete spike events, and the timing of those events can help determine when enough evidence has accumulated. If useful spikes arrive early and strongly support one class, the model can answer quickly. If the model needs a longer simulation window to collect enough spikes, the decision comes later.

Converted SNNs show why latency must be measured directly. A converted SNN often begins from an ANN and approximates its continuous activations using spike rates. To estimate a large activation, the converted network may need to observe spikes over a longer time window. That longer window can preserve competitive performance, but it can also increase decision delay. Surrogate gradient SNNs are trained directly with spiking behavior in mind, so they can learn spike patterns that support faster decisions. STDP-based SNNs use spike-timing-based adaptation and can be very efficient in spike count and energy, while their early inference may take slightly longer as spike patterns settle.

The graph shows that models with similar goals can still differ sharply in how fast they answer. CNN-based ANNs are reported around the 20-50 ms range depending on model complexity and hardware configuration. Converted SNNs sit around 20 ms because they may need longer simulation windows to approximate ANN activation rates. Surrogate gradient SNNs reach about 10 ms, making them strong candidates when a system needs both competitive accuracy and fast response. STDP-based SNNs stabilize below about 15 ms under optimized conditions, trading some early-inference speed for very low spike count and energy use.

The table turns the visual pattern into a direct model-selection guide. ANN/CNN systems can require tens of milliseconds. Converted SNNs improve the computation style by using spikes, yet their latency can remain around 20 ms when rate-coded propagation needs enough time to represent activation values. Surrogate gradient SNNs stand out at roughly 10 ms, which means the model can often decide after a shorter spike window. STDP-based SNNs appear slightly slower than surrogate-trained SNNs in early response, but still remain in a low-latency range near or below 15 ms when optimized.

Latency separates model quality from deployment cost. Accuracy asks whether the answer is correct. Energy asks how much work the system spends. Spike count asks how much event traffic the network generates. Latency asks whether the answer arrives soon enough to be useful. A model can look attractive on one metric and less attractive on another. For example, an STDP-based SNN can be appealing for ultra-low-power settings because it produces sparse spiking activity and low energy consumption, while a surrogate gradient SNN can be appealing when the application needs a faster decision with competitive accuracy. Latency makes that tradeoff visible instead of hiding it inside a single performance number.

**Question.** If two SNNs reach similar accuracy, should the one with lower latency always be preferred?

**Answer.** Lower latency is valuable when the application needs fast responses, such as event-based sensing, gesture recognition, or edge inference under real-time constraints. The lower-latency model gives an answer sooner after the stimulus appears. The choice still depends on the full metric profile: energy consumption, spike count, convergence behavior, and accuracy can change the best decision for a particular device or task.

A common beginner confusion is to treat latency as the same thing as computation size. Latency is about elapsed decision time, measured from stimulus to answer. A model with fewer spikes may often use less energy, but it still needs to produce a reliable decision within a time window. A model with many operations may answer quickly on specialized hardware, while a sparse spiking model may need a longer observation window before its output is stable. The metric always returns to the same clock-based question: when did the input begin, and when did the decision become available?

Real-time inference is the setting where this clock becomes central. In real-time inference, the value of a prediction depends partly on its timing. A late gesture classification, a late sensor warning, or a late edge-device response loses usefulness even if the class label is eventually right. SNNs are attractive here because their event-based computation can allow decisions after a small number of meaningful spikes, especially when the network has been trained to use early spike timing effectively.

**Question.** Why does latency use a difference between two times instead of just recording the decision time?

**Answer.** The decision time alone only says when the answer happened on a clock. Latency measures how long the model took after the stimulus appeared. If one input begins at 100 ms and another begins at 500 ms, their decision times cannot be compared fairly unless each is measured relative to its own stimulus time. Subtracting $t_{\text{stimulus}}$ from $t_{\text{decision}}$ turns raw clock time into decision delay.

The mental model is a stopwatch attached to each input. The stopwatch starts when the stimulus is presented and stops when the network commits to a decision. ANN/CNN models, converted SNNs, surrogate gradient SNNs, and STDP-based SNNs can then be compared by the same question: how many milliseconds did the answer take? Once latency is measured this way, speed becomes a concrete part of SNN evaluation rather than an impression. A good SNN decision is therefore understood through a chain of measurable events: a stimulus begins, spikes carry evidence through time, an output decision forms, and latency records the delay between the beginning and the answer.
