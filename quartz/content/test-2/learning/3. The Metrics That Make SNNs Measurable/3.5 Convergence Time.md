## Convergence Time

Imagine training three spiking neural networks to recognize the same kind of input pattern. After each pass through the training data, you test each network and record its accuracy. One network reaches useful accuracy quickly, another reaches a similar accuracy only after many more passes, and a third improves slowly and levels off before it becomes accurate enough for the application. If you only looked at the final accuracy, the first two networks might appear equally good. If you only looked at inference latency, you would learn how quickly a trained network responds after deployment. Convergence time measures a different part of the story: how quickly the network learns during training.

Training happens in repeated rounds called **epochs**. An epoch is one complete pass through the training data. After one epoch, the model has had one full opportunity to adjust its internal parameters using the examples it was given. After ten epochs, it has gone through that same training set ten times. A **learning curve** records how performance changes across these epochs, often by plotting accuracy upward or loss downward as training continues. When the curve rises quickly, the model is learning useful patterns early. When it rises slowly, the model needs more training rounds before it becomes useful. When it flattens, the model has reached a stable region where additional epochs produce smaller changes.

A practical training process needs a performance goal. That goal is called a **target accuracy**: the accuracy level the model must reach before we consider the training successful enough for the intended use. A target accuracy might be chosen because the application needs a certain reliability level, or because several models are being compared under the same evaluation protocol. For example, if the target accuracy is 90%, then a model that reaches 90% after 8 epochs has learned faster than a model that reaches 90% after 18 epochs, even if both eventually pass the same threshold.

Convergence time turns that idea into a measurable quantity by asking for the earliest training epoch at which the model reaches the chosen target accuracy:
$$
T_{\text{conv}} = \min \{ e : A(e) \ge A_{\text{target}} \}
$$
Here, $T_{\text{conv}}$ is the **convergence time**, measured in epochs. The symbol $e$ represents an epoch number. The function $A(e)$ means the model's accuracy after epoch $e$. The value $A_{\text{target}}$ is the target accuracy. The expression $\min$ means "choose the smallest value that satisfies the condition." The condition $A(e) \ge A_{\text{target}}$ says that the model's accuracy at epoch $e$ has reached or exceeded the required accuracy. The formula lets you compute the first moment in training when the model becomes good enough by the chosen standard.

A small worked interpretation makes the formula concrete. Suppose an SNN has accuracies of 72%, 81%, 87%, 91%, and 92% across epochs 1 through 5, and the target accuracy is 90%. The model misses the target at epoch 3 because 87% is below 90%. It reaches the target at epoch 4 because 91% is at least 90%. Since epoch 4 is the earliest epoch where the condition is true, the convergence time is $T_{\text{conv}} = 4$ epochs. If another SNN reaches 90% only at epoch 12, the first network has the faster training speed under this target, even if both networks later achieve similar accuracy.

This metric is especially useful for spiking neural networks because different training strategies can learn at different rates. Surrogate-gradient-trained SNNs can reduce training loss rapidly and stabilize by about the 20th epoch in the reported comparison, while STDP-based SNNs tend to converge more slowly and plateau at higher loss values. That means convergence time can distinguish two learning rules even before we discuss their energy use or inference behavior. A surrogate-trained model that reaches the target earlier may be attractive when the goal is fast supervised training and strong classification performance. An STDP-based model may still be attractive when very low energy use or unsupervised adaptation is the priority, but its longer convergence behavior tells us that it usually needs more training exposure before stabilizing.

The phrase **training speed** can be misleading if it is treated as the same thing as response speed. In SNN evaluation, **latency** describes how long a trained model takes to produce an output during inference, such as responding within a small simulation window after receiving an input. Convergence time describes how many epochs are needed during training before the model reaches the target accuracy. These are both speed-like measurements, but they live at different stages of the model's life. Latency is about deployed response. Convergence time is about learning progress.

**Question.** If an SNN has very low inference latency, does that automatically mean it has a short convergence time?

**Answer.** Low inference latency means the trained network can produce an output quickly once it is being used. Short convergence time means the network reached the target accuracy in few training epochs. A model can respond quickly after training while still requiring many epochs to learn. Another model can learn quickly but require a longer simulation window during inference. The two measurements answer different questions, so they should be recorded separately.

Convergence time also helps separate model quality from deployment cost. Final accuracy tells us how good the trained model becomes. Energy per inference tells us how much energy each deployed prediction costs. Spike count tells us how much event activity the network uses. Latency tells us how quickly a prediction arrives. Convergence time adds the missing training-side view: how quickly the learning process reaches a usable level. Without it, two SNNs that end near the same accuracy could appear equivalent even though one required far more training epochs to get there.

Consider a comparison among three SNN approaches. A surrogate-gradient SNN may reach competitive accuracy within a small number of epochs and stabilize by the 20th epoch, while maintaining moderate spike counts and low inference latency. A converted SNN may also achieve competitive performance, but can require higher spike counts and longer simulation windows. An STDP-based SNN may use very little energy per inference, around the low millijoule range in the reported comparison, yet converge more slowly and stabilize at a higher loss. Convergence time makes these differences easier to reason about because it prevents "good final score" from hiding the cost of reaching that score.

**Question.** Why does convergence time use the minimum epoch instead of an average epoch or the final epoch?

**Answer.** The minimum epoch captures the first point where the model becomes usable under the chosen target. The final epoch only says what happened when training stopped, which may be long after the model already reached the target. An average over epochs would mix together early weak performance and later stronger performance, making it harder to identify the moment of success. The minimum epoch directly answers the practical question: "How soon did training reach the required accuracy?"

A useful mental model is to picture training as a climb toward a marked line on a hill. Accuracy is the height of the climber, the target accuracy is the marked line, and each epoch is one step along the path. Convergence time is the number of the first step that reaches the line. It does not describe how fast the climber runs after reaching the top, and it does not describe how much energy each later action costs. It describes the learning journey up to the point where the model first becomes good enough, which is why it belongs beside accuracy, latency, energy, and spike count as a separate metric for understanding spiking neural networks.
