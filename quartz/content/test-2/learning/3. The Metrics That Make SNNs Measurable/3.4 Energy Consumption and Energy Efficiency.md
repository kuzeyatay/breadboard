### Energy Consumption and Energy Efficiency

After spike count as computational activity, energy consumption and energy efficiency becomes the next question to resolve. The idea now is to make the relationship concrete: what changes in the mechanism, measurement, result, or limitation, and why that change matters for the system being studied.

To measure that saving, energy has to become an accounting quantity. A vague statement like "this model is efficient" does little by itself, because two different spiking models can reach similar accuracy while producing very different numbers of spikes. The measurable question is: how many energy-costing events happened, and how expensive was each kind of event? In an SNN, two pieces are especially useful for that accounting. One piece is the cost of producing or handling spikes. The other piece is the cost of synaptic operations, the computations that happen when a spike influences connected neurons through synapses.

A spike is a discrete event emitted by a neuron. If a neuron fires many times during one inference, it contributes many events to the total activity. A synaptic operation happens when that activity is transmitted through a connection and affects another neuron's accumulated signal. A single spike can produce many synaptic operations if it fans out to many downstream connections, so spike count and synaptic operation count are related but measured separately. Spike count tells us how often neurons speak. Synaptic operation count tells us how much connected computation that speaking triggers.

This leads to a simple energy model:
$$
E_{\text{total}} = E_{\text{spike}} \times N_{\text{spikes}} + E_{\text{synop}} \times N_{\text{synops}}
$$
Here, $E_{\text{total}}$ means total energy consumption for the inference. $E_{\text{spike}}$ is the energy cost of one spike. $N_{\text{spikes}}$ is the number of spikes generated. $E_{\text{synop}}$ is the energy cost of one synaptic operation. $N_{\text{synops}}$ is the number of synaptic operations performed. The formula lets you compute energy as a weighted sum: count each kind of activity, multiply it by the cost of that activity, and add the results.

A concrete interpretation makes the formula feel less abstract. Suppose one inference produces $4{,}000$ spikes, each spike costs $0.001$ energy units, and the same inference performs $50{,}000$ synaptic operations, each costing $0.00002$ energy units. The spike contribution is $0.001 \times 4{,}000 = 4$. The synaptic-operation contribution is $0.00002 \times 50{,}000 = 1$. The total energy is $4 + 1 = 5$ energy units. In this example, most of the energy comes from spikes; in another network, the synaptic operations could dominate if there are many more connections activated per spike. The formula gives a way to see which part of the system is actually spending the energy.

The comparison among ANN, converted SNN, surrogate-gradient SNN, and STDP-based SNN behavior fits this accounting view. The ANN case sits around 200 mJ per inference because continuous-valued operations keep dense computation active. The converted SNN drops to about 20 mJ, but it still produces around 20,000 spikes because rate-coded activity can require many spike events to represent values over time. The surrogate-gradient SNN lowers energy further to about 15 mJ and around 12,000 spikes, showing that training the spike patterns directly can reduce activity while keeping useful performance. The STDP-based SNN reaches about 5 mJ and roughly 4,000 spikes, the sparsest and lowest-energy case in the comparison, with a slightly reduced accuracy tradeoff. The trend connects the formula to behavior: lower spike counts and fewer resulting operations reduce the activity terms that feed total energy.

**Question.** If two SNNs use the same hardware, why can the one with fewer spikes still fail to be the lower-energy model?

**Answer.** Fewer spikes reduce the spike-related part of the energy, $E_{\text{spike}} \times N_{\text{spikes}}$, but total energy also includes synaptic operations. If the lower-spike model sends each spike through many more active connections, its $N_{\text{synops}}$ can be large enough that $E_{\text{synop}} \times N_{\text{synops}}$ raises the total energy. Energy follows both spikes and synapses.

Once total energy is measurable, the next question is whether the energy bought useful correctness. A model that spends very little energy while making poor predictions may be unattractive for a real task. A model with excellent accuracy may also be unattractive for a small device if it consumes too much power per inference. The useful metric has to keep accuracy and energy in the same frame.

Normalized energy efficiency does that with a division:
$$
\eta_{\text{norm}} = \frac{A}{E_{\text{total}}}
$$
Here, $\eta_{\text{norm}}$ means normalized energy efficiency. $A$ is accuracy, usually treated as a percentage or fraction of correct predictions. $E_{\text{total}}$ is the energy consumption for the inference, computed from the activity and operation costs. The formula answers the question: how much correctness does the model deliver per unit of energy spent?

If accuracy is written as a percentage, a model with $95\%$ accuracy and $20$ mJ per inference has an efficiency score of $95 / 20 = 4.75$ accuracy-per-mJ units. A model with $90\%$ accuracy and $5$ mJ per inference has an efficiency score of $90 / 5 = 18$ accuracy-per-mJ units. The second model gives up some raw accuracy, but each millijoule buys much more correct behavior. That is why normalized energy efficiency is a tradeoff metric. It keeps accuracy visible while rewarding models that achieve useful accuracy with less energy.

This helps explain why different SNN training approaches can be preferred in different settings. Surrogate-gradient SNNs can stay close to ANN-like accuracy while using far less energy than dense continuous computation. Converted SNNs can preserve competitive performance but may require higher spike counts and longer simulation windows, which raises activity. STDP-based SNNs can be especially attractive for ultra-low-power settings because they produce very sparse activity and very low energy per inference, even when their accuracy is slightly lower. A battery-powered edge device may prefer the model that gives enough correctness at a much lower energy cost, while a task with tighter accuracy demands may prefer spending more energy for a higher score.

**Question.** Does a higher normalized energy efficiency score always mean the model has higher accuracy?

**Answer.** A higher score means the model delivers more accuracy per unit of energy. The score can rise because accuracy improves, because energy drops, or because both happen together. A slightly less accurate model can have higher normalized energy efficiency when its energy consumption is much lower.

The mental model is an energy ledger tied to useful performance. Spikes are event messages, synaptic operations are the connected computations those messages trigger, and total energy is the cost of both counted together. Accuracy says how often the model is correct. Normalized energy efficiency joins the two sides by asking how much correctness is obtained for each unit of energy. This turns SNN evaluation into a practical selection problem: choose the network whose spike activity, synaptic work, energy consumption, and accuracy fit the constraints of the task.
