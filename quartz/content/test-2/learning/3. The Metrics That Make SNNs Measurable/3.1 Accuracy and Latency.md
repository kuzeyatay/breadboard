### Accuracy and Latency

Imagine an SNN watching a simple input, such as an image or sensor reading, and producing a prediction. The input appears, spikes begin to flow through the network, neurons accumulate evidence over time, and eventually the system settles on an answer. If the network says "cat" when the target answer is "cat," the prediction is successful. If it says "dog," the prediction fails. That gives us one kind of measurement: whether the answer is right. But an SNN is also a time-based system, so another question appears immediately: how long did the network need before it reached that answer?

Those two questions lead to two different metrics: **accuracy** and **latency**. Accuracy measures correctness. Latency measures time to decision. A model can be accurate but slow, or fast but less reliable. In an SNN, both measurements are needed because the network does its work through spike events that unfold over time. The final prediction tells us what the network decided; the decision time tells us how quickly the spike-based computation became useful.

Start with accuracy, because it is the most direct way to make behavior measurable. A prediction is the model's chosen output for one input. A target is the answer we want the model to match. A **correct prediction** happens when the prediction and target agree. If an SNN receives an image whose target class is "3," and the network predicts "3," that trial counts as correct. If it predicts "8," that trial counts as incorrect. Accuracy takes many such trials and asks what fraction were correct.

The formula is:
$$
\text{Accuracy}=\frac{\text{Number of correct predictions}}{\text{Total number of predictions}}
$$
Here, **accuracy** is the fraction of predictions that match their targets. The **number of correct predictions** is the count of trials where the model's output equals the target. The **total number of predictions** is the total count of inputs on which the model made a prediction. The formula lets you compute how often the SNN chooses the right answer across a set of examples.

For example, suppose an SNN classifies 100 sensor inputs. If 96 predictions match their targets, then:
$$
\text{Accuracy}=\frac{96}{100}=0.96
$$
An accuracy of $0.96$ is often written as $96\%$. This means that out of every 100 predictions, about 96 are correct. The number does not tell you which individual examples were difficult, and it does not tell you how long the network took. It gives a clean behavioral measurement: when the network commits to an answer, how often is that answer right?

That measurement is especially useful because SNNs can be trained or built in different ways. Some methods aim to preserve high prediction quality, while others reduce spike activity or shorten the time window used for inference. Accuracy gives all of these systems a shared correctness scale. If one SNN reaches $97.8\%$ accuracy and another reaches $95.5\%$, the first one is making fewer classification mistakes on the measured task. The metric turns "seems good" into a countable statement about correct and total predictions.

**Question.** If two SNNs both reach $96\%$ accuracy, are they equally good for every use case?

**Answer.** They are equally good only on the correctness measurement being reported. They both make the same fraction of correct predictions, but accuracy does not say how long each inference takes. One network might reach its answer quickly after only a short burst of spikes, while the other might need a longer simulation window. For time-sensitive use, the faster network may be more useful even when the accuracy number is the same.

Latency enters because an SNN does not usually compute its output in a single timeless step. A stimulus appears, spikes are generated, neurons integrate incoming signals, and a decision becomes available after some elapsed time. A **stimulus time** is the moment the input begins affecting the network. A **decision time** is the moment the network has produced the answer being measured. **Latency** is the time between those two moments.

The formula is:
$$
\text{Latency}=t_{\text{decision}}-t_{\text{stimulus}}
$$
Here, $\text{Latency}$ is the elapsed time needed to reach a decision. $t_{\text{stimulus}}$ is the time when the stimulus appears or begins. $t_{\text{decision}}$ is the time when the model's decision is made available. The formula lets you compute how long inference takes from input arrival to usable output.

Suppose a neuromorphic vision system receives a stimulus at $0$ milliseconds and reaches its decision at $10$ milliseconds. Then:
$$
\text{Latency}=10\text{ ms}-0\text{ ms}=10\text{ ms}
$$
That means the system needed 10 milliseconds to turn the incoming event pattern into a decision. If another SNN reaches the same kind of decision at $30$ milliseconds, its latency is longer. The difference matters whenever the value of a prediction depends on timing. A fast decision can be useful in real-time settings because the network responds while the situation is still current.

The spike-based nature of SNNs makes latency more than a stopwatch detail. A neuron in an SNN often behaves like a simple accumulator: it receives incoming spikes, its internal state moves toward a firing threshold, and it emits a spike when that threshold is crossed. A decision may require enough spikes to arrive so that downstream neurons separate one answer from another. If the network waits for a longer time window, it may collect more evidence. If it decides earlier, it may act with less accumulated evidence. Latency therefore measures how quickly the network turns temporal spike activity into a usable output.

A beginner confusion often comes from treating accuracy and latency as if one automatically determines the other. A high-accuracy SNN has learned or preserved useful decision boundaries, but that does not guarantee a short decision time. A low-latency SNN reaches a decision quickly, but that does not guarantee the decision is correct. The two metrics look at different sides of inference. Accuracy asks, "Was the final answer right?" Latency asks, "How much time passed before the answer appeared?"

**Question.** Why can't accuracy alone describe whether an SNN is successful?

**Answer.** Accuracy only measures the fraction of correct predictions. It does not reveal how quickly the network reaches those predictions, how many spikes were needed, or what computational cost was involved. For an SNN, time is part of the computation, so a complete evaluation needs a metric such as latency alongside accuracy.

Consider two SNNs used on the same classification task. One reaches about $97.8\%$ accuracy with latency around $10$ milliseconds. Another reaches about $95.5\%$ accuracy while emphasizing a different efficiency profile. The first system looks stronger if the goal is low-latency correctness: it is both highly accurate and quick to decide. The second may still be valuable in situations where other constraints dominate, but accuracy alone would hide the time dimension, and latency alone would hide the correctness dimension.

The mental model is to picture inference as a race toward a reliable answer. The stimulus starts the clock. Spikes carry evidence through the network. The decision time stops the clock. Accuracy checks whether the answer at the finish line matches the target, while latency measures how long the race took. An SNN is measurable because these two quantities turn its behavior into numbers: one number for correctness, one number for time. Together they let you ask whether the network merely gets the answer right, or gets it right quickly enough for the task in front of it.
