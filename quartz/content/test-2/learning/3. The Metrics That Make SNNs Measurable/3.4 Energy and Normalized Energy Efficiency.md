## Energy and Normalized Energy Efficiency

Imagine two spiking models watching the same event stream from a small camera. Both usually choose the right class, and both produce spikes while they decide. One model fires constantly, sending thousands of events through many synapses before each answer. The other stays mostly quiet and fires only when enough evidence has built up. If accuracy is the only number you look at, the two models may appear nearly equal. Once spike count and energy consumption are included, their behavior becomes measurable in a richer way: the answer has a cost, and the cost depends on how much spiking activity the network needed to produce it.

A spiking neural network spends energy when neurons emit spikes and when those spikes cause downstream synaptic updates. A spike is a discrete event, so a quiet neuron contributes little activity during the moments when it remains silent. When a neuron fires, the network must move that event forward, update receiving neurons, and possibly trigger later spikes. The physical cost of one inference therefore grows with the amount of event traffic and the amount of synaptic work caused by that traffic. Spike count gives a visible trace of that traffic, while energy consumption turns it into a cost that can be compared across models.

The simplest energy metric begins by separating the cost into two parts. The total energy for an inference can be written as
$$
E_{\text{total}} = E_{\text{spike}} + E_{\text{synop}}
$$
Here, $E_{\text{total}}$ means the total energy consumed to process one input and produce one output. $E_{\text{spike}}$ means the energy associated with generating and transmitting spike events. $E_{\text{synop}}$ means the energy associated with synaptic operations, the updates that happen when spikes arrive at connections between neurons. This formula lets you compute the energy cost of one inference by adding the energy used by spiking activity to the energy used by synaptic processing.

A concrete interpretation helps. Suppose a model produces many spikes during one input. Those spikes raise $E_{\text{spike}}$, because more events must be generated and communicated. If those spikes fan out through many connections, they also raise $E_{\text{synop}}$, because each arriving spike can cause synaptic work in the next layer. A model with fewer spikes can reduce both terms at once: fewer events are emitted, and fewer downstream operations are triggered. That is why spike count is more than a descriptive statistic. It is a behavioral signal that helps explain where energy is being spent.

This energy view separates performance from cost. Accuracy tells you how often the model is correct. Total energy tells you how much work the model performs to obtain those answers. A conventional artificial neural network can achieve strong accuracy while using continuous-valued operations throughout the network. In the reported comparison, the ANN consumes about 200 mJ per inference. A converted SNN lowers that cost to about 20 mJ, while still producing a relatively high spike count of about 20,000 spikes per inference because rate-coded propagation requires many events over time. A surrogate-gradient SNN reaches about 15 mJ with about 12,000 spikes, showing that learning spike patterns directly can reduce both energy and event traffic. An STDP-based SNN reaches about 5 mJ with about 4,000 spikes, showing the sparsest activity and lowest energy among the compared models, with a tradeoff in raw accuracy.

Those numbers give the formula a physical meaning. Moving from 200 mJ to 20 mJ is a tenfold reduction in energy per inference. Moving from 20 mJ to 5 mJ is another fourfold reduction. The difference comes from how much computation is carried by sparse events rather than continuous activity. A quiet interval in an SNN can be useful because the network avoids unnecessary operations during that interval. When the task or hardware rewards low power, the sparse model can become more attractive even when another model has slightly higher accuracy.

**Question.** If two SNNs have similar accuracy, why might the one with fewer spikes consume less energy?

**Answer.** Fewer spikes usually mean fewer emitted events and fewer downstream synaptic operations. In the energy formula $E_{\text{total}} = E_{\text{spike}} + E_{\text{synop}}$, reducing spike traffic can lower $E_{\text{spike}}$, and reducing the number of spike-triggered connection updates can lower $E_{\text{synop}}$. Similar accuracy therefore does not imply similar energy cost.

Once energy is measurable, the next step is to connect it directly to correctness. A model that uses almost no energy but answers poorly is weak. A model that answers well but spends far more energy than needed may be unsuitable for an edge device, robot, or low-power sensor. Normalized energy efficiency combines these two concerns in one ratio:
$$
\eta_{\text{energy}} = \frac{\text{Accuracy}}{E_{\text{total}}}
$$
Here, $\eta_{\text{energy}}$ is normalized energy efficiency. Accuracy is the fraction or percentage of correct predictions, depending on the chosen reporting scale. $E_{\text{total}}$ is the total energy consumption for the inference or evaluation unit being compared. The ratio tells you how much correctness the model delivers per unit of energy. If accuracy is written as a percentage, the value reads as percentage-points of accuracy per millijoule when energy is measured in mJ. If accuracy is written as a fraction between 0 and 1, the value reads as fractional correctness per millijoule.

Consider two models. Model A reaches 98% accuracy and consumes 200 mJ per inference. Its efficiency is
$$
\eta_{\text{energy}} = \frac{98}{200} = 0.49
$$
Using percentage accuracy per mJ. Model B reaches 95% accuracy and consumes 20 mJ per inference. Its efficiency is
$$
\eta_{\text{energy}} = \frac{95}{20} = 4.75
$$
On raw accuracy alone, Model A looks stronger. On normalized energy efficiency, Model B delivers far more correct performance per millijoule. The ratio makes the tradeoff visible: a small loss in accuracy can be worthwhile when the energy reduction is large.

A beginner confusion often appears here: normalized energy efficiency can make a slightly less accurate model look better than a more accurate model. That is exactly what the metric is designed to reveal. Accuracy measures correctness by itself. Energy efficiency measures correctness under an energy budget. In a cloud setting with abundant power, the highest-accuracy model may be preferred. In a battery-powered or neuromorphic setting, the model that gives strong accuracy with sparse spiking and low synaptic work may be the better engineering choice.

**Question.** Can normalized energy efficiency replace accuracy when evaluating an SNN?

**Answer.** Normalized energy efficiency should be read alongside accuracy. It explains how much accuracy is obtained per unit of energy, but the accuracy value still matters because an extremely efficient model with poor correctness may fail the task. The metric is most useful when comparing models that all reach an acceptable accuracy range and differ in energy consumption.

The mental model is a chain. Spikes create event traffic. Event traffic causes synaptic operations. Spikes and synaptic operations consume energy. Total energy gathers those costs into one measurable quantity. Normalized energy efficiency then asks how much correctness the network buys with that energy. An SNN becomes measurable because its behavior, its cost, and its accuracy can be placed in the same frame: the best design is judged by what it gets right and by how economically it gets there.
