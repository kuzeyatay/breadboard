Because spike count already gives a way to see how active an SNN is, the natural next question is how that activity turns into a cost. Imagine a small vision sensor making one prediction from a camera frame. If the scene is quiet, many neurons stay silent. If an edge moves across the image, only the neurons involved in that change may fire. A spike count tells us how many discrete events occurred, but a deployed system still needs a practical answer: how much energy did one inference consume? Energy gives the spike count a physical meaning. It turns "the network fired 4,000 times" into "the network spent a certain amount of work to produce one prediction."

An inference is one complete pass from input to output: the sensor receives data, neurons respond over time, synapses pass signals forward, and the model produces a class or decision. During that process, energy is spent in two closely related ways. A neuron spends energy when it emits a spike event. A synapse spends energy when it carries that event to another neuron and updates or accumulates its effect. The first cost belongs to spiking activity itself; the second cost belongs to synaptic operations, the communication and accumulation work caused by spikes moving through the network.

That gives a simple way to measure total energy per inference. Count the spike events, count the synaptic operations they trigger, assign an energy cost to each kind of work, and add the two parts:
$$
E_{\text{total}} = N_{\text{spikes}} \cdot E_{\text{spike}} + N_{\text{synops}} \cdot E_{\text{synop}}
$$
Here, $E_{\text{total}}$ is the total energy consumption for one inference. $N_{\text{spikes}}$ is the number of spikes generated during that inference. $E_{\text{spike}}$ is the energy cost of producing one spike. $N_{\text{synops}}$ is the number of synaptic operations caused by spike transmission and accumulation. $E_{\text{synop}}$ is the energy cost of one synaptic operation. The formula lets you compute energy from behavior: more spikes and more synaptic activity raise the cost, while sparse firing lowers it.

A useful way to read the formula is to treat every spike as the beginning of a small chain of work. One spike costs energy at the neuron, then may cause several downstream synapses to do work. If a neuron fires into 100 outgoing synapses, one event can create many synaptic operations. That is why spike count is a strong proxy for network activity but total energy gives a fuller measurement. A low spike count usually helps, yet the fan-out pattern of the network also shapes the final energy cost.

For a concrete interpretation, suppose one inference produces $4{,}000$ spikes, each spike costs $0.0002$ mJ, the spikes cause $80{,}000$ synaptic operations, and each synaptic operation costs $0.00001$ mJ. The spike portion is $4{,}000 \times 0.0002 = 0.8$ mJ. The synaptic portion is $80{,}000 \times 0.00001 = 0.8$ mJ. The total energy is $1.6$ mJ for that inference. The calculation shows why energy is best understood as work per inference: the model spends energy only through the events and operations needed to reach its prediction.

This measurement also explains why sparse event-driven processing can be so efficient. A conventional dense network repeatedly processes continuous-valued activations across large arrays of operations. A spiking network can let silence carry meaning: when a neuron has no spike to send, downstream work can be avoided. Reported comparisons show this pattern clearly. ANN-style computation can reach around 200 mJ per inference, while converted SNNs can reduce the cost to about 20 mJ, surrogate-gradient SNNs to about 15 mJ, and STDP-based SNNs to about 5 mJ. The spike counts follow the same general direction: converted SNNs may produce around 20,000 spikes per inference, surrogate-gradient SNNs around 12,000, and STDP-based SNNs around 4,000. The trend means that fewer events usually go together with lower energy, especially when the network and hardware can avoid work during silent periods.

Energy alone still leaves out the purpose of the inference. A model that spends very little energy but gives poor predictions is cheap in the same way an unplugged device is cheap: it consumes little while providing little useful output. Accuracy supplies the usefulness side of the measurement. Accuracy is the fraction or percentage of predictions the model gets correct. To connect usefulness and cost, normalized energy efficiency compares prediction quality with energy consumption:
$$
\eta_{\text{norm}} = \frac{A}{E_{\text{total}}}
$$
Here, $\eta_{\text{norm}}$ is normalized energy efficiency, $A$ is accuracy, and $E_{\text{total}}$ is the energy consumed per inference. If accuracy is written as a fraction, such as $0.95$, then the metric reads as accuracy per unit of energy. If accuracy is written as a percentage, such as $95\%$, the same idea applies as percent accuracy per millijoule. The formula lets you ask how much predictive value the model gives for each unit of energy it spends.

Consider two SNNs. Model A reaches $96\%$ accuracy and uses $15$ mJ per inference. Its efficiency is $96 / 15 = 6.4$ percentage-points per mJ. Model B reaches $90\%$ accuracy and uses $5$ mJ per inference. Its efficiency is $90 / 5 = 18$ percentage-points per mJ. Model B spends far less energy for each unit of correct predictive performance, even though Model A has higher raw accuracy. This is the tradeoff the metric is designed to make visible. Accuracy tells you how often the model is right; total energy tells you what the prediction costs; normalized energy efficiency connects the two into one deployment-aware measure.

**Question.** If one SNN has the lowest energy consumption, should it always be chosen?

**Answer.** The decision depends on the amount of accuracy delivered for that energy. A very low-energy model can be attractive for edge devices, sensors, and battery-powered systems, especially when its accuracy remains within the needed range. If its accuracy drops below the task requirement, the energy saving loses practical value. Normalized energy efficiency helps compare models by asking how much correct prediction performance each millijoule buys.

A beginner confusion often comes from treating spike count, energy, and efficiency as the same measurement. They are connected measurements with different jobs. Spike count describes event activity. Total energy converts event activity and synaptic work into a physical cost. Normalized energy efficiency connects that cost to prediction quality. A network with fewer spikes often consumes less energy, but the final energy also depends on how costly each spike is, how many synaptic operations are triggered, and what hardware carries out those operations.

**Question.** Why include synaptic operations if spike count is already measured?

**Answer.** A spike becomes computational work when it affects other neurons through synapses. Two networks can produce the same number of spikes while creating different amounts of downstream synaptic activity. Including $N_{\text{synops}} \cdot E_{\text{synop}}$ captures the communication and accumulation work that spike count alone can hide.

The mental model is a chain from events to work to useful work. Spikes are the visible events. Synaptic operations are the work those events cause inside the network. Total energy adds the cost of both so one inference becomes measurable. Normalized energy efficiency then asks whether the prediction quality justifies that cost. An SNN becomes attractive when sparse spiking keeps the work per inference low while accuracy stays high enough for the task.
