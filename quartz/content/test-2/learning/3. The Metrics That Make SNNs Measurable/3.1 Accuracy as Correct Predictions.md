### Accuracy as Correct Predictions

Once a spiking neural network has produced a prediction, the simplest way to judge its behavior is to ask whether the prediction matches the right answer. Imagine a small vision system on an edge device that sees handwritten digits. Each input image enters the network, spikes move through the layers over a short simulation window, and the network finally chooses one label: maybe `0`, `1`, `2`, and so on. For one image, the question is plain: did the network choose the correct digit? For a whole set of images, that same yes-or-no check becomes a measurable signal. Instead of relying on a vague impression that the model "seems good," accuracy turns many individual decisions into one number.

A prediction is the model's chosen answer for one input. A correct prediction is a chosen answer that matches the true label. A total prediction is one opportunity the model had to make a choice, usually one input example in an evaluation set. If a network classifies 100 digit images, it has made 100 total predictions. If 96 of those labels match the true labels, it has made 96 correct predictions. Accuracy measures the fraction of total predictions that were correct:
$$
\text{Accuracy} = \frac{\text{number of correct predictions}}{\text{total number of predictions}}
$$
In this formula, the numerator, `number of correct predictions`, counts how many times the network's output matched the expected answer. The denominator, `total number of predictions`, counts how many examples were evaluated. The result, `accuracy`, is a fraction between 0 and 1. Multiplying by 100 turns that fraction into a percentage. With 96 correct predictions out of 100 total predictions,
$$
\text{Accuracy} = \frac{96}{100} = 0.96 = 96\%
$$
That percentage says: out of every 100 examples like the ones tested, the model answered correctly about 96 times. It gives a direct measure of task performance. For an SNN trained to recognize images, gestures, or signals, accuracy answers the most immediate question: how often does the network get the answer right?

Accuracy is especially useful because it separates performance from cost. Performance means the quality of the answer. Cost means what the network had to spend to produce that answer, such as time, energy, and spike activity. If two models both classify the same test set, accuracy lets us compare their correctness before asking how expensive their computations were. A surrogate-gradient SNN that reaches about 97.8% accuracy is performing extremely well on correctness. An STDP-based SNN around 95.5% accuracy is slightly lower on correctness. Accuracy gives a clean way to see that difference.

A helpful analogy is grading two students on the same quiz. If one student gets 98 out of 100 questions correct and another gets 96 out of 100 correct, the score tells you who answered more questions correctly. That score does not tell you how long each student took, how much effort they spent, or whether one method would work better under a strict time limit. Accuracy plays the role of the quiz score. It is the starting measurement of correctness, and other metrics explain the conditions under which that correctness was achieved.

For spiking neural networks, those conditions matter because spikes are the whole computational currency. An SNN may reach high accuracy by producing many spikes over a longer simulation window. Another SNN may give up a small amount of accuracy while using far fewer spikes and much less energy. A converted SNN can achieve competitive accuracy, yet it may require a higher spike count and longer simulation time because information is often carried through rate-coded spike patterns. A surrogate-gradient SNN can balance high accuracy with low latency, reaching fast inference around 10 milliseconds in some settings. An STDP-based SNN can use very sparse spiking activity and very low energy, such as around 5 millijoules per inference, while accepting somewhat lower accuracy and slower convergence.

This is why accuracy must sit inside a multi-metric evaluation. A multi-metric evaluation judges a model along several axes at once: accuracy for correctness, latency for time, energy consumption for power cost, spike count for event activity, and convergence for how training progresses. Each metric answers a different practical question. Accuracy asks, "How often is the answer right?" Latency asks, "How quickly does the answer arrive?" Energy asks, "How much power does the answer cost?" Spike count asks, "How much spiking activity was needed?" Convergence asks, "How efficiently did learning settle into useful behavior?"

**Question.** If one SNN has 98% accuracy and another has 96% accuracy, should the 98% model always be chosen?

**Answer.** The 98% model is better on correctness, but model choice depends on the job. If the 98% model needs far more spikes, more energy, or a longer simulation window, the 96% model may be better for a battery-powered sensor, a robot that needs fast reactions, or a neuromorphic chip designed for sparse activity. Accuracy tells you which model answers more often correctly; the other metrics tell you whether those answers are practical.

This becomes clearer when network size or task difficulty grows. On a simpler task, several models may appear close in accuracy. On a harder task such as CIFAR-10-scale image recognition, accuracy gaps can widen: conventional neural networks may reach around 92%, while direct SNN approaches may sit closer to 85%. That correctness gap is real and must be measured. At the same time, the energy gap can move in the opposite direction: surrogate-trained SNNs may operate at less than 10% of the energy cost of conventional networks. A single accuracy number cannot express that tradeoff. The model with the highest correctness may be too costly for a small device, while the model with lower correctness may enable real-time, low-power operation.

A common beginner confusion is to treat accuracy as the "final score" of a model. Accuracy is better understood as the correctness coordinate in a larger map. If two models have the same accuracy, the faster and lower-energy one may be preferable. If one model has slightly lower accuracy but uses dramatically less energy, it may be the better engineering choice. If an application is medical diagnosis or safety-critical control, even a small accuracy loss may be unacceptable. If the application is an always-on environmental sensor, energy savings may be decisive. The meaning of an accuracy number depends on the constraints around the task.

**Question.** What does 95% accuracy mean for an SNN making 1,000 predictions?

**Answer.** It means about 950 predictions were correct out of 1,000 total predictions. The formula is $\text{Accuracy} = 950 / 1000 = 0.95 = 95\%$. That tells you the network's correctness rate, but it does not tell you how many spikes were produced, how long inference took, or how much energy was consumed.

Accuracy gives SNN evaluation its first firm measurement: a count of correct answers divided by all attempted answers. From there, the evaluation becomes richer. A spiking model is valuable because it can use time and sparse events as part of computation, so judging it only by correctness leaves out much of what makes it useful. The right mental model is to picture accuracy as the vertical measure of "how right the answers are," while latency, energy, spike count, and convergence describe the path the network took to get those answers. A strong SNN is therefore measured by both the quality of its predictions and the efficiency of the spiking process that produced them.
