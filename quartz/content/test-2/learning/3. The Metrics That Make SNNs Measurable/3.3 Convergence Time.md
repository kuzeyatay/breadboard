### Convergence Time

After accuracy tells you how good a trained SNN can become and training loss shows how its errors shrink, a practical question remains: how long does the model take to become useful? Imagine training two spiking neural networks for the same gesture-recognition task on an edge device. Both eventually reach the same target accuracy, so a final accuracy number makes them look equal. During training, though, one reaches that useful level after 8 epochs, while the other needs 20 epochs. The first method gives you a usable model sooner, spends less training computation getting there, and gives engineers faster feedback when they adjust the architecture or learning rule.

An **epoch** is one full pass through the training data. If the training set contains recordings of many spike patterns, one epoch means the model has had one opportunity to learn from each training example. After each epoch, the model's **training accuracy** can be measured: the fraction of training examples it classifies correctly at that point. Training accuracy usually changes across epochs because the model's weights are being updated. Early epochs often show low accuracy because the network has not yet shaped its spike responses into useful patterns. Later epochs may improve quickly, improve slowly, or stop improving.

A training method needs a standard line that says, "this model is now good enough for the comparison we care about." That line is the **target accuracy**. The target accuracy is a chosen performance threshold, such as 90% or 95%, used to compare how quickly different methods reach the same useful level. Once that threshold is chosen, convergence time can be measured by asking for the earliest epoch where the model reaches it.

*Convergence time as minimum epoch reaching target accuracy* *(p. 6)*

The equation expresses convergence time as a **minimum epoch**: the first epoch index that satisfies a target condition. In words, convergence time is the smallest epoch number $e$ such that the model's accuracy at epoch $e$ is at least the target accuracy. A compact way to read the formula is:
$$
T_{\text{conv}} = \min \{ e \mid A(e) \ge A_{\text{target}} \}
$$
Here, $T_{\text{conv}}$ is the convergence time, measured in epochs. The symbol $e$ stands for an epoch number. The function $A(e)$ means the model's accuracy after epoch $e$. The value $A_{\text{target}}$ is the target accuracy chosen for the comparison. The symbol $\min$ means "choose the smallest value that works." The formula lets you compute the first moment in training when the model becomes accurate enough by the chosen standard.

A simple example makes the metric concrete. Suppose an SNN is trained for 20 epochs, and the target accuracy is 90%. Its measured accuracies after the first several epochs are 62%, 74%, 83%, 88%, 91%, and 92%. The first epoch where accuracy reaches or exceeds 90% is epoch 5, so the convergence time is 5 epochs. If another SNN reaches 90% only at epoch 14, then both models may eventually be useful, but the first one becomes useful much sooner.

Convergence time turns training into a time-based cost. Every extra epoch requires more passes through data, more weight updates, and more computation. In SNNs, that cost has a special meaning because training methods can behave very differently. Surrogate gradient-trained SNNs often reduce training loss more rapidly and can stabilize by around the 20th epoch. That means their learning trajectory reaches a useful region quickly, making them attractive when fast adaptation or repeated retraining is needed. STDP-based SNNs can be more energy-efficient during inference and can use fewer spikes, but they tend to converge more slowly and may plateau at higher loss values. Their learning can be attractive for low-power unsupervised adaptation, while their convergence behavior warns that reaching precise classification performance may require more patience.

A beginner may wonder why final accuracy alone cannot settle the comparison. Final accuracy answers, "How good did the model become by the end of the training run?" Convergence time answers, "How soon did the model become good enough?" Those are different engineering questions. If two models both reach 92% accuracy by epoch 20, but one crossed 90% at epoch 6 and the other crossed it at epoch 19, the earlier-converging model gives a faster route to useful performance. In a research setting, that can mean quicker experimentation. In an edge-learning setting, it can mean less training energy and faster deployment after conditions change.

**Question.** If two SNNs reach the same target accuracy, should they always be treated as equally good training methods?

**Answer.** No. If they reach the target at different epochs, the method with the smaller convergence time becomes useful sooner. That shorter path usually means lower training cost over time, because fewer full passes through the data are needed before the model reaches the chosen performance threshold.

Convergence time also helps interpret training loss. Loss measures how wrong the model's predictions are during training, while accuracy measures how many predictions are correct. A model whose loss drops quickly is often moving toward better predictions quickly, but the convergence-time metric ties that movement to a specific usable threshold. If loss stabilizes early and accuracy reaches the target early, the training method looks efficient. If loss decreases slowly or plateaus at a higher value, the model may need more epochs before it becomes useful, or it may struggle to reach the target at all.

The phrase **minimum epoch** carries the practical discipline of the metric. A model may reach the target accuracy, dip below it, and later recover. The convergence-time definition focuses on the first epoch that reaches the threshold. That first crossing is useful because it records how quickly the method can produce a model that meets the selected standard. In stricter evaluation setups, engineers may also check whether the model remains stable afterward, but the convergence-time value itself is the first successful crossing.

**Question.** What happens if an SNN never reaches the target accuracy during the training run?

**Answer.** Its convergence time is undefined for that target within the observed training window. If training was run for 20 epochs and the model never reached the target accuracy, the correct interpretation is that it did not converge to the required level within 20 epochs. That result is still informative because it shows that the method either needs more training time, a different target, or a different training strategy.

Convergence time gives SNN evaluation a sense of motion. Accuracy is a destination, loss is the shape of the learning path, and convergence time marks the first point where the path reaches useful performance. When comparing surrogate gradient training, converted SNNs, and STDP-based learning, this metric keeps the comparison fair: each method is judged by how quickly it reaches the same chosen standard. A strong mental model is to picture training as a curve climbing toward usefulness across epochs. The target accuracy is a horizontal line across that curve, and convergence time is the earliest place where the curve touches or crosses that line.
