### Convergence Time and Learning Curves

After accuracy, loss, latency, energy, and spike count are on the table, a practical question remains: how long does a spiking model take to become useful during training? Imagine training three SNNs for the same classification task. At the end, two of them may reach similar accuracy, but one may become reliable after only a few training epochs while another may still be slowly improving many epochs later. If the final accuracy is the only number recorded, those two models can look almost equally good even though their training behavior feels very different to the person trying to build, tune, and deploy the system.

A training epoch is one full pass through the training data. After each epoch, the model has had one more opportunity to adjust its internal parameters. Accuracy tells us the fraction of examples the model classifies correctly, while training loss measures how costly the model's mistakes are under the chosen learning objective. Loss is often more sensitive than accuracy early in training: a model can still classify many examples incorrectly while already making less confident or less severe mistakes. That is why convergence is usually understood by watching a curve over time rather than by checking a single final score.

Convergence time turns that moving behavior into a measurable quantity. To define it, choose a target accuracy, meaning the level of training accuracy that counts as "good enough" for the task. Then watch the training accuracy after each epoch and ask when the model first reaches that target. The metric is
$$
T_{\mathrm{conv}} = \min \{ e \mid A_{\mathrm{train}}(e) \geq A_{\mathrm{target}} \}
$$
Here, $T_{\mathrm{conv}}$ is the convergence time, measured in epochs. The symbol $e$ is an epoch number. The expression $A_{\mathrm{train}}(e)$ means the training accuracy after epoch $e$. The symbol $A_{\mathrm{target}}$ is the target accuracy chosen before making the comparison. The word $\min$ means "the smallest value that satisfies the condition," so the formula asks for the earliest epoch where the training accuracy is at least the target.

A concrete interpretation makes the formula easier to use. Suppose the target accuracy is 90%. If a surrogate-gradient SNN reaches 90% training accuracy at epoch 12, while a converted SNN reaches it at epoch 16 and an STDP-based SNN has not reached it by epoch 20, then their convergence behavior differs even before discussing their final energy use or spike counts. The surrogate-gradient model has the shortest convergence time for that target. The converted model is slower for the same target. The STDP-based model may still be attractive for low-energy or unsupervised settings, but its learning curve says it takes longer to reach that particular supervised accuracy threshold.

**Question.** If two models both reach 95% accuracy by the end of training, why compare convergence time at all?

**Answer.** Final accuracy tells you where the models end up, while convergence time tells you how quickly they become useful. A model that reaches a target accuracy in fewer epochs is easier to train to a working level, can require less tuning time, and may be better suited when rapid adaptation matters.

Training loss curves show the same story from the error side. A curve that falls steeply over early epochs means the model is rapidly reducing its mistakes. A curve that flattens high means the model is still making relatively costly errors even after repeated passes through the data. The training loss comparison below shows how different SNN learning approaches behave as training proceeds.

![Convergence behavior of SNN models training loss across epochs](/test-2/assets/source-visuals/2510-27379v1-page-10-graph-g1-convergence-behavior-of-snn-models-training-loss.png)

*Convergence behavior of SNN models training loss across epochs* *(p. 10)*

The downward direction of each curve represents improvement because lower training loss means the model's predictions are becoming less costly under the training objective. The surrogate-gradient SNN drops loss more rapidly and stabilizes by around the 20th epoch, which indicates fast optimization behavior. The converted SNN also improves, but its loss decreases more slowly, consistent with a method that depends on translating a trained artificial neural network into spiking behavior and may need a longer simulation window to approximate continuous activations. The STDP-based SNN reduces loss more gradually and plateaus at a higher loss value, showing slower convergence under this supervised-loss view.

Each row is tied to an epoch, so the table records the state of training after repeated passes through the data. Each model type has its own training-loss values, allowing the learning methods to be compared at the same epoch rather than only at the end. Reading across a row answers, "Which model has the lower loss at this training point?" Reading down a column answers, "How quickly is this model reducing loss as training continues?" Those two readings are different but complementary: one compares models at a fixed time, while the other tracks the improvement rate of a single model.

Learning curves shift attention from loss to accuracy. Instead of asking how much error remains, they ask how often the model is correct after each epoch. This makes convergence time visually natural: draw a horizontal target accuracy line, then find where each model's accuracy curve first crosses it.

The steepness of a learning curve shows how quickly useful accuracy is being gained. A curve that climbs quickly during the early epochs has short convergence time for moderate targets because it reaches those thresholds sooner. A curve that rises slowly can still improve, but it delays the epoch at which the model becomes accurate enough for the chosen goal. The surrogate-gradient curve reaches useful accuracy sooner, matching the faster loss reduction seen in the training-loss graph. The converted SNN follows with competitive performance but slower progress. The STDP-based curve rises more gradually, reinforcing the idea that its strengths are better understood alongside low spike count and low energy rather than only through fast supervised convergence.

A common beginner confusion is to treat convergence time as a built-in property of a model, as if each method has one fixed number attached to it. The target accuracy is part of the measurement. If the target is modest, several methods may reach it quickly. If the target is high, the measured convergence time can spread apart sharply, and a method that looked acceptable under a lower threshold may no longer reach the target within the observed training window. Convergence time therefore depends on both the learning curve and the chosen target.

**Question.** Can a model with slower convergence still be a good choice?

**Answer.** Yes. Convergence time measures training speed toward a target accuracy, while deployment cost depends on other metrics such as latency, energy, and spike count. An STDP-based SNN can converge more slowly under a supervised accuracy target while still being valuable when sparse spiking and low energy are the priority.

This separation is what makes convergence time useful in SNN evaluation. Accuracy describes how well the trained model performs. Latency describes how quickly it responds during inference. Energy and spike count describe how expensive the response is to produce. Convergence time describes how quickly training reaches a usable level. Those metrics answer different questions, so a model can be strong on one and weaker on another. Surrogate-gradient SNNs often occupy a balanced position because they can approach ANN-like accuracy, keep latency low, and converge faster by the observed training horizon. Converted SNNs can retain competitive accuracy but may require more spikes and longer simulation behavior. STDP-based SNNs can be highly efficient in energy and spiking activity while converging more slowly and reaching lower supervised accuracy.

The mental model is a race toward a chosen line, watched through two instruments at once. The loss curve shows how quickly the model's mistakes become less costly. The learning curve shows how quickly correct predictions accumulate. Convergence time marks the first epoch where the accuracy curve reaches the target line. Once those pieces are connected, "how quickly does this SNN learn?" becomes a measurable question rather than a vague impression.
