### Energy Efficiency and Convergence Time

Imagine two SNNs that both recognize the same input correctly most of the time. One reaches that accuracy by firing many spikes over a long simulation window and consuming more energy. The other reaches nearly the same accuracy with fewer spikes, less computation, and fewer training epochs. If accuracy is the only number being watched, these two systems can look almost equal. Once energy and training speed enter the picture, they become very different choices.

Accuracy tells us how often the model's predictions are correct. Energy consumption tells us how much computational cost the model spends to produce those predictions. In an SNN, that cost is closely tied to spike-based activity: when neurons communicate through discrete spike events, computation is concentrated around the moments when spikes occur. A quieter network can often spend less energy because fewer spike events need to be transmitted and processed. This is why energy must be measured beside accuracy. A model that is slightly less accurate may still be more useful in an energy-limited setting if it spends far less energy per inference.

To make that tradeoff measurable, we combine accuracy and energy into a single ratio called **normalized energy efficiency**. The idea is simple: ask how much predictive performance the model delivers for each unit of energy it consumes.
$$
\eta = \frac{A}{E}
$$
Here, $\eta$ is the normalized energy efficiency, $A$ is the model's accuracy, and $E$ is the energy consumption. If accuracy is written as a percentage, such as $97.8$, and energy is measured in millijoules per inference, then $\eta$ tells us how many percentage-points of accuracy are obtained per millijoule. If accuracy is written as a decimal, such as $0.978$, then the ratio has the same meaning but a different numerical scale. The formula lets us compare models by asking how much useful predictive behavior is obtained for each unit of energy spent.

Consider a surrogate-gradient SNN with accuracy around $97.8\%$ and energy consumption around $15$ mJ per inference. Its normalized energy efficiency, using percentage accuracy, is
$$
\eta = \frac{97.8}{15} \approx 6.52
$$
That means the model delivers about $6.52$ percentage-points of accuracy per millijoule. Now consider an STDP-based SNN with accuracy around $95.5\%$ and energy consumption around $5$ mJ per inference:
$$
\eta = \frac{95.5}{5} = 19.1
$$
The STDP-based model has lower raw accuracy, yet it delivers much more accuracy per millijoule. This is the kind of relationship accuracy alone hides. The surrogate-gradient model is attractive when high accuracy and low latency are the priority. The STDP-based model is attractive when energy is the tighter constraint. Normalized energy efficiency makes that behavior visible as a measurable quantity rather than a vague impression.

A common beginner confusion is to treat "more accurate" as automatically "better." A better mental picture is to imagine paying for each prediction with a small energy budget. If two models both perform well, the one that spends much less energy may be the stronger design under that budget. The word "normalized" signals that the comparison has been scaled by energy consumption, so the metric rewards useful output per unit cost rather than useful output alone.

**Question.** If Model A has $98\%$ accuracy and uses $20$ mJ per inference, while Model B has $96\%$ accuracy and uses $8$ mJ per inference, which has higher normalized energy efficiency?

**Answer.** Model A has $\eta = 98 / 20 = 4.9$ percentage-points per mJ. Model B has $\eta = 96 / 8 = 12$ percentage-points per mJ. Model B is more energy efficient because it delivers more accuracy for each millijoule consumed, even though its raw accuracy is lower.

Energy efficiency measures how costly inference is after a model is being used. Training behavior needs another measurement because two models can end with similar accuracy after very different learning journeys. One model may reach useful accuracy quickly. Another may require many epochs before it becomes reliable. An **epoch** is one full pass through the training data. If training accuracy is checked after each epoch, the learning process becomes a curve over time: low accuracy at the beginning, then rising accuracy as the model adjusts.

To measure learning speed, we define a **target accuracy threshold**. This is the level of accuracy we decide is "good enough" for the task, such as $95\%$. Then we ask for the first epoch at which the model reaches that threshold. That first successful epoch is the **convergence time**.
$$
T_c = \min \{t : A(t) \geq A_{\text{target}}\}
$$
Here, $T_c$ is the convergence time, $t$ is an epoch number, $A(t)$ is the accuracy measured at epoch $t$, and $A_{\text{target}}$ is the target accuracy threshold. The expression $\min$ means "choose the smallest epoch number that satisfies the condition." The condition $A(t) \geq A_{\text{target}}$ means the model's accuracy at that epoch has reached or exceeded the target.

Suppose the target accuracy is $95\%$. A model's accuracy over epochs might be $80\%$, $89\%$, $93\%$, $95.2\%$, and $96\%$. The first epoch that reaches at least $95\%$ is epoch $4$, so $T_c = 4$. If another model reaches $95\%$ only at epoch $12$, both models may eventually become usable, but the first model learns faster by this metric. Convergence time answers a different question from final accuracy: it asks how quickly the model becomes useful.

This matters especially when comparing SNN training approaches. Surrogate-gradient SNNs can achieve high accuracy, around $97.8\%$, with low latency around $10$ ms and moderate energy around $15$ mJ per inference. STDP-based SNNs can reach very low energy consumption, around $5$ mJ per inference, with sparse spiking activity, but their convergence can be slower and their accuracy can be somewhat lower, around $95.5\%$. Converted SNNs can be competitive in accuracy, but they may require higher spike counts and longer simulation windows. These observations are easier to reason about when each performance axis has its own measurement: accuracy for correctness, energy for cost, normalized energy efficiency for usefulness per energy spent, and convergence time for learning speed.

**Question.** A model has accuracies $70\%, 84\%, 91\%, 94\%, 96\%$ over epochs $1$ through $5$. If the target accuracy is $95\%$, what is its convergence time?

**Answer.** The first epoch where accuracy reaches or exceeds $95\%$ is epoch $5$, because $96\%$ is the first value above the target. Therefore $T_c = 5$.

The two metrics also work together. Normalized energy efficiency focuses on the cost of producing accurate predictions. Convergence time focuses on how many training epochs are needed before the model reaches a chosen target. A model with high final accuracy, high energy consumption, and slow convergence may be impressive in one dimension while expensive in others. A model with slightly lower accuracy, very low energy consumption, and acceptable convergence may be a stronger fit when energy and training speed are constrained.

A useful mental model is to treat an SNN as a learner that must spend two kinds of resources. During training, it spends epochs to become accurate; convergence time measures how quickly that spending reaches a target. During inference, it spends energy to make predictions; normalized energy efficiency measures how much accuracy is obtained for that spending. Accuracy remains essential, but these metrics turn accuracy into part of a larger measurement system. They reveal whether a model is merely correct, or correct in a way that is fast enough to learn and efficient enough to run.
