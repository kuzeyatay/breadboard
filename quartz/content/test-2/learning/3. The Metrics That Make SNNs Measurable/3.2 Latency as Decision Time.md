## Latency as Decision Time

Imagine a small vision system on a drone that has just seen a hand gesture. The camera records the change, the model begins processing, and the drone must decide whether to turn, hover, or land. Accuracy tells whether the chosen label is correct, and energy tells how costly the computation is, but a real-time system also needs to know when the answer arrives. A correct decision that arrives too late can behave like a wrong decision in practice, because the world has already moved on. Latency is the metric that turns this timing problem into something measurable.

Latency begins with a simple timeline. At one point, a stimulus appears: a gesture starts, a sound begins, or a sensor detects an event. This moment is the **stimulus onset time**, the time when the input first becomes available to the system. Later, the model produces a usable output, such as a class label or action choice. This later moment is the **decision time**, the time when the system has enough evidence to commit to an answer. **Latency** is the elapsed time between those two moments.
$$
\text{Latency} = t_{\text{decision}} - t_{\text{onset}}
$$
Here, $\text{Latency}$ is the time the model takes to respond, usually measured in milliseconds for fast inference tasks. $t_{\text{onset}}$ is the stimulus onset time, the clock time when the input begins. $t_{\text{decision}}$ is the decision time, the clock time when the model's output is ready to use. The formula lets you compute response time by subtracting the start of the input from the moment of decision. If a gesture begins at $0$ ms and the model produces its class label at $10$ ms, then the latency is $10 - 0 = 10$ ms. If the same model waits until $20$ ms to decide, its latency doubles, even if the final label remains correct.

A spiking neural network makes this metric especially natural because time is already part of the computation. A spike is an event that occurs at a particular moment. When information is carried by spike timing and spike patterns, the model's answer can emerge as soon as enough events have arrived. The system can often decide before a long fixed window has finished, which is why latency has to be measured directly rather than inferred from accuracy. Two models can reach similar accuracy while requiring different amounts of time to collect evidence.

A beginner may wonder whether latency is simply another name for computational speed. It is more specific than that. Speed can describe many things: arithmetic operations per second, hardware clock rate, or total throughput across many samples. Latency follows one sample from the instant its stimulus begins to the instant its decision is available. For real-time inference, this single-sample response time is the quantity that determines whether the system can react while the input still matters.

**Question.** If two models both classify a gesture correctly, why compare their latency?

**Answer.** Correctness and response time answer different questions. Accuracy asks whether the chosen label matches the target. Latency asks how long the system needed before that label became available. A gesture recognizer, hearing aid, robot controller, or event-based sensor system needs both: the decision should be reliable, and it should arrive quickly enough to guide action.

The table gives the numerical comparison: a CNN-style ANN typically falls in the 20-50 ms range, a converted SNN is around 20 ms, a surrogate-gradient SNN is around 10 ms, and an STDP-based SNN can stabilize below 15 ms under optimized conditions. These values make the timing tradeoff concrete. The ANN can be strong in raw classification performance, but its continuous-valued processing can require a longer response window. A converted SNN inherits behavior from an ANN and often needs a longer simulation window so spike rates can approximate the original activations. A surrogate-gradient SNN is trained directly with spiking behavior in mind, so it can produce low-latency responses around 10 ms while keeping competitive accuracy. An STDP-based SNN adapts through spike-timing relationships and can be very efficient, though its early inference behavior can be slightly slower before the response stabilizes.

A useful way to picture latency is to imagine listening for a word in a noisy room. You do not need to wait forever before deciding what word was spoken; you wait until enough sound evidence has arrived. A model behaves similarly during inference. It observes incoming information, accumulates evidence internally, and reaches a decision when the evidence is sufficient. In an SNN, that evidence often arrives as spikes over time. A shorter latency means the model needed less elapsed time to reach a usable decision. A longer latency means the model needed a longer observation or simulation period before committing.

Real-time inference depends on this timing boundary. In an offline setting, a model can process stored data after the fact, and a delay of tens of milliseconds may be acceptable. In an interactive or embodied setting, the model's output is part of a feedback loop. A sensor detects a change, the network responds, and an action follows. Latency measures whether that loop closes quickly enough. A 10 ms decision can support fast reaction; a 50 ms decision may still be useful for many tasks, but it changes what kinds of control and interaction are possible.

**Question.** Why can a converted SNN have higher latency than a directly trained surrogate-gradient SNN?

**Answer.** A converted SNN often represents ANN-like activation strength through spike rates, so it may need a longer simulation window to collect enough spikes for a stable approximation. A surrogate-gradient SNN is trained directly to use spiking activity during learning, so its spike patterns can support earlier decisions, giving it lower latency in the comparison.

Latency makes SNN behavior measurable by attaching the model's decision to a clock. The stimulus onset starts the clock, the decision stops it, and the difference tells how long inference took for that sample. Once latency is measured this way, model choice becomes clearer: a designer can ask whether the system needs the fastest response, the lowest energy, the highest accuracy, or a balance among them. The mental model is a timeline with evidence flowing through it. A stimulus begins, spikes or activations carry information forward, the model gathers enough evidence, and the decision arrives. Latency is the length of that journey from input onset to usable output.
