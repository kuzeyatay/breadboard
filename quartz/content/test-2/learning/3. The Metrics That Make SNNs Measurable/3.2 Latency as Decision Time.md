## Latency as Decision Time

After measuring whether an SNN gives the right answer and how much energy it spends to get there, the next question is whether the answer arrives soon enough to be useful. Imagine a small vision sensor on a robot arm watching for a hand entering its workspace. The scene may be quiet for most of the time, then a sudden movement appears. The robot does not need a beautiful answer five seconds later; it needs a timely answer while the hand is still near the arm. In that situation, the model's behavior has a clock attached to it. A prediction becomes useful only when it arrives within the time allowed by the task.

A spiking neural network is naturally suited to this kind of timing question because its computation unfolds through events. A stimulus begins, spikes are produced across neurons, information moves through the network, and at some later moment the network has enough evidence to make a decision. The metric that measures this elapsed response time is **latency**. Latency tells us how long inference takes after the input begins. It turns "the model responded quickly" into something measurable.

To define latency, start with two moments on a timeline. The **stimulus onset time** is the moment the input begins. For a camera-like event sensor, this could be the instant a gesture starts producing events. For a classification task, it could be the moment the input sample is presented to the network. The **decision time** is the moment the SNN produces the output that will be treated as its answer. Depending on the setup, that answer might come from the first output neuron to spike, from the strongest accumulated output activity, or from a readout taken after enough spikes have arrived.

Once those two times are known, latency is the distance between them:
$$
\text{Latency} = t_{\text{decision}} - t_{\text{onset}}
$$
Here, $\text{Latency}$ is the elapsed response time for one inference, $t_{\text{decision}}$ is the time when the model's answer is available, and $t_{\text{onset}}$ is the time when the stimulus begins. The formula lets you compute how long the network took to respond after the input started.

Suppose a gesture begins at $t_{\text{onset}} = 120$ milliseconds, and the SNN produces its decision at $t_{\text{decision}} = 128$ milliseconds. The latency is
$$
128\text{ ms} - 120\text{ ms} = 8\text{ ms}
$$
That 8 milliseconds is the model's response delay for that input. It is the time from the beginning of the stimulus to the usable decision. It is separate from how long the model took to train, how many epochs were used during learning, or how long the whole experiment ran. Latency belongs to inference: one stimulus arrives, the model processes it, and a decision appears.

This distinction becomes easier to feel if you picture a stopwatch. The stopwatch starts when the input appears. It stops when the model's output can be used. Everything before the input appears belongs outside the measurement, and everything after the decision is already too late to change that inference. Latency is the interval inside that stopwatch window.

**Question.** If an SNN is trained for many epochs, does that make its latency larger?

**Answer.** Training time and latency measure different parts of the system. Training time describes how long the model takes to learn its parameters. Latency describes how long a trained model takes to make one decision after a stimulus begins. A model could take a long time to train and still respond quickly during inference, or it could train efficiently but require a long simulation window before it can decide.

Latency becomes especially meaningful in SNNs because spikes arrive over time. A dense continuous-valued network often processes a full array of activations through each layer for a given input. A spiking network can use sparse events, so the timing of those events affects when enough evidence has reached the output. If the output neurons reach a decision after only a short burst of spikes, the latency is short. If the network must wait through a longer simulation window to collect enough spike activity, the latency grows.

For real-time inference, short latency can matter more than peak accuracy. Consider two gesture-recognition models for a small robot. One model reaches slightly higher accuracy but waits 50 milliseconds before deciding. Another model is a little less accurate but usually decides within 10 milliseconds. If the robot must react while the motion is still happening, the faster model may be the better tool. The useful question becomes: which model is accurate enough and fast enough for the physical situation?

Reported SNN behavior shows why this metric belongs beside accuracy and energy. Surrogate-trained SNNs can approach ANN accuracy within a small margin while producing decisions with latency as low as about 10 milliseconds. In event-based gesture-recognition settings, surrogate SNNs have been reported around 5-10 milliseconds of inference latency, while ANN counterparts fall closer to 20-50 milliseconds. The shape of that comparison is practical: the SNN response window is compressed. A decision that arrives in 5-10 milliseconds leaves more time for a robot, sensor, or edge device to act on it.

The reason this helps edge systems is that edge devices live near the data. A camera on a drone, a sensor in a wearable, or a controller on a robot cannot always send information to a large remote server and wait. The device often has limited power, limited memory, and a tight response deadline. Latency measures whether the SNN's event-driven computation can meet that deadline. When latency is low, real-time inference becomes more plausible because the model's answer arrives while the input is still relevant.

A beginner confusion often comes from treating latency as if it were the same as "speed" in a vague sense. Latency is more precise. It is measured from a defined starting point to a defined ending point. If those points are unclear, the number becomes hard to interpret. Saying "the model is fast" gives an impression. Saying "the model reaches a decision 10 milliseconds after stimulus onset" gives a measurable behavior that can be compared across methods and matched to an application's timing needs.

**Question.** Why can a model with slightly lower accuracy be preferable if its latency is much shorter?

**Answer.** A real-time system needs a decision before the useful action window closes. If a robot, sensor, or edge device must respond immediately, a slightly less accurate answer that arrives in time can be more valuable than a more accurate answer that arrives too late. Latency measures that timing constraint directly, so it helps choose models for tasks where response time is part of success.

Latency also interacts with spike count and energy, even though each metric answers a different question. A shorter response can mean the network needs fewer time steps before making a decision. Fewer active time steps can reduce the amount of spiking computation required for that inference. That is why latency often appears in the same evaluation set as accuracy, energy consumption, spike count, and convergence behavior. Together, these metrics describe whether an SNN is correct, efficient, quiet, trainable, and fast enough.

The mental model is a timeline with a task deadline. The stimulus begins, the SNN reacts through spikes, the output accumulates enough evidence, and the decision appears. Latency is the measured gap between the beginning and the decision. Once that gap is visible, SNN performance becomes easier to reason about: a model is useful for real-time inference when its decisions arrive within the time window demanded by the world it is sensing and controlling.
