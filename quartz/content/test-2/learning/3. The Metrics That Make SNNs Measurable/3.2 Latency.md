## Latency

Imagine a small vision system watching a hand gesture so a robot can respond. The stimulus begins when the camera or event sensor receives the gesture, and the decision arrives when the network has produced enough activity to choose a class such as "swipe left" or "swipe right." Earlier metrics can tell us whether the chosen class is correct and how much energy the network spends, but a real robot also needs to know how long it waits before acting. A perfect answer that arrives too late can miss the moment it was meant to guide. Latency measures that waiting time.

A spiking neural network makes this timing question especially natural because its information arrives as events spread through time. A conventional network often processes a fixed block of numbers and returns an output after the computation is complete. A spiking network may receive a stimulus, accumulate spikes, update neuron states, and gradually build evidence for a decision. Silence, early spikes, and later spikes all belong to the computation. That means the model's behavior has a time shape: a decision can appear after a short burst of evidence, or it can require a longer simulation window before the output becomes clear. Latency turns that time shape into a measurable cost.

The measurement begins with two moments. The **stimulus time** is the moment the input is presented to the network. If a gesture-recognition sensor begins receiving the gesture at time $t_\text{stimulus}$, that time marks the start of the clock. The **decision time** is the moment the model produces its decision, written as $t_\text{decision}$. In a classifier, this could be the first time one output class reaches a chosen confidence threshold, the time when an output neuron spikes decisively, or the end of a simulation window where the largest output response is read. The exact rule must be fixed before comparing models, because different decision rules can change the measured waiting time.

Once those two times are defined, latency is the elapsed time between them:
$$
\text{Latency} = t_\text{decision} - t_\text{stimulus}
$$
Here, $\text{Latency}$ is the time cost of producing the decision, $t_\text{decision}$ is the time at which the network's output is treated as its answer, and $t_\text{stimulus}$ is the time at which the input begins. The subtraction gives the duration between input arrival and usable output. If a gesture appears at $0$ milliseconds and the network commits to a class at $8$ milliseconds, then the latency is $8 - 0 = 8$ milliseconds. If another model reaches the same kind of decision at $35$ milliseconds, its latency is $35$ milliseconds under the same measurement rule.

This simple formula does more than attach a number to speed. It separates the quality of the answer from the time cost of obtaining it. Two SNNs may reach similar classification performance, yet one may need many more simulation steps before its output stabilizes. In that case, accuracy alone would make them look similar, while latency reveals that one model is better suited to situations where response time is part of the task. A low-latency model can make a timely decision from fewer milliseconds of spiking activity; a high-latency model asks the system to wait longer before acting.

A concrete comparison makes the idea easier to feel. Suppose two gesture-recognition systems are placed on a robot arm. The first system usually decides within $5$ to $10$ ms. The second system usually takes $20$ to $50$ ms. Both times may sound short to a human reader, but for a machine coordinating motion, sensing, and control, the difference changes what kinds of actions are feasible. A $5$ to $10$ ms inference can fit into a tight real-time loop, where the robot updates its response almost immediately as the world changes. A $20$ to $50$ ms inference creates a longer pause between perception and action. Latency is the metric that makes this pause visible.

**Question.** If a spiking network starts receiving a stimulus at $12$ ms and produces its decision at $27$ ms, what is its latency?

**Answer.** The latency is $27\text{ ms} - 12\text{ ms} = 15\text{ ms}$. This means the network needed 15 milliseconds from the start of the stimulus to the moment its decision became available.

Latency depends on decision timing, so the definition of "decision" carries real weight. In an SNN, spikes can arrive early, late, or sparsely. A network may show a weak early preference for one class and a stronger later preference after more spikes have accumulated. If the decision rule accepts the first confident output spike, latency may be low. If the rule waits until the full simulation window finishes, latency may be higher. The metric therefore measures the behavior of the model together with the chosen readout rule. For fair comparison, each model must be judged using a consistent timing convention.

This is where latency becomes part of real-time performance. A system is real-time when its answers arrive quickly enough to be useful inside the environment where it operates. The requirement comes from the task. A slow offline analysis tool may tolerate long inference windows because the answer can arrive after the event. A robot, a sensor-driven controller, or an edge device reacting to live input needs the decision while the situation is still current. In that setting, latency measures whether the network's temporal computation fits the pace of the world around it.

A beginner may wonder whether lower latency always means a better SNN. The better mental picture is a tradeoff among timing, accuracy, energy, and spike activity. A model that decides extremely early may sometimes act before enough evidence has arrived. A model that waits longer may gather more spikes and stabilize its output, but the extra waiting can reduce usefulness in real-time settings. Surrogate-gradient-trained SNNs are often valued because they can preserve strong accuracy while reaching low latencies, with reported low-latency behavior around 10 ms and gesture-recognition inference around 5-10 ms in neuromorphic settings. Converted SNNs can remain competitive, but they may require longer simulation windows, which raises the time before the decision is ready.

**Question.** Why can two models with similar accuracy have very different usefulness in a real-time application?

**Answer.** Accuracy says how often the decision is right, while latency says how long the system waits for that decision. If two models are similarly accurate but one decides in 10 ms and the other in 50 ms, the 10 ms model can fit faster perception-action loops and may be more practical for live control.

Latency also helps keep deployment cost separate from model quality. A model can look strong when judged only by final answers, yet still demand too much waiting time for an edge or robotics use case. By measuring latency alongside accuracy, energy consumption, spike count, and convergence behavior, SNN evaluation treats a decision as an event with a cost in time. That view fits the nature of spiking computation: the network uses temporal signals, so the moment when enough evidence becomes available is part of what the model has learned to do.

The useful mental model is a stopwatch attached to the input-output path. The clock starts when the stimulus enters, keeps running while spikes carry and accumulate evidence, and stops when the network's decision is accepted. The resulting duration is latency. Once that duration is measured, an SNN can be judged as a working temporal system rather than only as a final label generator. Low latency means the network turns incoming spikes into usable decisions quickly enough for time-sensitive action; higher latency means the network spends more time collecting or propagating evidence before it can answer.
