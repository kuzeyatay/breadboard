## The Leaky Integrate-and-Fire Neuron

Imagine a spiking neuron receiving a stream of input spikes from earlier neurons in a network. Each incoming spike is a tiny event: it arrives at a particular moment, nudges the receiving neuron, and then disappears. The neuron needs a way to remember recent nudges long enough for several of them to combine, while still letting old nudges fade so that a distant event does not control the neuron forever. That temporary internal "readiness to fire" is called **membrane potential**. You can think of it as a running level inside the neuron: inputs raise it, time pulls it back down, and crossing a chosen level turns the changing internal state into a visible spike.

A single input spike usually should not force the neuron to fire by itself. In a useful spiking system, the neuron responds when enough evidence has arrived close enough together in time. If a sensor is watching an edge move across an image, for example, one small signal might be noise, but several signals arriving in a short window suggest that something meaningful is happening. The membrane potential gives the neuron that short memory. Each input contributes to the internal level, so nearby inputs can accumulate. This accumulation is called **integration**, because the neuron is combining incoming effects across time.

The word **leaky** describes the other half of the behavior. The membrane potential gradually decays when input stops or arrives too slowly. The leak prevents the neuron from treating all past inputs as equally fresh. A useful analogy is a bucket with a small hole in it. Drops of water are incoming spikes. The water level is membrane potential. A burst of drops can raise the level quickly, but slow drops may leak away between arrivals. The bucket reaches a marked line only when enough drops arrive close together. In the neuron, that marked line is the **firing threshold**, the level of membrane potential required to emit a spike.

The Leaky Integrate-and-Fire model, often shortened to **LIF**, keeps this decision rule deliberately simple. It tracks membrane potential over time. Incoming synaptic inputs push the potential upward. Leakage pulls it downward. When the potential reaches the threshold, the neuron fires one spike. Immediately after firing, the model sets the membrane potential back to a lower value called the **reset** level. The reset gives the neuron a fresh starting point after it has already signaled that the recent input was strong enough.

The shape of the membrane potential over time makes the decision rule easier to see.

*LIF neuron membrane potential model with threshold over time* *(p. 4)*

The rising parts of the graph show integration: input current pushes the membrane potential upward as evidence accumulates. The horizontal threshold line marks the firing condition. A spike appears only when the rising membrane potential reaches that line. After the spike, the sharp drop shows reset: the neuron has sent its event, so its internal level is returned to a lower value before it begins accumulating again. The curved or sloped behavior between rises reflects leakage across time. If the input is too weak or too spread out, the potential drifts away from the threshold rather than climbing indefinitely.

This graph also shows how timing becomes part of the representation. The neuron is not only asking whether input exists. It is asking when enough input arrives close enough together to cross the threshold. Two neurons could receive the same number of input spikes, but the one receiving them in a tight burst may fire earlier, while the one receiving them slowly may fire later or remain silent. The output spike therefore carries information through its occurrence and its timing. A spike at an early moment can mean that the neuron reached certainty quickly; a later spike can mean that the evidence accumulated more gradually.

A beginner often imagines the threshold as a simple on/off switch attached directly to input. The better picture is an internal level that changes before the switch is reached. The input does not become the output immediately. It first changes the membrane potential. The leak continuously shapes that potential. The threshold then converts the changing internal voltage into a discrete event. This conversion is the bridge between two kinds of representation: a continuously changing membrane potential inside the neuron and a spike train made of distinct events outside the neuron.

Consider a neuron with a low leak receiving frequent input spikes. Each input raises the membrane potential, and little is lost between arrivals, so the graph climbs quickly toward the threshold. The neuron fires soon, resets, and may fire again if the input continues. Now imagine the same input arriving with a stronger leak. Each rise is followed by more decay, so the potential may hover below threshold. The neuron is still receiving signals, but the signals are not accumulating strongly enough in time to produce a spike. This is why leak controls sensitivity to temporal patterns. It makes recent input matter more than older input.

The threshold controls how much accumulated evidence is needed before the neuron speaks. A lower threshold makes firing easier because fewer or weaker inputs can push the membrane potential across the line. A higher threshold makes firing more selective because the neuron waits for stronger or more tightly timed input. The reset level controls what happens after the spike. A deeper reset means the neuron must rebuild more membrane potential before the next spike. A reset closer to threshold makes repeated firing easier when input remains strong.

**Question.** If a neuron receives many small inputs, why might it still fail to spike?

**Answer.** The inputs may arrive too slowly or be too weak relative to the leak and threshold. Each input raises the membrane potential, but leakage lowers it over time. If the potential loses too much between inputs, the accumulated level never reaches the firing threshold, so the neuron remains silent.

The LIF neuron is useful because it reduces spike generation to a small chain of causes that can be reasoned about directly. Input spikes raise membrane potential. Leak makes that potential fade with time. A threshold defines the level needed for a spike. The spike is the neuron's discrete output event. Reset returns the neuron to a ready state so the process can continue. This simple cycle lets a network process information sparsely: neurons fire mainly when the incoming pattern is strong enough in time, instead of continuously sending values at every moment.

**Question.** What exactly turns the neuron's changing membrane potential into a spike?

**Answer.** The firing threshold performs that conversion. The membrane potential can rise and fall continuously as inputs arrive and leakage acts. When that potential reaches the threshold, the neuron emits a discrete spike and then resets its membrane potential, creating a clear event from a gradually changing internal state.

A good mental model is a leaky accumulator with a decision line. The neuron listens to incoming spikes, stores their recent influence as membrane potential, forgets older influence through leakage, and fires when the stored influence crosses threshold. After firing, reset clears the accumulated level so the next spike depends on the next pattern of input. In this way, a single spiking neuron decides when to emit a spike by turning the timing and strength of incoming events into a rising-and-leaking internal voltage, then marking the moment that voltage becomes strong enough to matter.
