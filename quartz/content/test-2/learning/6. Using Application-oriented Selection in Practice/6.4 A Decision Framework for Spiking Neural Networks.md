## A Decision Framework for Spiking Neural Networks

Once you understand LIF neurons, competitive architectures, the three main training paradigms, and multi-metric evaluation, the practical question becomes concrete: which kind of spiking neural network fits a particular application? Imagine choosing a model for a battery-powered gesture sensor. The system must recognize movements quickly, consume little energy, and maintain useful accuracy. A model with the highest benchmark accuracy may drain the battery too rapidly, while the lowest-energy model may sacrifice more accuracy than the application can tolerate. A defensible choice therefore begins with deployment priorities and traces them backward through measurable performance, training strategy, network activity, and spike-based processing.

Begin by describing the application as a set of requirements. Ask how accurate the prediction must be, how quickly it must arrive, how much energy one inference may consume, and whether the system can generate many spikes without exceeding its processing budget. Training behavior also belongs in this requirement set. A model that converges quickly may be valuable when labeled data and training time are available, while a learning rule that adapts gradually from local spike timing may suit an unsupervised setting. These requirements create an application profile: an ordered statement of which outcomes are essential, which are desirable, and which tradeoffs are acceptable.

The order matters because SNN selection is inherently multi-metric. Accuracy measures how often the model produces the correct prediction. Latency measures how long it takes to produce that prediction. Energy measures the consumption associated with an inference. Spike count records how many discrete events the network generates per inference, providing an indication of activity and communication load. Convergence describes how training performance develops over time. Each metric answers a different deployment question, so the application profile determines how much influence each metric receives.

Consider a real-time recognition system that must respond in roughly 10 milliseconds. Latency becomes a hard requirement: a model that exceeds the response window cannot complete the intended task reliably, regardless of its strength on another metric. Within the models that satisfy that response window, accuracy and energy can then separate the candidates. Benchmark results on event-based datasets such as DVS Gesture and SHD report SNN inference latencies around 5-10 milliseconds on neuromorphic platforms. Directly trained surrogate-gradient SNNs reach about 10 milliseconds while retaining competitive accuracy, whereas converted SNNs may require about 20 milliseconds because longer simulation windows help their spike rates approximate the original ANN activations. For this application profile, surrogate-gradient training forms the stronger initial candidate.

That recommendation follows a mechanism-to-deployment chain. A signal such as an image, audio sample, or sensor stream is encoded into a spike train. Spiking neurons process those events through their timing and intensity, and the output spike pattern is decoded into a prediction, decision, or control signal. The encoding method and training paradigm influence how many spikes must accumulate before the output becomes dependable. ANN-to-SNN conversion commonly uses rate-coded propagation, so the network may need a longer observation window and more spikes to represent activation strength. More spikes increase activity, and a longer window increases response time. The internal representation therefore reaches directly into deployment behavior.

The same chain clarifies energy-oriented selection. Reported comparisons place a conventional ANN/CNN at approximately 200 millijoules per inference. A converted SNN uses about 20 millijoules and produces roughly 20,000 spikes. A surrogate-gradient SNN uses about 15 millijoules with roughly 12,000 spikes. An STDP-based SNN reaches about 5 millijoules with roughly 4,000 spikes. The downward movement in energy accompanies increasingly sparse spiking activity: fewer events require less event-driven processing and communication. Across this evaluated comparison, SNN approaches achieve roughly 90-97% lower energy use than the ANN while accepting different performance tradeoffs.

Suppose the gesture sensor has an extremely small battery and can tolerate slightly reduced accuracy. STDP becomes a plausible choice because it combines the lowest reported energy consumption with the sparsest spike activity. Spike-Timing Dependent Plasticity adjusts connections through the relative timing of spikes, allowing learning to emerge from local activity. Its gradual adaptation leads to slower convergence, and early inference can also be slower while spike patterns stabilize. Under optimized conditions, reported latency settles below 15 milliseconds. The resulting recommendation is qualified: choose STDP when ultra-low energy, sparse activity, and unsupervised learning receive higher priority than peak accuracy or rapid convergence.

**Question.** If STDP uses the least energy, why would an application choose a surrogate-gradient SNN?

**Answer.** Energy is one requirement among several. A surrogate-gradient SNN offers a stronger balance when the application also demands competitive accuracy, responses near 10 milliseconds, and faster training progress. Reported surrogate-trained models approach ANN accuracy within about 1-2% and converge strongly by the twentieth epoch while using about 15 millijoules per inference. STDP reaches lower energy and spike count, but its slightly reduced accuracy and slower adaptation may carry greater operational cost in an accuracy-sensitive or time-sensitive task.

ANN-to-SNN conversion supports a different application profile. Conversion begins with a trained ANN and translates its continuous activations into spiking behavior. It can preserve competitive predictive performance and offers a practical path when an effective ANN already exists. Its rate-coded operation, however, is associated with approximately 20,000 spikes and longer simulation windows in the reported comparison. A qualified recommendation would therefore select conversion when reusing an existing ANN is a central constraint and when the deployment can accommodate energy near 20 millijoules and latency near 20 milliseconds. The recommendation remains tied to the evaluated conditions rather than becoming a universal claim about every converted network.

Evidence-based selection becomes clearer when every recommendation is written as a short argument: application requirement, relevant metric, measured result, mechanism that helps explain the result, and limitation that bounds the conclusion. For example: "Use a surrogate-gradient SNN for a latency-sensitive classifier because it achieves competitive accuracy with responses around 10 milliseconds; direct spike-based optimization supports efficient temporal decisions, while the measured advantage remains specific to the evaluated event-based tasks and neuromorphic conditions." This form exposes the reasoning, making it possible to challenge an assumption or replace a measurement without discarding the whole decision process.

**Question.** How should two models be compared when each wins on different metrics?

**Answer.** Translate the application into thresholds and priorities before comparing the models. Eliminate candidates that fail a hard threshold, such as maximum latency or energy per inference. Compare the remaining candidates using the priorities that affect deployment success. A low-power unsupervised device may favor STDP; a latency-and-accuracy target may favor surrogate-gradient training; an ANN reuse constraint may favor conversion. The winning model is the one whose measured tradeoffs best fit the stated operating conditions.

A sound SNN decision therefore resembles a continuous path from events to consequences. Encoding turns application signals into spikes; neuron and network dynamics transform those spikes; training shapes the useful patterns; spike count and simulation time influence energy and latency; and the deployment profile determines which outcomes carry the greatest weight. A recommendation becomes defensible when every link in that path is visible, every metric serves an application requirement, and every conclusion states the conditions under which the choice remains appropriate.
