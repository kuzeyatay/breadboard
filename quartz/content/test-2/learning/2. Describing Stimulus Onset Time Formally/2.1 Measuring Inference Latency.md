### Measuring Inference Latency

Once an SNN has received spikes and produced a decision, we can ask a practical question: how long did that decision take? Imagine an input stream in which a stimulus begins at a known instant-perhaps the first event in a recorded sample. The network processes the resulting spikes until its output satisfies the rule for making a decision. The time separating those two events measures the network's **inference latency**.

Inference latency is useful because inference unfolds over time. A spiking network may receive evidence gradually as spikes arrive, and its internal state changes in response to those events. Eventually, the accumulated activity becomes sufficient to produce an output, such as selecting a class. Measuring the elapsed time from the beginning of the stimulus to that decision reveals how quickly the network converts incoming evidence into a usable result.

The measurement begins with two timestamps. The **stimulus onset time** is the instant at which the input stimulus begins. We write it as $t_{\text{onset}}$. The **decision time** is the instant at which the network produces its decision according to a specified decision rule. We write it as $t_{\text{decision}}$. Both timestamps must use the same clock and the same unit, such as milliseconds.

Latency is the elapsed time between these events. Elapsed time is found by subtracting the earlier timestamp from the later one:
$$
L = t_{\text{decision}} - t_{\text{onset}}
$$
Here:

- $L$ is the **inference latency**, the duration required to respond to the stimulus.
- $t_{\text{decision}}$ is the **decision time**, when the model's output becomes a decision.
- $t_{\text{onset}}$ is the **stimulus onset time**, when the input begins.

The formula computes a duration rather than a clock reading. If a stimulus begins at $103\ \text{ms}$ on an experiment's clock and the network decides at $111\ \text{ms}$, then
$$
L = 111\ \text{ms} - 103\ \text{ms} = 8\ \text{ms}
$$
The inference latency is therefore $8\ \text{ms}$. The absolute timestamp $111\ \text{ms}$ tells us when the decision occurred within the experiment, while the difference of $8\ \text{ms}$ tells us how long the response took. This subtraction also allows trials that begin at different clock times to be compared through their response intervals. A trial beginning at $500\ \text{ms}$ and ending with a decision at $508\ \text{ms}$ has the same $8\ \text{ms}$ latency.

Defining the decision event precisely is part of defining the measurement. A decision might occur when one output reaches the network's chosen response condition. Whatever rule is used, $t_{\text{decision}}$ records the instant that rule is satisfied. Changing the rule can change the measured latency because the network may need more or less accumulated evidence before the output qualifies as a decision. A latency value therefore describes the response time under a particular input, decision rule, and timing procedure.

Stimulus onset also requires a consistent operational definition. For an event stream, onset can be the timestamp marking the beginning of the presented sample or the first input event designated as belonging to that stimulus. The experiment must choose one definition and apply it consistently. Otherwise, two identical network responses could appear to have different latencies simply because their starting points were recorded differently.

Consider a sample whose designated onset occurs at $20\ \text{ms}$. Spikes then arrive and affect the network over several milliseconds. Suppose the output satisfies the decision rule at $27.5\ \text{ms}$. The latency is
$$
L = 27.5\ \text{ms} - 20\ \text{ms} = 7.5\ \text{ms}
$$
This value describes the full response interval measured from stimulus onset. It includes the time during which the network receives and processes enough spike-based evidence to decide. The number alone does not explain every internal event during those $7.5\ \text{ms}$, but it gives a compact measurement of when the complete inference process produced an actionable output.

**Question.** If the first recorded spike arrives at $42\ \text{ms}$ and the network decides at $49\ \text{ms}$, is the latency always $7\ \text{ms}$?

**Answer.** The latency is $7\ \text{ms}$ when $42\ \text{ms}$ is the experiment's defined stimulus onset. If the stimulus was designated to begin earlier, such as at $40\ \text{ms}$, then the latency is $49-40=9\ \text{ms}$. The calculation depends on the defined onset event, so the starting rule must accompany the reported value.

Inference latency belongs to the model's response phase. **Training time** describes the separate process of adjusting the model's parameters before evaluation or deployment. A network might take a long time to train and then respond quickly to each new sample. Conversely, a completed training process does not determine how rapidly one later decision appears. Inference latency isolates the interval for a single response by starting the clock at stimulus onset and stopping it at the decision event.

**Question.** What does a short inference latency reveal about an SNN decision?

**Answer.** It reveals that the network reached its defined decision condition soon after the stimulus began. The interpretation stays tied to that condition: the network accumulated sufficient spike-based evidence quickly under the chosen timing and decision rules. Latency by itself does not describe accuracy, energy use, or how the model was trained, so those properties require their own measurements.

A useful mental model is a stopwatch attached to two clearly defined events. Stimulus onset starts the stopwatch, incoming spikes drive the inference process, and the decision event stops it. Subtracting the start timestamp from the stop timestamp turns those events into an elapsed duration. That duration-$L=t_{\text{decision}}-t_{\text{onset}}$-captures how quickly an SNN transforms a timed stimulus into a decision.
