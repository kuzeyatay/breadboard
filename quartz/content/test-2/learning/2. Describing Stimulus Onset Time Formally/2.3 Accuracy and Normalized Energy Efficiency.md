## Accuracy and Normalized Energy Efficiency

Once a network has turned an input into a predicted class label, imagine checking a batch of 100 predictions against the correct labels. Some predictions match, while others miss. Counting those matches gives a direct way to answer a basic evaluation question: **How often does the network predict the correct class?** This question leads to classification accuracy. If the same 100 predictions also require a measured amount of energy, a separate practical question appears: **How much predictive success does the network deliver for each joule it consumes?** Normalized energy efficiency answers that question by combining accuracy with inference energy.

Begin with a single prediction. A **correct prediction** occurs when the predicted class equals the known correct class. If an input belongs to class A and the network predicts class A, the prediction contributes one to the correct-prediction count. If the network predicts class B, it contributes zero. Repeating this comparison across a collection of inputs produces two counts: the number of correct predictions and the total number of predictions.

The correct-prediction fraction is therefore
$$
\text{Correct-prediction fraction}
=
\frac{N_{\text{correct}}}{N_{\text{total}}}
$$
Where $N_{\text{correct}}$ is the number of predictions whose predicted labels match the correct labels, and $N_{\text{total}}$ is the total number of predictions evaluated. The fraction lies between $0$ and $1$. A value of $0$ means that none of the predictions were correct, while a value of $1$ means that every prediction was correct.

Percentages provide a familiar way to express this fraction. Multiplying a fraction by $100\%$ converts it into the number of correct predictions expected per hundred predictions. Classification accuracy is thus
$$
A
=
\frac{N_{\text{correct}}}{N_{\text{total}}}\times 100\%
$$
Where $A$ is the classification accuracy expressed as a percentage, $N_{\text{correct}}$ is the number of correct predictions, $N_{\text{total}}$ is the number of evaluated predictions, and $100\%$ converts the fraction into percentage form. This formula lets you compute the share of predictions that receive the correct class label.

Suppose a network makes 200 predictions and 184 of them are correct. Its correct-prediction fraction is
$$
\frac{184}{200}=0.92
$$
Converting that fraction to a percentage gives
$$
A=0.92\times 100\%=92\%
$$
The value $92\%$ means that 92 out of every 100 predictions were correct over this evaluation set. It describes predictive performance without yet considering how much computation, time, or energy produced those predictions.

Accuracy alone therefore answers a focused question about correctness. Two systems can both reach $92\%$ accuracy even if one uses far more energy per inference. An **inference** is one use of the trained network to produce a prediction from an input. If energy is measured over several inferences, the total energy can be divided by the number of inferences to obtain energy per inference. Energy is measured in **joules**, abbreviated $J$.

Consider two networks that each make 92 correct predictions out of 100. Their accuracies are both $92\%$. Suppose one consumes $2\ J$ per inference and the other consumes $0.5\ J$ per inference. Accuracy treats them equally because their prediction counts are equal. Energy use distinguishes them because the second network consumes one quarter as much energy for each prediction. Choosing between them requires keeping correctness and energy visible together.

Normalized energy efficiency combines these quantities as a ratio:
$$
\eta
=
\frac{A}{E}
$$
Where $\eta$ is the normalized energy efficiency, $A$ is classification accuracy, and $E$ is the energy consumed per inference in joules. When $A$ is written as a percentage, $\eta$ has units of percentage points per joule, often written $\%/J$. The formula computes how much measured accuracy is delivered per joule of inference energy.

For the network with $92\%$ accuracy and an energy cost of $2\ J$, the normalized energy efficiency is
$$
\eta=\frac{92\%}{2\ J}=46\ \%/J
$$
For the network with the same accuracy and an energy cost of $0.5\ J$,
$$
\eta=\frac{92\%}{0.5\ J}=184\ \%/J
$$
The second value is four times larger because the same predictive accuracy is achieved with one quarter of the energy. A larger ratio can arise from higher accuracy, lower energy consumption, or a combination of both. The two component measurements reveal which cause produced the change.

Accuracy may also be written as a fraction between $0$ and $1$. Using $A=0.92$ for the first network gives
$$
\eta=\frac{0.92}{2\ J}=0.46\ J^{-1}
$$
This value carries the same information as $46\ \%/J$, because $0.92$ and $92\%$ are two forms of the same accuracy. Comparisons require one consistent convention. Mixing fractional accuracy for one system with percentage accuracy for another would make the resulting ratios differ by a factor of 100 even when the underlying performance is identical.

**Question.** Can a network with lower accuracy have higher normalized energy efficiency?

**Answer.** Yes. Suppose network X reaches $95\%$ accuracy using $5\ J$ per inference, while network Y reaches $90\%$ accuracy using $1\ J$. Their normalized energy efficiencies are
$$
\eta_X=\frac{95\%}{5\ J}=19\ \%/J
$$
And
$$
\eta_Y=\frac{90\%}{1\ J}=90\ \%/J
$$
Network X answers more predictions correctly, while network Y delivers more accuracy per joule. The appropriate choice depends on how strongly the evaluation values raw correctness and energy use.

**Question.** Does a larger accuracy-per-joule value reveal the network's actual energy consumption?

**Answer.** The ratio combines accuracy and energy into one number, so the component values remain necessary for interpretation. For example, $90\%$ accuracy at $1\ J$ and $45\%$ accuracy at $0.5\ J$ both produce $90\ \%/J$. The equal ratios arise from very different predictive performance. Reporting accuracy, energy per inference, and normalized energy efficiency together preserves the full picture.

A useful mental model is to imagine every inference spending an energy budget to purchase predictive success. Classification accuracy measures how reliably the predictions match the correct labels. Energy per inference measures the size of the budget. Normalized energy efficiency measures how much accuracy that budget delivers per joule. Reading all three quantities together separates correctness, resource use, and the relationship between them.
