## Spike Count and Inference Energy

Once stimulus onset has been placed on a timeline, the network's response can be followed timestep by timestep. At each timestep, every neuron either emits a spike or remains silent. Recording those events reveals when the response begins, while counting them reveals how much spiking activity the network produces during one inference. An **inference** is one complete processing run, from presenting an input through obtaining the network's output.

Imagine observing three neurons for four timesteps after a stimulus arrives. The recorded spike pattern might be

| Neuron | $t=1$ | $t=2$ | $t=3$ | $t=4$ |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Neuron 1 | 0 | 1 | 0 | 1 |
| Neuron 2 | 0 | 1 | 1 | 0 |
| Neuron 3 | 0 | 0 | 1 | 0 |

Here, $1$ means that the neuron emitted a spike during that timestep, and $0$ means that it remained silent. The table preserves temporal structure: activity begins at $t=2$, reaches two simultaneous spikes at $t=2$ and $t=3$, and falls to one spike at $t=4$. If the immediate goal is to measure total activity, all emitted spikes can be accumulated into a single number.

Let $s_i[t]$ be the spike indicator for neuron $i$ at timestep $t$:
$$
S_i[t] =
\begin{cases}
1, & \text{if neuron } i \text{ emits a spike at timestep } t,\\
0, & \text{if neuron } i \text{ remains silent at timestep } t.
\end{cases}
$$
The **total spike count** is then
$$
N_{\mathrm{spike}}
=
\sum_{t=1}^{T}
\sum_{i=1}^{N}
S_i[t]
$$
In this expression, $N_{\mathrm{spike}}$ is the total number of emitted spikes during the inference, $N$ is the number of observed neurons, $T$ is the number of timesteps, $i$ identifies a neuron, $t$ identifies a timestep, and $s_i[t]$ records whether that neuron emitted a spike at that time. The inner sum counts the spikes produced by all neurons during one timestep. The outer sum repeats that count across the full observation window.

For the three-neuron example, the count is
$$
N_{\mathrm{spike}}
=
(0+1+0+1)
+
(0+1+1+0)
+
(0+0+1+0)
=
5
$$
The network therefore emits five spikes during the inference. This value measures the amount of spike activity, while the original table retains the timing of that activity. Two responses can each contain five spikes even when one responds immediately after stimulus onset and the other responds several timesteps later. Spike count and onset time therefore answer different questions: onset time describes when activity begins, and total spike count describes how many events occur over the chosen interval.

**Question.** If two neurons spike during the same timestep, does that count as one event because they occur at the same time?

**Answer.** Each emitted spike belongs to a particular neuron, so two neurons firing together contribute two events. The sum runs over both neuron identity and time. Simultaneous spikes share a timestep, yet each adds $1$ to $N_{\mathrm{spike}}$.

Counting events still leaves another practical question: how much energy is required to process one inference? Energy depends on the work performed for each event. A spike must be generated, communicated, or otherwise handled, and its arrival can trigger computations at synaptic connections. A **synaptic operation** is one such computation associated with transmitting or applying a signal through a connection between neurons.

Spike events and synaptic operations can occur in related ways while carrying different costs. One emitted spike may reach several downstream synapses. If a neuron connects to four downstream neurons, one spike from that neuron can lead to four synaptic operations. Another neuron may connect to ten downstream neurons, causing ten operations per emitted spike. The same total spike count can therefore produce different amounts of synaptic work.

Let $N_{\mathrm{synop}}$ denote the total number of synaptic operations performed during the inference. Let $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{spike}}$ be the energy cost of handling one spike event, and let $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{synop}}$ be the energy cost of one synaptic operation. A simple energy model decomposes total inference energy into these two contributions:
$$
E_{\mathrm{inference}}
=
N_{\mathrm{spike}}\varepsilon_{\mathrm{spike}}
+
N_{\mathrm{synop}}\varepsilon_{\mathrm{synop}}
$$
Here, $E_{\mathrm{inference}}$ is the total energy consumed by one inference. The product $N_{\mathrm{spike}}\varepsilon_{\mathrm{spike}}$ gives the energy associated with spike events, and the product $N_{\mathrm{synop}}\varepsilon_{\mathrm{synop}}$ gives the energy associated with synaptic operations. Adding them produces an estimate of the total inference energy covered by this cost model.

The units make the distinction especially clear. $N_{\mathrm{spike}}$ is a count measured in spikes. If $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{spike}}$ is measured in joules per spike, their product is measured in joules. Likewise, $N_{\mathrm{synop}}$ is an operation count, and multiplying it by joules per operation produces another energy quantity. Only after the counts are multiplied by their corresponding per-event costs can the contributions be added as energy.

Suppose an inference emits $5$ spikes and performs $18$ synaptic operations. Assume that handling a spike costs $2$ nanojoules and each synaptic operation costs $0.5$ nanojoules. The spike-event contribution is
$$
E_{\mathrm{spike}}
=
5 \times 2\ \mathrm{nJ}
=
10\ \mathrm{nJ}
$$
And the synaptic-operation contribution is
$$
E_{\mathrm{synop}}
=
18 \times 0.5\ \mathrm{nJ}
=
9\ \mathrm{nJ}
$$
The total is therefore
$$
E_{\mathrm{inference}}
=
10\ \mathrm{nJ}
+
9\ \mathrm{nJ}
=
19\ \mathrm{nJ}
$$
Now imagine a different network that also emits five spikes but performs thirty synaptic operations. Under the same cost assumptions, its spike-event contribution remains $10\ \mathrm{nJ}$, while its synaptic-operation contribution becomes $15\ \mathrm{nJ}$. Its total reaches $25\ \mathrm{nJ}$. Equal spike counts can therefore accompany different inference energies because connection activity changes the number of operations.

**Question.** Can inference energy be calculated from total spike count alone?

**Answer.** Total spike count provides one required activity measurement, but the energy calculation also needs the number of synaptic operations and the cost assigned to each kind of work. A spike count becomes an energy contribution only when it is multiplied by an energy-per-spike value. The network's connectivity and spike destinations determine how much synaptic work accompanies those spikes.

A useful mental model is to picture the spike record as an event ledger. Each $1$ marks an emitted event at a particular neuron and timestep. Summing the ledger gives total spike count. Following each event through the network gives the synaptic-operation count. Multiplying both counts by their respective costs and adding the results gives total inference energy. Timing describes when the response unfolds, spike count describes how active it is, and the energy model describes the cost of carrying out that activity.
