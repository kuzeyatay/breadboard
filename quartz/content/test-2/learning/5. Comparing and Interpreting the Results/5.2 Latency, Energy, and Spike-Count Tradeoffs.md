## Latency, Energy, and Spike-Count Tradeoffs

Once inference latency, energy per inference, and total spike count are understood separately, they can be used together to choose a model for a real task. Imagine a small edge device that must recognize a hand gesture and respond while the gesture is still happening. Its model must reach a decision quickly, fit within a limited energy budget, and avoid generating unnecessary event traffic. A model that excels on only one of these requirements may still be a poor fit for the device. A useful comparison therefore asks three connected questions: How long does one inference take? How much energy does that inference consume? How much spiking activity occurs while producing the decision?

Latency measures the delay from receiving a sample to producing a decision. For a time-sensitive system, a few additional milliseconds can affect whether the response arrives at a useful moment. The reported latency comparison places an ANN CNN beside three SNN variants: a converted SNN, a surrogate-gradient SNN, and an STDP-based SNN. The ANN CNN requires roughly 20-50 milliseconds depending on model complexity and hardware configuration. The converted SNN is around 20 milliseconds because it may need a longer simulation window to represent continuous ANN activations as spike rates. The surrogate-gradient SNN reaches about 10 milliseconds, while the STDP-based SNN stabilizes below roughly 15 milliseconds under optimized conditions.

The latency table makes the response-time differences concrete. The model column identifies the learning or representation strategy, while the inference-latency column records the time required for a sample to become a prediction. Reading across the models shows why the training strategy affects response time. Conversion relies on spikes accumulated across time to approximate the activations of an already-trained ANN, so a longer observation window can improve that approximation while delaying the decision. Surrogate-gradient training learns spiking behavior directly, allowing useful temporal patterns to form within a shorter window. STDP changes connections according to spike timing and may respond more gradually during early inference as its activity adapts.

The same comparison becomes easier to see as a shape when the values are displayed as bars.

![Bar chart comparing inference latency across ANN and SNN models](/test-2/assets/source-visuals/2510-27379v1-page-8-graph-g1-bar-chart-comparing-inference-latency-across-ann.png)

*Bar chart comparing inference latency across ANN and SNN models* *(p. 8)*

Shorter bars represent faster decisions. The surrogate-gradient SNN sits near 10 milliseconds, giving it the strongest reported balance for applications that need rapid inference while retaining competitive accuracy. The STDP-based model remains within a low-latency range, though its gradual spike adaptation can make it somewhat slower. The converted SNN takes around twice as long as the surrogate-trained model because rate-coded conversion often needs more time steps. The ANN range can extend higher still. These bars answer the timing question, but model selection also depends on the work performed during that time.

Energy per inference measures the energy consumed to process one sample and produce one output. Its unit here is the millijoule, abbreviated mJ. If a device performs $N$ inferences and each inference consumes $E$ millijoules, then its inference energy is $N \times E$. Here, $N$ is the number of samples processed and $E$ is the measured energy per sample. This simple multiplication lets us interpret apparently small per-inference differences at operating scale. For example, a model using 20 mJ per inference consumes 20,000 mJ across 1,000 inferences, whereas a model using 5 mJ consumes 5,000 mJ over the same workload.

Spike count records how many discrete spike events the SNN generates during one inference. Each event can trigger communication and synaptic processing, so fewer spikes often reduce computational activity. Energy also includes the cost of the operations and communication associated with those spikes, along with effects of the architecture and execution platform. Two models can therefore have similar spike counts while consuming different amounts of energy per event, or different spike counts while achieving similar total energy. Spike count describes activity; energy per inference describes the resulting resource cost.

The ANN CNN consumes approximately 200 mJ per inference through continuous-valued operations. Because the ANN communicates through activations rather than spikes, its spike-count entry belongs to a different representation category from the three SNN values. The converted SNN lowers energy to about 20 mJ, yet generates roughly 20,000 spikes as rate-coded activity propagates across its simulation window. The surrogate-gradient SNN reduces both measures to about 15 mJ and 12,000 spikes. Direct learning shapes when spikes occur, producing a sparser pattern than conversion while also supporting latency near 10 milliseconds. The STDP-based SNN reaches the lowest reported energy, about 5 mJ, and the sparsest activity, about 4,000 spikes per inference.

*SNN energy efficiency summary comparing energy per inference and spike count across ANN, converted SNN, surrogate-gradient SNN, and STDP-based SNN models* *(p. 9)*

The downward movement in both energy and spike count reveals a relationship: models generating less event activity can require less event-driven computation. Their separate axes preserve two distinct meanings. The converted model's 20,000 spikes explain part of its resource demand, while its 20 mJ value reports the measured cost of the complete inference. Likewise, the STDP model's 4,000 spikes describe a sparse activity pattern, and its 5 mJ value describes the energy outcome. Its low activity does not determine its response time by itself; latency also depends on when enough informative spikes arrive for the model to decide.

**Question.** If the STDP-based SNN has the fewest spikes and lowest energy, why might a time-sensitive application still choose the surrogate-gradient SNN?

**Answer.** The surrogate-gradient SNN reaches a decision in about 10 milliseconds, combines that response time with competitive accuracy, and converges by the twentieth training epoch. The STDP-based model prioritizes sparse activity and energy near 5 mJ, while its gradual adaptation can produce a somewhat slower response and slightly reduced accuracy. A strict energy budget favors STDP; a task requiring a strong joint balance of response speed, accuracy, and convergence can favor surrogate training.

**Question.** Does a lower spike count guarantee lower latency?

**Answer.** Spike count measures how many events occur, while latency measures how long the model takes to reach its decision. A model may emit only a few spikes but wait longer for them to accumulate or become informative. Another model may emit more spikes in a short, concentrated interval and decide sooner. Their relationship depends on both the number and timing of events.

A practical mental model treats each inference as a journey with three different costs. Latency is the duration of the journey, energy is the resource spent completing it, and spike count is the amount of event activity generated along the way. Conversion saves substantial energy relative to continuous ANN computation but pays for rate approximation with more spikes and a longer window. STDP produces the sparsest, lowest-energy journey. Surrogate-gradient training shapes spike timing directly and occupies the middle ground in energy and activity while delivering the fastest reported SNN response and convergence by epoch twenty. Choosing among them means matching that whole pattern to the application's timing, energy, activity, and performance requirements.
