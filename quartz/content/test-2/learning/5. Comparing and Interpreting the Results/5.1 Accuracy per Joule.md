## Accuracy per Joule

Having compared classification accuracy and energy per inference separately, we can now combine them into a single question: how much correct predictive performance does a model deliver for the energy it consumes? Imagine choosing a network for a battery-powered device that must classify thousands of inputs. A highly accurate model may drain the battery quickly, while a lower-energy model may make more mistakes. Reading accuracy alone favors predictive performance; reading energy alone favors thrift. A useful choice requires both quantities to enter the same calculation.

Classification accuracy is the proportion of predictions a model gets correct. If a model classifies 94 out of 100 images correctly, its accuracy is $0.94$, or $94\%$. Energy per inference is the energy consumed to process one input and produce one prediction. Energy is measured in joules, abbreviated $J$; one millijoule, abbreviated $mJ$, is one-thousandth of a joule:
$$
1\ \text{mJ}=0.001\ \text{J}
$$
A model consuming $5\ \text{mJ}$ per inference therefore consumes $0.005\ \text{J}$ per inference. Converting all measurements to the same unit keeps comparisons consistent.

To combine predictive performance and energy use, define normalized energy efficiency as accuracy divided by energy:
$$
\eta=\frac{A}{E}
$$
Here, $\eta$ is normalized energy efficiency, often interpreted as accuracy per joule; $A$ is classification accuracy, written consistently as either a fraction or a percentage; and $E$ is the energy consumed per inference in joules. The ratio computes how much accuracy accompanies each joule of energy use. A larger value means that more predictive performance is obtained from the same energy budget.

Suppose Model X achieves $94\%$ accuracy while consuming $0.020\ \text{J}$ per inference. Using accuracy as a fraction gives
$$
\eta_X=\frac{0.94}{0.020\ \text{J}}=47\ \text{J}^{-1}
$$
Now suppose Model Y achieves $90\%$ accuracy while consuming $0.005\ \text{J}$:
$$
\eta_Y=\frac{0.90}{0.005\ \text{J}}=180\ \text{J}^{-1}
$$
Model X classifies four additional inputs correctly per hundred in this example, while Model Y delivers far more accuracy per unit of energy. The ratio exposes a tradeoff hidden by either measurement alone. A system with a strict accuracy requirement may still favor Model X. A device with a tight battery or thermal budget may favor Model Y. Accuracy per joule helps compare the exchange between the two goals; the operational requirement determines how that exchange should influence the final choice.

The reported comparison applies this joint view to an ANN, a converted SNN, a surrogate-gradient SNN, and an STDP-based SNN. Their energy consumption differs sharply: the ANN uses approximately $200\ \text{mJ}$ per inference, the converted SNN about $20\ \text{mJ}$, the surrogate-gradient SNN about $15\ \text{mJ}$, and the STDP-based SNN about $5\ \text{mJ}$. The ANN occupies the high-accuracy, high-energy end of the comparison. The converted SNN lowers energy substantially while retaining competitive predictive performance. The surrogate-gradient SNN reduces energy further and keeps its accuracy close to the ANN, within roughly one to two percentage points in the reported results. That pairing gives it a strong balance between predictive quality and computational savings. The STDP-based SNN reaches the lowest energy consumption and therefore gains a large denominator advantage in the efficiency calculation, although its lower accuracy changes whether it is suitable for an application. The graph's shape should therefore be read diagonally across performance and energy: moving toward lower energy improves the ratio, while moving toward higher accuracy also improves it.

A beginner may wonder whether the lowest-energy model must always have the highest accuracy per joule. Energy has a strong effect when it changes by a large factor, but the numerator still contributes. If two models consume similar energy, even a modest accuracy difference can determine the larger ratio. If one model saves a great deal of energy while losing only a small amount of accuracy, its ratio can rise dramatically. Both values must be inserted before the ordering is known.

The dataset also affects the numerator. MNIST and CIFAR-10 present different classification problems, so a model's accuracy must be paired with the dataset on which it was measured. **Question.** Why convert millijoules to joules before calculating accuracy per joule?

**Answer.** The name of the metric specifies joules in the denominator, so the energy value must use joules. For example, $5\ \text{mJ}=0.005\ \text{J}$. Dividing $0.90$ by $5$ would calculate accuracy per millijoule, while dividing $0.90$ by $0.005$ calculates accuracy per joule. Both can describe efficiency, but their numerical values differ by a factor of one thousand. A shared unit makes model comparisons interpretable.

**Question.** How can a model with slightly lower accuracy be the stronger efficiency choice?

**Answer.** Accuracy per joule responds to proportional changes in both quantities. If accuracy falls from $0.95$ to $0.92$, the numerator falls by about three percent. If energy simultaneously falls from $0.200\ \text{J}$ to $0.015\ \text{J}$, the denominator becomes more than thirteen times smaller. The energy reduction dominates the small accuracy reduction, producing a much larger ratio. Whether that model should be selected still depends on whether its absolute accuracy satisfies the application's requirement.

The resulting mental model is a rate: classification accuracy is the useful predictive outcome, energy per inference is the resource spent to obtain it, and accuracy per joule describes the outcome delivered per unit of that resource. Each dataset supplies its own accuracy numerator, each model supplies an energy denominator, and their ratio reveals the joint performance-efficiency tradeoff.
