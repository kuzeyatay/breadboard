## Latency Across ANN and SNN Models

Once decision latency and the simulation window are understood, the reported results can be read as a race: each model receives a sample, performs its required computation over time, and eventually produces a decision. Imagine a hand-gesture controller waiting to classify a movement. A response near 10 milliseconds feels immediate, while a response that takes several tens of milliseconds leaves less time for sensing, communication, and action. The useful comparison therefore asks how long each model takes to reach its own decision under the reported evaluation conditions.

A model's measured latency is its **latency operating point**: the response time produced by a particular combination of model, training strategy, simulation window, hardware, and evaluation setup. An operating point is a reported result under defined conditions rather than a permanent property of an entire model family. The comparison includes a CNN-based artificial neural network, an SNN produced through ANN-to-SNN conversion, an SNN trained directly with surrogate gradients, and an SNN trained through Spike-Timing-Dependent Plasticity, or STDP.

The model-by-model values make the ordering visible. The surrogate-gradient SNN reaches a decision at about 10 milliseconds, giving it the lowest reported latency among the four compared models. The STDP-based SNN operates below 15 milliseconds under optimized conditions. The converted SNN takes about 20 milliseconds, while CNN-based ANNs occupy a broader range of roughly 20-50 milliseconds depending on model complexity and hardware configuration. These numbers describe the reported benchmark context, which includes event-based datasets such as DVS Gesture and SHD and neuromorphic deployment conditions for the lowest SNN latencies.

Reading the table row by row connects each training strategy to a latency in milliseconds. The surrogate-gradient row provides the low-latency operating point of approximately 10 ms. The STDP row sits slightly higher, reflecting a response that stabilizes below 15 ms under optimized conditions. The converted-SNN row rises to approximately 20 ms. The table should therefore be read as a comparison of measured operating points within a shared evaluation context, rather than as a promise that every implementation will reproduce exactly the same time.

Direct surrogate-gradient training helps explain the fastest result. A spiking neuron produces discrete events, yet the threshold that creates a spike makes ordinary gradient calculations difficult. Surrogate-gradient training uses a differentiable approximation during learning so that gradient-based optimization can shape the network's temporal behavior. Because the SNN is trained in its spiking form, learning can organize when spikes occur and how rapidly enough evidence accumulates for a decision. The resulting model can combine competitive accuracy with a response near 10 ms.

Consider a simple interpretation of that response. If an event-based sensor begins producing spikes when a gesture starts, a surrogate-trained SNN operating near 10 ms needs only a short interval of activity before its output becomes decisive. Training has adjusted the spiking dynamics directly, so early spikes can carry useful evidence. The model reaches its decision without waiting through a long sequence merely to reconstruct another network's continuous activation values.

ANN-to-SNN conversion follows a different route. A conventional ANN is trained first, and its continuous activations are then represented through spiking activity. The converted SNN often needs to observe spikes across a longer simulation window so that spike rates approximate those original activation magnitudes. If a larger ANN activation is represented by a higher firing rate, the system needs enough time to distinguish a genuinely high rate from a brief fluctuation. Extending the observation period improves that estimate, but each additional simulation step delays the final decision. This **conversion latency cost** appears in the converted model's operating point of about 20 ms.

An analogy is estimating rainfall intensity. One raindrop tells you that rain has begun, but estimating a rate such as drops per second requires an observation interval. A longer interval gives a steadier rate estimate and postpones the moment when the estimate is available. Converted SNNs face a similar timing requirement when spike rates stand in for ANN activations. Their competitive performance can therefore come with longer simulation windows and correspondingly higher latency.

The STDP-based SNN follows below 15 ms, the converted SNN rises to roughly 20 ms, and the ANN comparison extends higher within its reported range. The gap between the surrogate and converted bars encodes the practical consequence of training directly in the temporal spiking domain instead of recovering continuous activations through rate estimates over a longer window.

STDP reaches a different operating point because it adapts connections from the relative timing of spikes. Its early inference behavior can be slightly slower while spike activity gradually adapts, after which optimized models stabilize below 15 ms. That places STDP between the surrogate-trained and converted models in the reported latency ordering. Latency alone identifies the fastest decision here; choosing among strategies requires keeping the intended metric in view and reserving other performance dimensions for their own comparisons.

**Question.** Does the approximately 10 ms surrogate-gradient result mean that every surrogate-trained SNN decides in 10 ms?

**Answer.** No. Approximately 10 ms is the reported latency operating point under the stated benchmark and deployment conditions. Dataset, architecture, hardware, simulation settings, and the criterion used to declare a decision can shift the measured latency. The result supports a relative interpretation: direct surrogate-gradient training produced the fastest response in this comparison.

**Question.** Why can a converted SNN take longer even though it uses spikes?

**Answer.** Conversion often represents an ANN's continuous activation through a spike rate. A reliable rate estimate requires observing spikes across multiple simulation steps. That longer simulation window adds waiting time before the output can be interpreted confidently, producing the reported conversion latency cost of roughly 20 ms.

The complete mental model links training strategy to the time needed for evidence to become usable. Surrogate-gradient training shapes spiking dynamics directly, allowing informative temporal activity to support a decision near 10 ms. STDP adapts through spike timing and settles into a sub-15 ms response under optimized conditions. ANN-to-SNN conversion preserves a trained ANN's behavior by estimating activations from spike rates, so it often needs a longer simulation window and reaches a decision around 20 ms. Latency is therefore the visible outcome of how each method teaches-or translates-the network to communicate through time.
