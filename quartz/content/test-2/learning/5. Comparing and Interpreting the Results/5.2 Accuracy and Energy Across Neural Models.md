## Accuracy and Energy Across Neural Models

Now that classification accuracy, normalized energy consumption, and the three SNN training strategies are familiar, the results can be read as a model-selection problem. Imagine choosing a neural model for a system with a limited energy budget. High classification accuracy remains valuable, yet every inference also consumes energy. A useful comparison must therefore answer two separate questions: How reliably does each model classify the inputs, and how much energy does it require to do so?

The comparison includes four models: a conventional artificial neural network, an SNN produced through ANN-to-SNN conversion, an SNN trained directly with surrogate gradients, and an SNN trained through spike-timing-dependent plasticity, or STDP. Their classification performance is measured on MNIST and CIFAR-10, while their energy use is expressed through normalized energy consumption. Normalization places the models on a shared relative scale, making their energy requirements easier to compare even when accuracy is reported as a percentage. Higher accuracy values are preferable, whereas lower normalized energy consumption values are preferable because they indicate that less energy is required.

A careful interpretation begins by reading one metric at a time. Start with MNIST accuracy and compare all four models within that column. Then repeat the process for CIFAR-10 accuracy. Finally, inspect normalized energy consumption independently. This order prevents a visually strong result in one metric from dominating the entire judgment.

Read the table row by row after completing the column comparisons. The ANN row establishes the strongest raw-accuracy reference and the highest energy requirement. Its position represents a performance choice that gives classification accuracy a very high priority. The converted SNN row preserves competitive classification performance while sharply reducing energy use. Conversion therefore carries much of the trained ANN's behavior into a spike-based model, although its energy requirement remains higher than those of the directly trained SNN approaches.

The surrogate-gradient SNN moves farther toward lower energy consumption while staying close to ANN-level accuracy. Across the reported results, surrogate-trained SNNs approach ANN accuracy within roughly one to two percentage points. That small accuracy gap accompanies a large reduction in energy consumption. For a system that values both reliable classification and low energy use, this row represents a balanced compromise.

The STDP-based SNN occupies another part of the tradeoff. It achieves the lowest energy consumption among the four models and also exhibits the sparsest spiking activity in the broader comparison, reaching approximately 5 mJ per inference and about 4,000 spikes. Its classification accuracy is lower than the other approaches. A severe energy constraint can make this exchange worthwhile because a modest reduction in classification performance buys the largest energy saving.

This row-by-row reading reveals why the word *best* needs a stated criterion. The ANN leads when raw accuracy receives the greatest weight. The STDP-based SNN leads when minimizing energy dominates the decision. The surrogate-gradient SNN becomes especially attractive when accuracy and energy both carry substantial weight. The converted SNN offers another intermediate choice, retaining competitive accuracy while consuming much less energy than the ANN.

The two dataset columns also need separate attention. MNIST and CIFAR-10 are distinct classification tests, so success on one column cannot stand in for success on the other. A model's rank may remain similar across both datasets, or the gaps between models may change. Comparing the columns shows whether a model's apparent advantage is broadly present across the reported tasks or especially pronounced on one of them. This is the essence of a multi-dataset comparison: each dataset supplies another view of model behavior, and the combined evidence supports a more stable judgment.

Follow one metric across the four model groups rather than comparing every bar at once. The accuracy series places the ANN at the high-performance end, with the converted and surrogate-gradient SNNs remaining competitive and the STDP-based SNN giving up more accuracy. The energy series reverses the practical preference: the ANN has the greatest consumption, the converted SNN uses far less, the surrogate-gradient SNN reduces it further, and the STDP-based SNN reaches the lowest level. The shape therefore communicates two different rankings. Moving toward lower energy generally changes the amount of accuracy retained, and each training strategy lands at a different point along that exchange.

Suppose a designer cares equally about classification performance and energy. The ANN's accuracy advantage must then be considered alongside its much larger energy requirement, reported at approximately 200 mJ per inference in the energy comparison. The converted SNN lowers this to about 20 mJ, while the surrogate-gradient SNN reaches about 15 mJ. The STDP-based SNN reaches about 5 mJ. These values correspond to large relative reductions, with the SNN approaches achieving roughly 90-97% lower energy use than the ANN while retaining different levels of classification performance.

Now suppose the priorities change. A strict energy budget pushes the choice toward the STDP-based SNN. A strong accuracy requirement with meaningful energy limits favors the surrogate-gradient SNN. A requirement centered almost entirely on the highest reported accuracy favors the ANN. The measured results stay fixed; the preferred model changes because the selection rule changes.

**Question.** Why should MNIST accuracy, CIFAR-10 accuracy, and normalized energy consumption be compared separately before choosing a model?

**Answer.** Each column answers a different question. MNIST accuracy measures classification performance on one dataset, CIFAR-10 accuracy measures it on another, and normalized energy consumption describes computational cost on a relative scale. Combining them too early can hide a weak result behind a strong one. Separate comparisons reveal each model's performance profile; the final choice can then reflect the actual balance of dataset performance and energy constraints.

A common beginner confusion comes from treating lower energy consumption as though it automatically implies better overall performance. Normalized energy consumption reports relative resource use, with lower values indicating less energy consumed, while accuracy measures successful classification, with higher values indicating better classification performance. They are separate objectives. The STDP-based SNN demonstrates this separation clearly: it achieves the lowest normalized energy consumption while accepting reduced accuracy. The ANN demonstrates the opposite priority, and the surrogate-gradient SNN occupies a middle region where accuracy remains close to the ANN while energy consumption falls dramatically.

**Question.** Does the surrogate-gradient SNN always provide the best compromise?

**Answer.** Its reported combination of competitive accuracy and low energy consumption makes it a strong balanced option, especially when both objectives receive substantial weight. A different priority can produce a different choice. Extremely tight energy constraints strengthen the case for STDP, while a decision focused on maximum raw accuracy strengthens the case for the ANN. A compromise only has meaning after the priorities have been stated.

The complete mental model is a landscape rather than a race with one finish line. MNIST accuracy and CIFAR-10 accuracy describe how well each model classifies two different datasets, while normalized energy consumption describes the resources spent during inference. ANN, converted SNN, surrogate-gradient SNN, and STDP-based SNN occupy different positions in that landscape. Choosing among them means identifying the position that fits the intended balance: maximum classification performance, minimum energy consumption, or a practical point between those goals.
