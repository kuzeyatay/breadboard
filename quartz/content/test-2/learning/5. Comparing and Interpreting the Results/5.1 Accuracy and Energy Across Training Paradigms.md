## Accuracy and Energy Across Training Paradigms

Now that surrogate-gradient training, ANN-to-SNN conversion, STDP, classification accuracy, and normalized energy efficiency are familiar, they can be brought together to make a practical model-selection decision. Imagine choosing a network for a battery-powered image classifier. A highly accurate model gives dependable predictions, while a low-energy model allows the device to operate longer between charges. Choosing well requires reading both measurements together, because improving one measurement can come with a change in the other.

Classification accuracy is the percentage of test images assigned the correct class. Energy consumption measures the computational energy used during inference, while normalized energy consumption expresses that usage relative to a common reference so that model families can be compared on the same scale. A lower normalized energy value means that a model uses a smaller fraction of the reference energy. These two metrics answer different questions: accuracy describes predictive performance, and normalized energy describes computational cost.

The comparison includes four model families. The conventional ANN performs continuous-valued operations throughout its layers. A converted SNN begins as a trained ANN and translates its activations into spike-based computation. A surrogate-gradient SNN is trained directly while using a differentiable approximation that allows gradient-based optimization despite the discrete nature of spikes. An STDP-based SNN adjusts connections through the relative timing of spike events. Each route produces a different combination of predictive performance and energy demand.

The performance summary places those combinations side by side for MNIST and CIFAR-10. MNIST contains relatively simple handwritten-digit images, whereas CIFAR-10 contains more varied natural images distributed across ten classes. Reading both accuracy columns reveals whether a model's performance remains strong as the visual classification task becomes more demanding. Reading the normalized-energy column beside them reveals the computational price associated with that performance.

Read the table by moving across each model row rather than scanning a single column in isolation. The ANN occupies the high-accuracy, high-energy end of the comparison. Conversion preserves competitive classification performance while substantially reducing energy consumption. Direct surrogate-gradient training brings SNN accuracy close to ANN accuracy-within roughly one to two percentage points in the reported comparison-while retaining much of the energy advantage associated with spike-based computation. STDP reaches the lowest energy level, accompanied by a larger reduction in accuracy.

The two dataset columns also reveal **dataset-dependent performance**: the value of a training paradigm depends partly on the problem it must solve. MNIST provides a setting in which several approaches can achieve strong classification results, so energy use may become the deciding measurement. CIFAR-10 places greater pressure on the learned representation, making accuracy differences more consequential. A method that appears highly attractive on MNIST may occupy a less favorable position on CIFAR-10 if its predictive performance falls more sharply there.

Suppose two candidate models both satisfy the accuracy requirement for a handwritten-digit device. If the surrogate-gradient SNN and STDP-based SNN both clear that requirement, the lower-energy STDP model may be the practical choice. Now suppose the application classifies varied natural images and requires the highest dependable accuracy available among the SNN options. The surrogate-gradient model may become preferable because it retains stronger predictive performance while still consuming far less energy than the ANN. The preferred model changes when the dataset and application requirement change.

This relationship is called an **accuracy-energy tradeoff**. A tradeoff appears when moving toward a better value on one metric tends to move away from a better value on another. Here, the ANN emphasizes raw accuracy and pays the largest energy cost, approximately 200 mJ per inference in the reported profile. The converted SNN reduces that cost to about 20 mJ. The surrogate-gradient SNN lowers it further to about 15 mJ while staying close to ANN accuracy. The STDP-based SNN reaches about 5 mJ per inference and accepts a more visible accuracy reduction.

The graph makes these relative positions easier to interpret as profiles rather than isolated measurements.

*Performance and energy comparison of ANN, converted SNN, direct surrogate-gradient SNN, and STDP-based SNN* *(p. 7)*

Follow the models from ANN through converted, surrogate-gradient, and STDP-based SNNs. Energy consumption drops steeply as computation becomes increasingly event-driven and sparse. Accuracy changes more gradually for conversion and surrogate-gradient training, then falls more noticeably for STDP. The shape therefore suggests a useful middle region: surrogate-gradient training gives up a small amount of accuracy while gaining a large energy reduction. Conversion also offers substantial savings, though its energy profile remains above that of the directly trained SNNs. STDP sits at the extreme low-energy end and suits situations where power constraints can outweigh the cost of reduced classification performance.

These positions demonstrate **non-dominance across metrics**. A model dominates another only when it is at least as good on every relevant metric and strictly better on at least one. The comparison does not produce one model that universally defeats all others. The ANN leads in raw accuracy while consuming the most energy. STDP leads in energy efficiency while producing lower accuracy. The surrogate-gradient SNN occupies a compromise position, and the converted SNN offers another balance between preservation of ANN performance and reduced computational cost.

**Question.** If the STDP-based SNN uses the least energy, why would an application choose a surrogate-gradient SNN?

**Answer.** The application may require an accuracy level that the STDP-based model does not reach, especially on the more demanding dataset. The surrogate-gradient SNN stays close to ANN accuracy while reducing energy consumption by roughly 90% or more relative to the ANN profile. It therefore offers a stronger balance when both prediction quality and battery or hardware limits carry substantial weight.

**Question.** Can MNIST accuracy alone identify the best training paradigm?

**Answer.** MNIST accuracy shows performance on one relatively simple classification task. A sound choice also considers CIFAR-10 accuracy, normalized energy consumption, and the application's minimum acceptable accuracy. Comparing the same model across both datasets reveals how its advantage changes with task difficulty, while the energy measurement reveals the cost of obtaining that performance.

A useful mental model is a map with accuracy increasing in one direction and energy consumption increasing in another. The ANN, converted SNN, surrogate-gradient SNN, and STDP-based SNN occupy different locations on that map. MNIST and CIFAR-10 can shift their relative attractiveness because each dataset asks a different amount from the learned representation. Model selection therefore means choosing the location that fits the application: maximum predictive performance, minimum energy use, or a balanced position where a large energy saving costs only a small amount of accuracy.
