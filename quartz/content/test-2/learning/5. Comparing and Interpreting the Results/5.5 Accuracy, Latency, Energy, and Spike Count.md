## Accuracy, Latency, Energy, and Spike Count

Once accuracy, decision latency, inference energy, spike count, and convergence time have been measured, model selection becomes a practical reasoning problem. Imagine choosing a network for a battery-powered gesture sensor that must respond within 15 milliseconds. A model with the highest classification accuracy may consume too much energy, while the model with the lowest energy use may sacrifice more accuracy than the application permits. The useful choice emerges only when the measurements are considered together.

This joint view is called **multi-metric evaluation**. Each model is represented by a collection of measurements rather than by a single winning number. Classification accuracy measures how often the model predicts the correct class. Decision latency measures how long it takes to produce an inference. Estimated inference energy measures the energy consumed for one prediction. Total spike count measures how many spike events the SNN generates during that prediction. Convergence time describes how quickly training reaches stable performance, often expressed through the number of training epochs required.

These metrics describe different parts of the system. Accuracy and latency characterize the result experienced at inference time. Energy and spike count describe computational activity and efficiency. Convergence time describes the training process that produces the model. A network can therefore be attractive during deployment while still requiring a slower or more difficult training process. Keeping training cost and inference behavior conceptually separate prevents convergence speed from being confused with decision speed.

The four model families occupy different regions of this multi-dimensional comparison. The ANN provides the strongest raw accuracy, but its continuous-valued computation consumes about 200 millijoules per inference in the reported comparison. Converting an ANN into an SNN reduces estimated energy to about 20 millijoules, a reduction of roughly 90%, while retaining competitive performance. That conversion still generates around 20,000 spikes per inference because rate-coded spike activity must approximate the ANN's continuous activations over a simulation window.

A surrogate-gradient SNN reaches a different balance. Its accuracy comes within about 1-2 percentage points of the ANN while estimated energy falls to approximately 15 millijoules per inference. Its activity also decreases to around 12,000 spikes. Directly learning useful spiking patterns allows it to combine competitive accuracy with sparser activity than the converted SNN. Its latency can be around 10 milliseconds, and its training behavior stabilizes comparatively quickly by roughly the twentieth epoch.

The STDP-based SNN moves farther toward sparse, low-energy operation. It uses about 5 millijoules per inference and produces around 4,000 spikes, the lowest values among the compared SNN approaches. Its accuracy is slightly lower, and its learning converges more gradually. Its latency can stabilize below 15 milliseconds under optimized conditions, although early inference behavior may be slower while spike activity adapts. These measurements describe a model that exchanges some predictive performance and training speed for very sparse computation.

Spike count helps explain the energy pattern. A spike represents an event that triggers communication or computation. More spikes generally create more activity, so the progression from roughly 20,000 spikes in the converted SNN to 12,000 in the surrogate-trained SNN and 4,000 in the STDP-based SNN accompanies a progression from about 20 to 15 to 5 millijoules. Energy remains a measured system-level quantity rather than a direct synonym for spike count, because deployment hardware and implementation also affect consumption. Spike count is best interpreted as an activity indicator that helps explain why energy differs.

Latency introduces another operating choice. A converted SNN may require a simulation window near 20 milliseconds so that accumulated spikes can approximate the original ANN activations. A shorter window produces an earlier decision but gives the network less time to represent those activation rates. A surrogate-trained SNN can reach competitive accuracy with responses around 10 milliseconds, placing it near an appealing accuracy-latency operating point. An **operating point** is one concrete configuration and its resulting measurements: the selected model, inference window, and deployment conditions considered together.

The same model can have several possible operating points. Extending its inference window may change its latency and spiking activity, while the hardware platform may change the observed execution time and energy. Comparisons therefore belong under equivalent evaluation conditions. Reported ANN latencies of roughly 20-50 milliseconds depend on model complexity and hardware, while SNN latencies of about 5-10 milliseconds have been observed on neuromorphic platforms for event-based tasks. The deployment environment belongs inside the comparison rather than being treated as an afterthought.

**Question.** If the STDP-based SNN has the lowest energy and spike count, why would anyone choose the surrogate-gradient SNN?

**Answer.** Energy and sparsity are only two requirements. A task may also demand accuracy close to the ANN, a response near 10 milliseconds, and faster convergence during training. The surrogate-gradient SNN comes within about 1-2 percentage points of ANN accuracy while using approximately 15 millijoules and 12,000 spikes per inference. The STDP-based model reduces those resource measurements further, to about 5 millijoules and 4,000 spikes, but accepts slightly lower accuracy and slower convergence. The preferred model follows the application's accuracy floor, latency deadline, energy budget, and training requirements.

Those requirements form a **deployment constraint**. A constraint turns a vague preference such as "fast" or "efficient" into a boundary: the prediction must arrive within a given time, energy must remain within a budget, or accuracy must exceed an acceptable minimum. Models that violate a required boundary leave consideration even when they excel elsewhere. For a system with a strict 12-millisecond response target, the surrogate-trained SNN's approximately 10-millisecond latency may fit while a converted SNN near 20 milliseconds may exceed the limit. For a system dominated by energy scarcity, the STDP-based SNN's approximately 5-millijoule inference cost may justify its accuracy tradeoff.

After required constraints are applied, several candidates may remain. **Pareto-like operating-point reasoning** compares them without inventing a universal score. An operating point is dominated when another candidate is at least as good across all relevant metrics and better in one or more. A candidate remains worth considering when improving one of its measurements requires giving up something elsewhere. The ANN offers higher raw accuracy but uses far more energy. The STDP-based SNN offers the lowest energy and spike count but gives up some accuracy and convergence speed. The surrogate-gradient SNN occupies a middle region with near-ANN accuracy, low latency, and substantial energy savings. The converted SNN preserves a pathway from ANN performance while carrying a higher spike count and a longer simulation window.

**Question.** Should convergence time be combined directly with inference latency?

**Answer.** They should appear in the same evaluation while retaining separate meanings. Convergence time measures how training develops across epochs; inference latency measures how quickly one deployed input receives a decision. Surrogate training can converge by around the twentieth epoch and later deliver decisions near 10 milliseconds. STDP can converge more slowly yet eventually produce sparse, low-energy inferences below 15 milliseconds under optimized conditions. A frequently retrained system may prioritize convergence, while a fixed deployed model may place greater weight on inference latency and energy.

A sound comparison therefore begins with the deployment situation, translates that situation into constraints, and then examines the surviving operating points across accuracy, latency, energy, spike count, and convergence. The metrics form a connected picture: spike activity helps explain energy use, simulation time shapes latency, training strategy influences convergence, and each efficiency gain must be interpreted alongside predictive performance. Model selection becomes the act of choosing the tradeoff whose shape matches the job.
