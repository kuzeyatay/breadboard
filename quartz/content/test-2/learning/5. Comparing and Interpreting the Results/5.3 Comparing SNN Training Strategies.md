## Comparing SNN Training Strategies

Accuracy, latency, energy, spike count, and convergence each reveal one part of an SNN's behavior. Choosing a training strategy requires bringing those parts together. Imagine selecting a network for a small edge device that must respond quickly while operating from a limited battery. A model with excellent accuracy may still be unsuitable if it needs a long simulation window, generates thousands of extra spikes, or takes too long to learn. The useful question therefore becomes: which combination of strengths best matches the job the network must perform?

A fair comparison begins by understanding what each metric contributes. **Accuracy** measures how often the model produces the correct prediction. **Latency** measures how long the model takes to produce that prediction. **Energy per inference** estimates the energy consumed for one prediction, measured here in millijoules, or mJ. **Spike count** records how many spike events the network generates during an inference. **Convergence** describes how quickly training reaches a stable level of performance. These metrics interact because the same mechanism that improves one can place pressure on another. For example, allowing an SNN to process an input over a longer simulation window gives it more time to accumulate evidence, yet the additional time steps can increase latency and create more spikes.

Surrogate-gradient training offers the strongest overall balance among the compared SNN strategies. A spiking neuron produces a discrete event when its internal state crosses a threshold. That abrupt threshold makes ordinary gradient-based learning difficult because a tiny change in the neuron's input often produces no change in its spike output, followed by a sudden jump when the threshold is crossed. Surrogate-gradient training uses a smoother approximation during learning so that useful error signals can pass through the threshold operation. The resulting network is trained directly around spiking behavior while still benefiting from gradient-based optimization.

In the reported comparison, a surrogate-gradient SNN reaches about 97.8% accuracy, coming within roughly 1-2 percentage points of ANN accuracy. It also reaches useful convergence by approximately the twentieth epoch and achieves latency as low as 10 ms. An **epoch** is one complete pass through the training data, so convergence by the twentieth epoch indicates that the model acquires its effective behavior relatively quickly. Its energy use is about 15 mJ per inference, with approximately 12,000 spikes generated for each inference.

Those values form a useful performance profile rather than five isolated facts. The high accuracy indicates that direct spiking training preserves strong predictive performance. The 10 ms latency indicates that the network can reach a decision over a short processing interval. The moderate spike count helps keep energy at 15 mJ, far below the approximately 200 mJ reported for the continuously operating ANN comparison. Taken together, these measurements make surrogate-gradient training a strong candidate when an application requires both rapid responses and accuracy close to an ANN.

ANN-to-SNN conversion begins from a different place. An ANN is trained using continuous activations, and its learned behavior is then represented through spikes in an SNN. This approach carries much of the ANN's predictive ability into the spiking model, which gives converted SNNs competitive accuracy. The conversion must express a continuous activation through spike activity over time. A larger activation is commonly represented by a higher spike rate, so the network may need to observe several time steps before the rate becomes clear enough to support a decision.

Consider a unit whose ANN activation represents strong evidence for a feature. In a converted SNN, that evidence may appear as many spikes distributed across a simulation window. The downstream neuron needs enough of those events to estimate the intended activation. A longer window can make that estimate more reliable, while also delaying the output and increasing the total number of spikes. The reported converted SNN generates roughly 20,000 spikes per inference and consumes about 20 mJ, both higher than the surrogate-gradient SNN's approximately 12,000 spikes and 15 mJ. Its longer simulation window also produces greater latency. Conversion is therefore attractive when preserving an already trained ANN's performance is a leading concern and the application can accommodate greater activity and processing time.

Spike-Timing Dependent Plasticity, or STDP, creates another performance profile. STDP adjusts connections using the relative timing of spikes. When activity in two neurons occurs with a relevant temporal relationship, the connection between them changes. Learning is therefore driven by local spike timing rather than a network-wide gradient. This local, timing-sensitive process encourages sparse activity: the network can develop useful responses while generating comparatively few events.

The STDP-based SNN produces about 4,000 spikes per inference and uses approximately 5 mJ, the lowest spike count and energy consumption among the compared strategies. Its accuracy is about 95.5%, and its convergence is slower than that of the surrogate-gradient model. The relationship among these measurements is easy to interpret. Sparse spiking means fewer events need to be processed, which corresponds to lower inference energy. The learning rule reaches its effective organization more gradually, and the resulting accuracy is lower than the surrogate-trained result. For a device whose main constraint is an extremely small energy budget, 5 mJ per inference may be more valuable than the additional accuracy provided by a more active model.

**Question.** Does the smallest spike count always identify the best SNN?

**Answer.** A small spike count is especially valuable when event processing dominates energy use, but model choice also depends on accuracy, latency, and learning behavior. The STDP-based SNN has the fewest spikes at about 4,000 and the lowest energy at about 5 mJ, while the surrogate-gradient SNN provides higher accuracy and faster convergence with about 12,000 spikes and 15 mJ. The preferred profile depends on whether the application gives greater weight to energy savings or predictive and training performance.

This type of comparison can be understood as a **Pareto-style tradeoff**. A model belongs on the useful tradeoff boundary when improving one of its metrics would require giving up ground on another. Moving from STDP to surrogate-gradient training raises accuracy from about 95.5% to about 97.8% and improves convergence, while energy rises from about 5 mJ to about 15 mJ and spike count rises from about 4,000 to about 12,000. The move buys predictive performance and training speed with additional activity and energy. Moving toward conversion can preserve competitive ANN-like performance, while requiring roughly 20,000 spikes, about 20 mJ, and a longer simulation window.

**Question.** How should two models be ranked when each wins on different metrics?

**Answer.** Rank them by the constraints of the intended application. A low-latency accuracy target favors the surrogate-gradient SNN because it combines about 97.8% accuracy with latency as low as 10 ms and convergence by around 20 epochs. An ultra-low-power setting favors the STDP-based SNN because it reduces energy to about 5 mJ and activity to roughly 4,000 spikes. A workflow centered on carrying an existing ANN into spiking computation may favor conversion despite its higher spike count and longer window.

The complete mental model is a set of connected choices. The training strategy shapes how the network learns; that learning process shapes when and how often neurons spike; spike activity and simulation duration shape latency and energy; and those constraints accompany the final accuracy and convergence behavior. Surrogate-gradient training occupies the balanced region, conversion emphasizes continuity with ANN performance, and STDP emphasizes sparse, energy-efficient activity. Model ranking emerges only after the application decides which of those resources is most precious.
