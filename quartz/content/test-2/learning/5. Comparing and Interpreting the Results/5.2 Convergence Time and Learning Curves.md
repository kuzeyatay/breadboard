## Convergence Time and Learning Curves

Once training loss and training accuracy have been recorded after each epoch, they form a history of how learning unfolds. Imagine watching three SNNs train for 20 epochs. At epoch 20, all three provide a final loss and accuracy, yet those endpoint values hide the path each model took. One model may improve rapidly during the early epochs and then settle into small refinements. Another may improve at a steady but slower pace. A third may make limited early progress and reach a plateau, where additional epochs produce only small changes. A **training curve** reveals these patterns by placing the epoch number on the horizontal axis and a measured quantity, such as loss or accuracy, on the vertical axis.

A **loss curve** tracks training loss across epochs. Training loss measures the error represented by the model's training objective, so a downward movement generally indicates that training is reducing that error. The shape of the decline adds information beyond the value at any single epoch. A steep downward segment shows that loss is being reduced quickly over that interval. A shallow segment shows slower improvement. A nearly flat segment indicates that the loss has stabilized around its current level during the displayed epochs.

The three trajectories below illustrate these different training characters over the same 20-epoch window.

![Training loss over 20 epochs for converted, surrogate-gradient, and STDP-based SNN models](/test-2/assets/source-visuals/2510-27379v1-page-10-graph-g1-training-loss-over-20-epochs-for-converted-surr.png)

*Training loss over 20 epochs for converted, surrogate-gradient, and STDP-based SNN models* *(p. 10)*

The surrogate-gradient SNN has the steepest and most consistent decrease in training loss. Its curve therefore describes rapid progress toward a lower-loss state during the observed training period, followed by increasing stability near the later epochs. The converted SNN shows a more moderate decline, meaning that its loss improves over the same period at a slower pace. The STDP-based SNN decreases more gradually and levels off at a higher loss. Reading the entire shape supports a stronger interpretation than comparing only the three values at epoch 20: the curves reveal when improvement occurs, how consistently it occurs, and where it begins to slow.

A useful analogy is three runners covering the same course while their positions are recorded once per minute. Their positions at the final recording tell you where they finish, while the full record tells you who accelerated early, who moved steadily, and who slowed before the end. Loss curves play the role of that full record. The vertical direction shows whether the measured error is decreasing, and the slope shows the pace of that decrease.

Each row is a snapshot taken after a particular epoch. Reading downward within one model's column shows how its loss changes as training continues. Reading across a row compares the models at the same stage of training. The surrogate-gradient column confirms the sharp and sustained reduction visible in the graph. The converted-SNN column confirms a moderate reduction, and the STDP-based column confirms the slower decrease and earlier plateau. The table also guards against a visual misreading caused by tightly spaced curves: the listed values provide the precise observations from which the plotted lines are formed.

**Question.** If two models have similar loss at epoch 20, can their training behavior still be meaningfully different?

**Answer.** Yes. One model may reach that loss early and remain stable, while the other may approach it gradually throughout all 20 epochs. Their endpoint losses are similar, but their curves describe different rates of learning. Comparing slopes, early progress, and stabilization reveals the difference.

Training accuracy provides a second view of the same learning process. An **accuracy learning curve** plots training accuracy against epoch. As the model learns to classify more training examples correctly, the curve rises. A steep rise represents rapid gains in accuracy, while a flatter region represents smaller gains from one epoch to the next. Accuracy curves are especially useful when training has a practical goal, such as reaching a chosen target accuracy.

![Training accuracy learning curves over 20 epochs for surrogate-gradient, converted, and STDP-based SNNs](/test-2/assets/source-visuals/2510-27379v1-page-11-graph-g1-training-accuracy-learning-curves-over-20-epochs.png)

*Training accuracy learning curves over 20 epochs for surrogate-gradient, converted, and STDP-based SNNs* *(p. 11)*

The surrogate-gradient curve rises more quickly, so it reaches a given attainable target earlier within the displayed range. The converted-SNN curve rises at a more moderate rate and reaches the same target later when its trajectory crosses that level. The STDP-based curve rises more slowly and stabilizes at a lower level. These shapes let us separate two measurements that answer different questions. **Convergence time** describes how early a model reaches a specified target, while **final accuracy** describes the accuracy recorded at the end of the chosen training window.

To calculate convergence time, imagine drawing a horizontal line across the accuracy graph at the target accuracy. The point where a model's curve first reaches or crosses that line determines its **minimum qualifying epoch**:
$$
E_{\mathrm{conv}}=\min \left\{e \mid A_{\mathrm{train}}(e)\geq A_{\mathrm{target}}\right\}
$$
Here, $e$ is an **epoch**, meaning one complete pass through the training data. $A_{\mathrm{train}}(e)$ is the training accuracy measured after epoch $e$. $A_{\mathrm{target}}$ is the selected target accuracy. The braces collect every epoch whose training accuracy reaches or exceeds the target, and $\min$ selects the earliest of those epochs. $E_{\mathrm{conv}}$ is the resulting convergence time, measured in epochs. The formula therefore computes the first epoch at which the target has been attained.

Suppose a target is set at 80% training accuracy. If a model records 78% after epoch 6 and 82% after epoch 7, then epoch 7 is its minimum qualifying epoch and its convergence time is 7 epochs. A model that reaches 80% at epoch 4 converges faster for that target. If the target is raised, both qualifying epochs may change, and a curve that never reaches the new level within the observed window has no qualifying epoch in that window.

**Question.** Does the model with the fastest convergence time always have the highest final accuracy?

**Answer.** Convergence time and final accuracy measure separate parts of the curve. A model can cross a moderate target early and then improve slowly, while another crosses that target later and finishes at a higher accuracy. A fair comparison therefore states both the chosen target and the final observed accuracy.

Loss and accuracy curves ultimately provide a paired mental model of training. Loss traces how the optimization objective is being reduced, accuracy traces how correct predictions accumulate, and the slope of either curve shows the pace of change. A target accuracy turns an accuracy curve into a measurable training milestone: draw the target level, find the earliest crossing, and report that epoch as the convergence time. Reading the trajectories together reveals whether an SNN learns quickly, improves steadily, or settles early within the displayed 20 epochs, while keeping speed of target attainment distinct from the performance reached at the end.
