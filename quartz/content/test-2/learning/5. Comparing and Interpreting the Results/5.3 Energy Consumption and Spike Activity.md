## Energy Consumption and Spike Activity

Having already met energy per inference, total spike count, and the three SNN training paradigms, we can now read them together as a profile of how each model performs its computation. Imagine choosing a network for a small battery-powered device that must classify one input at a time. Energy per inference tells you how much energy the device spends producing each prediction. Spike count tells you how many discrete events travel through the spiking network during that prediction. A useful comparison asks two linked questions: How expensive is one inference, and how much event activity produces it?

Energy per inference is the broader outcome. It captures the total energy associated with processing one input, usually reported here in millijoules, abbreviated mJ. Total spike count is a narrower activity measure: it counts the spikes generated during that inference. A model that communicates through fewer spikes has a **sparse inference profile**, meaning that only a relatively small number of spike events are needed to carry out the computation. Sparse activity often accompanies lower energy use because fewer events generally require fewer event-driven operations. Energy also reflects how the whole inference proceeds, including the kind of operations performed and how long spike propagation continues. Spike count therefore helps explain an energy result while energy per inference remains the direct measure of cost.

The paired trend becomes visible when the four evaluated approaches are placed side by side.

![Comparison of energy consumption and spike count per inference across ANN and SNN models](/test-2/assets/source-visuals/2510-27379v1-page-9-graph-g1-comparison-of-energy-consumption-and-spike-count.png)

*Comparison of energy consumption and spike count per inference across ANN and SNN models* *(p. 9)*

The graph shows a broad downward movement from the ANN CNN to the converted, surrogate-gradient, and STDP-based SNNs. The ANN CNN consumes about 200 mJ per inference. Its computation uses continuous-valued operations, so the spike count used to describe event-driven SNN activity does not provide the same kind of activity measure for this model. The high energy value establishes the reference point: dense continuous computation is considerably more expensive in this comparison than the event-driven alternatives.

ANN-to-SNN conversion reduces energy consumption to about 20 mJ per inference. Conversion begins with an ANN and represents its learned activations through spike activity. In the evaluated model, this produces roughly 20,000 spikes per inference. The model gains much of the energy advantage of spiking computation, yet its rate-coded propagation requires many spikes to represent activation strength. A stronger activation can require a higher firing rate over a simulation window, so information that previously occupied a continuous numerical value may be expressed through repeated events. The result is a large energy reduction accompanied by the highest reported spike count among the compared SNNs.

Surrogate-gradient training produces a different activity profile. A surrogate-gradient SNN is trained directly around spiking behavior while using a gradient approximation that makes optimization possible. It consumes about 15 mJ and generates roughly 12,000 spikes per inference. Compared with the converted SNN, it saves about 5 mJ while generating approximately 8,000 fewer spikes. Read together, these values indicate that directly learning spiking patterns leads to a sparser computation in this evaluation. The network reaches its output with fewer event transmissions, and its energy requirement falls alongside that reduction.

The STDP-based SNN occupies the sparsest and lowest-energy end of the graph. Spike-Timing Dependent Plasticity, or STDP, adjusts connections using the relative timing of spikes. The evaluated STDP model uses about 5 mJ per inference and generates roughly 4,000 spikes. Compared with the surrogate-gradient SNN, its spike count falls by about 8,000 and its energy falls by about 10 mJ. Compared with the converted SNN, it uses about one fifth of the energy and one fifth of the spikes. These paired reductions form a clear sparse inference profile: relatively little event activity accompanies a very low inference cost. The broader evaluation associates this efficiency with a slight reduction in accuracy, so STDP is especially attractive when the energy budget carries greater weight than obtaining the highest available predictive performance.

Reading across each row prevents a misleading ranking based on a single column. The model or training-paradigm column identifies how the computation was obtained. The energy column gives the measured cost of producing one inference. The spike-count column reveals the amount of event activity behind that result for the SNNs. Converted SNNs pair a major energy improvement with comparatively dense spike traffic. Surrogate-gradient SNNs pair low energy with a moderate spike count. STDP-based SNNs pair the lowest energy with the fewest spikes. Together, the graph and table show that all three SNN approaches greatly reduce energy relative to the ANN CNN, while their internal activity profiles remain meaningfully different.

**Question.** If the converted SNN already uses only about 20 mJ per inference, why should its roughly 20,000 spikes concern us?

**Answer.** The 20 mJ result confirms a large energy improvement over the roughly 200 mJ ANN CNN. The spike count adds information about how that improvement is achieved. Roughly 20,000 events indicate substantial activity, consistent with rate-coded propagation and a longer period of repeated spiking. A surrogate-gradient SNN reaches about 15 mJ with roughly 12,000 spikes, while the STDP-based SNN reaches about 5 mJ with roughly 4,000. For an energy-constrained choice, the converted model's spike count reveals additional room for reducing inference activity.

The relationship between activity and cost can be understood as a tradeoff rather than a fixed conversion rule. More spike events generally mean more event-driven processing, so a declining spike count gives a plausible explanation for declining energy across these SNNs. The exact energy associated with an inference also depends on how those events participate in the complete computation. Two models can therefore have different energy costs even when their spike counts are similar, and a spike count alone cannot substitute for an energy measurement. The paired values answer different parts of the same engineering question.

**Question.** Which model has the best energy-spike profile in this comparison?

**Answer.** The STDP-based SNN has the sparsest measured activity, at roughly 4,000 spikes, and the lowest reported energy, at about 5 mJ per inference. The surrogate-gradient SNN provides a middle profile of about 12,000 spikes and 15 mJ while remaining close to ANN accuracy in the broader evaluation. Model selection therefore depends on the intended balance: STDP favors ultra-low-energy operation, surrogate-gradient training balances energy efficiency with stronger predictive performance, and conversion offers a practical route from an ANN while retaining heavier spike activity.

A reliable mental model is to picture each inference as a job with both a final bill and an activity trace. Energy per inference is the bill. Spike count is the number of event signals used while completing the job. The converted SNN lowers the bill while remaining event-heavy, the surrogate-gradient SNN learns a leaner path, and the STDP-based SNN performs the sparsest computation at the lowest measured cost. Reading both measures together turns a simple energy ranking into an explanation of how each training paradigm shapes inference activity.
