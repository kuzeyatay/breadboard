## Accuracy and Energy Tradeoffs

Suppose an image classifier must choose between four models. One produces the most correct answers, while another uses one-fortieth as much energy for each prediction. Choosing by accuracy alone favors the first model; choosing by energy alone favors the second. A useful decision therefore needs both measurements at once: how often the model predicts correctly and how much energy it spends doing so.

The four choices here are a conventional artificial neural network (ANN), an ANN converted into a spiking neural network, an SNN trained with surrogate gradients, and an SNN trained through spike-timing-dependent plasticity (STDP). They differ in training strategy and in how they generate activity during inference. Those differences affect both classification accuracy and computational cost.

Classification accuracy is the percentage of examples assigned the correct label. If a model classifies 978 out of 1,000 images correctly, its accuracy is 97.8%. Normalized energy consumption expresses energy relative to a reference rather than requiring every comparison to begin with raw units. When the ANN is treated as the reference level, lower normalized values indicate that a model consumes a smaller fraction of the ANN's energy. The two metrics point in preferred directions: higher accuracy is desirable, while lower energy is desirable.

The reported comparison places these measurements beside one another for MNIST and CIFAR-10. MNIST contains relatively simple images of handwritten digits, while CIFAR-10 contains more varied natural images divided into ten classes. Because the datasets present different classification challenges, the accuracy column must always be read together with its dataset label. An accuracy result on MNIST answers a different experimental question from an accuracy result on CIFAR-10.

Read the table across each model row rather than ranking one column in isolation. The ANN row establishes the high-accuracy, high-energy reference. The converted SNN preserves much of the ANN's classification performance while substantially reducing energy consumption. The surrogate-gradient SNN also stays close to ANN-level accuracy and lowers energy further. The STDP-based SNN reaches the smallest energy value, accompanied by a larger reduction in accuracy.

The energy values make the size of this exchange concrete. The ANN consumes about 200 mJ per inference. The converted SNN uses about 20 mJ, the surrogate-gradient SNN about 15 mJ, and the STDP-based SNN about 5 mJ. Relative to the 200 mJ ANN reference, these correspond to normalized energy levels of approximately 1, 0.10, 0.075, and 0.025. In practical terms, the converted model uses about one-tenth of the reference energy, the surrogate-gradient model uses about three-fortieths, and the STDP model uses about one-fortieth.

Consider 1,000 inferences under the same reported energy assumptions. The ANN would consume about 200,000 mJ, or 200 J. The converted SNN would consume about 20 J, the surrogate-gradient SNN about 15 J, and the STDP-based SNN about 5 J. A small per-inference difference becomes a large accumulated difference when a system repeatedly processes data. Energy per prediction therefore belongs beside accuracy whenever the model will operate under a power budget or perform many inferences.

The accuracy side has a different shape. The ANN leads in raw accuracy, while the surrogate-gradient SNN remains close to it; a reported integrated result places the surrogate model around 97.8% and within roughly one to two percentage points of ANN accuracy. The STDP-based model reaches about 95.5% in that comparison while consuming only about 5 mJ per inference. Moving from the surrogate-gradient model to the STDP model therefore saves about 10 mJ per prediction, reducing energy by roughly two-thirds, while accepting a decrease of about 2.3 percentage points in accuracy.

The grouped bars reveal why a single ranking fails. Accuracy bars favor the ANN and the closely performing surrogate-gradient SNN, whereas normalized-energy bars favor the STDP-based SNN. The converted SNN occupies an intermediate position: it retains competitive accuracy and sharply lowers energy, yet it consumes more than the directly trained spiking alternatives. The surrogate-gradient model forms a particularly balanced point because its accuracy remains near the top while its energy falls from about 200 mJ to 15 mJ. The STDP model extends the energy saving to 5 mJ by moving farther away from the highest accuracy.

This is a **multi-axis result interpretation**: a model's position is judged along more than one measurement. The preferred position depends on how strongly the application values each axis. A system that prioritizes correct classifications may accept the ANN's energy cost or choose the surrogate-gradient SNN for near-ANN accuracy at much lower energy. A severely energy-constrained system may prefer the STDP-based SNN because each inference costs only a small fraction of the ANN reference. A converted SNN may suit a setting that values retaining ANN-like behavior while gaining a substantial energy reduction.

**Question.** If the surrogate-gradient SNN is both accurate and energy efficient, why would anyone select the STDP-based SNN?

**Answer.** The STDP-based SNN reduces reported energy from about 15 mJ to about 5 mJ per inference. That difference can dominate the decision when power is tightly limited or predictions occur repeatedly over a long operating period. The surrogate-gradient SNN becomes preferable when preserving more classification accuracy carries greater value than obtaining the final 10 mJ reduction.

Dataset-dependent comparison adds another layer. MNIST and CIFAR-10 should be inspected separately because model behavior can change with the difficulty and structure of the data. A model's strong MNIST accuracy does not determine its CIFAR-10 accuracy, and an accuracy gap has meaning only beside the energy paid to achieve it. The table supports comparing each model across the two datasets and then pairing those accuracies with the same reported energy measure. That reading shows whether an energy-saving strategy retains its classification quality as the task changes.

**Question.** Do these MNIST and CIFAR-10 results prove that one training strategy is universally best?

**Answer.** They support a comparison among the four models within the reported experimental setup. They do not establish a universal ordering for every dataset, implementation, or application. The evidence shows a recurring decision pattern within these benchmarks: ANN accuracy comes with the largest energy cost, surrogate-gradient training offers a strong balance, conversion provides substantial savings with competitive performance, and STDP reaches the lowest energy while giving up more accuracy.

The complete mental model is a map rather than a leaderboard. One direction on the map represents increasing classification accuracy; another represents decreasing energy consumption; MNIST and CIFAR-10 provide separate views of how the models occupy that map. The ANN sits near the accuracy-focused end, the STDP-based SNN near the energy-focused end, and the converted and surrogate-gradient SNNs occupy intermediate tradeoff positions. Model selection follows from choosing the region that matches the application's priorities, then checking that the same balance remains acceptable on the dataset the model must actually classify.
