Imagine choosing an SNN for a small robot that must recognize an obstacle, decide quickly, and operate for hours on limited energy. Earlier comparisons gave us several measurements: classification accuracy tells us how often the network predicts correctly; inference latency measures the delay from input to decision; energy per inference estimates the cost of one decision; spike count records how much event-driven activity the network produces; and convergence time describes how quickly training reaches stable performance. Model selection begins when these measurements are considered together rather than treated as separate scoreboards.

Each metric answers a different practical question. Accuracy asks whether the decisions are dependable. Latency asks whether they arrive soon enough to be useful. Energy per inference asks how quickly repeated decisions consume the available power budget. Spike count asks how much communication and computation the network triggers while processing an input. Convergence asks how much training effort is needed before the model becomes usable. A model can perform strongly on one dimension while imposing costs on another, so a defensible choice starts by deciding which costs the application can tolerate.

Consider two SNNs that achieve similar classification accuracy. One reaches a decision after a short simulation window, while the other needs a longer window in which more spikes accumulate. Their final predictions may look equally good in an accuracy column, yet their deployment behavior differs. The shorter-window model responds sooner and may generate less activity before deciding. The longer-window model delays the response and may require more event processing. Accuracy alone hides both differences.

A useful way to reason about the choice is to treat it as a multi-metric selection problem. Begin with the application's binding constraints: the requirements that cannot be relaxed without making the system ineffective. A robot reacting to a moving object has a strict response-time constraint. A battery-powered sensor operating remotely has a strict energy constraint. A system trained under limited computing resources may need rapid convergence. Once those constraints are clear, the metrics can be ranked by practical importance.

Surrogate-gradient training is a strong candidate when predictive performance and fast response carry high priority. A spike is a discrete event, which makes its exact derivative difficult to use during gradient-based learning. Surrogate-gradient methods replace that difficult training signal with a smooth approximation, allowing the network to be optimized end to end. In the reported comparison, surrogate-trained SNNs approached ANN accuracy within about one to two percentage points, converged rapidly by the twentieth epoch, and reached latency as low as 10 milliseconds. Those properties fit applications that need accurate decisions with limited delay.

Latency becomes especially meaningful in robotics and sensory processing because an input can change while the network is still deciding. At 10 milliseconds per decision, a system can potentially respond on a much shorter timescale than one requiring a long simulation window, assuming the surrounding sensing and control pipeline can keep pace. The latency number therefore represents more than computational speed: it limits how old the sensory evidence becomes before an action is selected.

ANN-to-SNN conversion serves a different priority. Conversion begins with a trained conventional neural network and maps its continuous activations into spiking activity. This route can preserve competitive predictive performance, making it attractive when an effective ANN already exists and retaining its accuracy is central. The conversion process commonly represents activation strength through spike rates. A larger activation becomes more spikes over a period of time, so the network may need a longer simulation window to estimate that rate reliably.

That mechanism explains why converted SNNs can carry higher spike counts and longer latency. The reported comparison associates converted models with roughly 20,000 spikes per inference and about 20 millijoules of energy. More spikes create more event traffic, while a longer observation period postpones the decision. Conversion can therefore fit an accuracy-focused transition from an established ANN pipeline, provided the deployment can support the resulting activity and simulation time.

STDP, or spike-timing-dependent plasticity, shifts attention toward timing-based adaptation and sparse operation. STDP changes a connection according to the relative timing of spikes in connected neurons. Repeated timing relationships gradually shape the network, allowing useful patterns to emerge through local activity. This learning process is particularly relevant when unsupervised adaptation and very low energy use matter more than reaching the highest available classification accuracy.

The measured tradeoff is concrete: the STDP-based SNN used about 5 millijoules per inference and generated roughly 4,000 spikes, the lowest values among the compared models, while showing slightly reduced accuracy and slower convergence. Sparse firing means fewer events must be communicated and processed. For an energy-constrained sensor that observes its environment continuously, saving energy on every inference can accumulate into a substantial operating advantage. Slower training may be acceptable when training occurs infrequently, while sparse inference affects every decision made after deployment.

**Question.** If lower spike count usually supports lower energy use, why measure both?

**Answer.** Spike count measures network activity, while energy per inference measures the resulting energy cost. The two are related because processing fewer events can reduce work, but energy also depends on where and how those events are executed. Recording both reveals whether sparse activity actually produces the expected deployment benefit. It also prevents spike count from being used as an unsupported substitute for a direct energy measurement.

These tradeoffs lead naturally toward neuromorphic hardware: computing platforms designed to support event-driven spiking operation. Platforms such as IBM TrueNorth and Intel Loihi motivate SNN deployment because they align computation with discrete spike events. When activity is sparse, the platform can focus work on the events that occur. An SNN selected for low spike count and low energy therefore has a deployment story that extends from its learning rule to its event-driven execution.

Hardware fit must remain part of the choice. Limited standardization across neuromorphic platforms complicates portable deployment because models, supported operations, and performance measurements may not transfer uniformly between systems. A result obtained in one setup cannot automatically guarantee the same latency or energy behavior elsewhere. Selection should therefore include the intended platform, the available execution path, and whether the measured advantages survive deployment on that target.

Scalable training creates a related constraint before deployment begins. As SNN tasks and models grow, training must remain practical in computation, time, and optimization behavior. Surrogate gradients provide effective end-to-end learning, conversion draws on established ANN training, and STDP offers local timing-based adaptation, yet each places different demands on the training process. Convergence measurements help expose those demands: rapid convergence can shorten experimentation, while slow convergence may limit how many large-scale models can be trained and evaluated.

**Question.** Which training strategy should be chosen when every metric matters?

**Answer.** Start with minimum acceptable accuracy and maximum tolerable latency, energy, and training cost. Eliminate candidates that violate any binding limit, then compare the remaining models using the priorities of the application. Surrogate-gradient training fits low-latency accuracy targets; conversion fits cases where preserving an existing ANN's performance is central and longer windows are acceptable; STDP fits sparse, ultra-low-power, timing-based adaptation when slower convergence and some accuracy reduction can be accepted.

The resulting mental model is a chain from application to metric, from metric to training strategy, and from training strategy to hardware behavior. Accuracy determines whether the prediction is useful, latency determines whether it arrives in time, energy and spike count determine whether repeated inference is sustainable, and convergence determines whether training can scale. Neuromorphic hardware can turn event-driven sparsity into practical efficiency, while platform differences require that those gains be checked on the intended deployment target. The strongest SNN choice is therefore the model whose complete pattern of costs and capabilities fits the job it must perform.
