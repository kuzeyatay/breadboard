## Latency Across ANN and SNN Approaches

Once inference latency has been defined as the time between presenting an input and receiving a model decision, the practical question becomes comparative: which approach responds fastest under the same evaluation conditions? Imagine a device that must recognize an event while it is unfolding. A decision arriving after the useful response window has closed provides little practical value, even when the classification itself is correct. The device therefore has a **response-time constraint**: a maximum delay within which inference must finish.

Suppose that constraint is 15 milliseconds per sample. A model completing inference in 10 milliseconds fits inside the available window with 5 milliseconds to spare. A model requiring 20 milliseconds exceeds the window by 5 milliseconds. This comparison turns latency from an isolated number into an operational judgment. Lower latency means the decision becomes available sooner, while the response-time constraint determines whether that speed is sufficient for the intended setting.

The reported comparison includes four approaches: a conventional convolutional artificial neural network, or ANN; an ANN converted into a spiking neural network; an SNN trained directly with surrogate gradients; and an SNN trained through spike-timing-dependent plasticity, or STDP. These names describe different routes to producing a decision. The converted SNN begins with an ANN and translates its activations into spike-based behavior. The surrogate-gradient SNN learns its spiking behavior directly through a gradient-based training method. The STDP-based SNN adapts connections using the relative timing of spikes. Here, those mechanisms matter because they lead to different amounts of time before enough computation has occurred to produce an inference.

The ANN records a latency of approximately 30 milliseconds. The converted SNN reduces that value to about 20 milliseconds. The surrogate-gradient SNN reaches approximately 10 milliseconds, while the STDP-based SNN completes inference in about 15 milliseconds. Reading the table by rows allows a direct comparison because every entry uses the same quantity: elapsed milliseconds per inference. A millisecond is one thousandth of a second, so 10 milliseconds equals 0.010 seconds and 30 milliseconds equals 0.030 seconds.

Those fractions of a second may sound uniformly small, yet their relationship is substantial. In this comparison, the surrogate-gradient SNN responds about 20 milliseconds sooner than the ANN. It also takes roughly one-third of the ANN's reported inference time. The STDP-based SNN responds about 15 milliseconds sooner than the ANN, while the converted SNN responds about 10 milliseconds sooner. These differences describe decision availability: the surrogate-gradient result becomes available earliest, followed by the STDP-based result, the converted-SNN result, and then the ANN result.

The tallest bar belongs to the ANN at roughly 30 milliseconds, and the shortest belongs to the surrogate-gradient SNN at roughly 10 milliseconds. The converted SNN sits between them at about 20 milliseconds. Its longer delay is consistent with the need for a longer simulation window to represent ANN activation levels through rates of spike events. The STDP-based SNN reaches about 15 milliseconds. Its bar remains shorter than those of the converted SNN and ANN, while its latency is slightly higher than that of the surrogate-gradient SNN. The associated interpretation is that gradual spike adaptation can delay early responses before behavior stabilizes under optimized conditions.

Return to the imagined 15-millisecond response-time constraint. The surrogate-gradient SNN fits comfortably within it. The STDP-based SNN reaches the boundary. The converted SNN and ANN exceed it. If the constraint were relaxed to 25 milliseconds, both the converted SNN and the two directly trained SNN approaches would fit, while the ANN's approximately 30-millisecond result would remain outside the allowed window. Model suitability therefore depends on both sides of the comparison: the measured inference time and the maximum delay the task can tolerate.

**Question.** Does the 10-millisecond surrogate-gradient result mean every surrogate-gradient SNN will always respond in 10 milliseconds?

**Answer.** The 10-millisecond value is a reported experimental result under the evaluated conditions. Inference latency can vary with the model's complexity, the hardware configuration, the data being processed, the simulation window, and other details of the evaluation protocol. The supported claim is narrower: the surrogate-gradient SNN was the fastest of these four reported model results, reaching approximately 10 milliseconds in this comparison. A prediction about another system requires measurements from that system or evidence that its relevant conditions are comparable.

This boundary is especially useful when interpreting statements such as "SNNs can achieve low latency." Reported evaluations on event-based datasets include SNN latencies as low as roughly 5-10 milliseconds per sample on neuromorphic platforms, while CNN-based ANNs commonly fall in a broader range of roughly 20-50 milliseconds depending on model complexity and hardware. These ranges support the possibility of faster SNN inference in suitable configurations. They preserve variation between models and platforms, so they provide context for the four plotted values without turning one measurement into a universal performance guarantee.

**Question.** Can the latency chart alone establish which model is best?

**Answer.** The chart establishes a response-time ordering for the reported experiment. It supports choosing the surrogate-gradient SNN when the immediate requirement is the lowest measured latency among these four results. A complete model choice also depends on the task's required performance and other separately measured constraints. Latency answers one precise question: how long the system waits for a decision.

The resulting mental model is a race against a deadline. Each model starts when the input arrives, performs inference through its own computational process, and finishes when a usable decision appears. The ANN reaches the finish at about 30 milliseconds, the converted SNN at about 20, the STDP-based SNN at about 15, and the surrogate-gradient SNN at about 10. The response-time constraint draws a deadline across that race, separating results that arrive in time from those that arrive too late. The ordering describes this experiment, while the deadline gives the ordering practical meaning and the evaluation context defines how far the conclusion can travel.
