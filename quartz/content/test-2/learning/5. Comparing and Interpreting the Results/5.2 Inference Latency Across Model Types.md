## Inference Latency Across Model Types

Decision latency becomes useful when a model must turn incoming activity into an answer before that answer loses value. Imagine a gesture-recognition system receiving events from a camera as a hand begins to move. The model accumulates enough evidence to classify the gesture, then produces its decision. A response after roughly 10 milliseconds can support a more immediate interaction than one requiring 20 milliseconds or longer. Comparing models therefore requires asking how quickly each model gathers sufficient evidence, rather than relying on the broad labels "ANN" and "SNN."

An artificial neural network, or ANN, usually processes continuous-valued activations through a fixed sequence of layers. Its inference latency depends on the amount of computation in those layers and the hardware performing it. In the evaluated CNN-based ANNs, decisions typically require about 20-50 milliseconds per sample. That range reflects differences in model complexity and hardware configuration: a larger model or a less suitable processor can take longer even when the computational method remains the same.

A spiking neural network, or SNN, processes discrete spike events over time. Its latency includes a temporal requirement that deserves careful attention. The network may need to run for several simulation steps while spikes travel, membrane states accumulate evidence, and output activity becomes decisive. The duration represented by those steps is the **simulation window**. A short window can yield an early decision, while a longer window gives the network more spike observations from which to estimate its output. Consequently, the training paradigm shapes latency by determining how efficiently the useful information is expressed in time.

Reading the table by training paradigm reveals a practical latency ranking: the directly trained surrogate-gradient SNN responds fastest, the optimized STDP-based SNN follows, the converted SNN requires more time, and the ANN baseline can require the longest time depending on its model and hardware configuration.

The converted SNN's latency follows from how conversion works. ANN-to-SNN conversion begins with an ANN whose activation values carry information as numerical magnitudes. The conversion process represents those magnitudes through spike activity, often using spike rates. A larger ANN activation may become a higher firing rate, while a smaller activation becomes a lower firing rate. The SNN must observe enough spikes to distinguish those rates reliably. For example, observing two spike trains for only a very brief interval may make rates of "moderately high" and "slightly higher" look similar. Extending the simulation window supplies more events and produces a more stable approximation of the original ANN activations. That additional evidence improves the rate estimate while adding simulation-time cost, which helps explain the converted model's latency of about 20 milliseconds.

Surrogate-gradient training takes a different route. It trains the spiking network directly, using a gradient approximation that allows learning despite the abrupt, discrete nature of spike generation. Direct training can shape when useful spikes occur, allowing the network to produce informative activity earlier in the simulation. The evaluated surrogate-gradient SNN reaches a decision at about 10 milliseconds while maintaining competitive accuracy. Its speed therefore comes from a learned temporal response: early spikes can carry enough task-relevant evidence for the output to settle quickly.

STDP, or spike-timing-dependent plasticity, changes connections according to the relative timing of spikes. In the evaluated STDP-based model, gradual spike adaptation makes the early inference phase somewhat slower than that of the surrogate-gradient model. Under optimized conditions, its decision latency stabilizes below about 15 milliseconds. This remains a rapid response, while its position behind the surrogate-trained model shows how learning dynamics can affect when decisive evidence appears.

**Question.** If an SNN processes sparse spike events, why can a converted SNN still take about 20 milliseconds to decide?

**Answer.** Sparse event processing and short decision time describe different properties. A converted SNN can perform relatively little work during each simulation step yet still require many steps to estimate firing rates accurately. Its total decision latency includes the full simulation window needed to accumulate those spikes. The longer window explains why its latency can approach that of the faster ANN cases even though its internal activity is spike-based.

Latency-sensitive model choice depends on the application's timing limit. A system requiring the earliest reliable response would favor the surrogate-gradient SNN in this evaluation because its latency is approximately 10 milliseconds. An application that can allow a few additional milliseconds while prioritizing sparse activity may find the STDP-based model suitable. A converted SNN may be attractive when retaining behavior learned by an ANN is valuable and a roughly 20-millisecond simulation window is acceptable. ANN latency remains dependent on model complexity and hardware, with the evaluated range extending from about 20 to 50 milliseconds.

**Question.** Does the lowest measured latency automatically make the surrogate-gradient SNN the best model?

**Answer.** Model choice combines latency with the required accuracy, energy use, adaptation style, and deployment conditions. The surrogate-gradient SNN offers the strongest latency result here and competitive accuracy, making it a strong candidate for time-sensitive inference. Other models can suit different priorities: STDP supports unsupervised adaptation and sparse activity, while conversion preserves a path from an already trained ANN. The latency ranking identifies how quickly each evaluated model decides; the application determines how much that speed should influence the final choice.

A useful mental model is to picture inference as an evidence-collection process. An ANN moves continuous values through its layers, a converted SNN watches spike rates until they approximate those values, a surrogate-gradient SNN learns to place useful evidence early, and an STDP-based SNN lets timing-driven activity stabilize. Inference latency records when that evidence becomes sufficient for a decision, while simulation-time cost explains why event-driven computation can still require a longer wait.
