## Training Loss, Accuracy, and Convergence

The three training paradigms-ANN-to-SNN conversion, surrogate-gradient training, and STDP-based learning-produce spiking networks through different learning processes. Once those processes are understood, their training curves reveal how quickly each model improves and when its improvement begins to settle. Imagine watching three runners approach the same checkpoint over 20 time steps. Their final positions provide useful information, but the paths they take reveal much more: one may advance rapidly and level off, another may improve steadily, and a third may make only gradual progress. A **learning curve** plays the same role for a trained model. Its horizontal axis records training epochs, while its vertical axis records a measured quantity such as training loss or training accuracy.

An **epoch** represents one complete pass through the training data. After each epoch, the model's current behavior is measured, producing another point on the curve. Connecting these points shows the direction and rate of learning. A steep improvement means that a large change occurred over a small number of epochs. A flatter segment means that additional epochs are producing smaller changes. When the curve settles enough to satisfy a chosen performance criterion, the model is said to have **converged** under that criterion. Its **convergence time** is the earliest epoch at which the criterion is met.

Training loss measures how strongly the model's outputs disagree with the desired training outcomes according to its learning objective. Lower loss represents closer agreement with that objective. The shape of a loss curve therefore shows how effectively training is reducing the model's error signal. A sharp downward slope indicates rapid reduction, a gentle downward slope indicates slower reduction, and a plateau indicates that the measured loss is changing very little across successive epochs.

The loss trajectories over the displayed 20 epochs make the three behaviors visible at once:

*Training loss over 20 epochs for converted, surrogate-gradient, and STDP-based SNN models* *(p. 10)*

The surrogate-gradient curve descends most rapidly and consistently. Its steep decline means that each early epoch removes a comparatively large portion of the measured training loss. As training proceeds, the curve becomes flatter and stabilizes around the twentieth epoch. Within this 20-epoch observation window, that shape supports the interpretation that surrogate-gradient training reaches a settled region faster than the other SNN strategies.

The converted SNN follows a more moderate trajectory. Its loss improves across training, but its descent is less rapid than the surrogate-gradient trajectory. Visually, it occupies the middle convergence pattern: meaningful progress occurs, yet the model approaches its later loss level more gradually. The STDP-based SNN changes most slowly and plateaus at a higher loss. Its shallow trajectory indicates that successive epochs yield smaller reductions in the measured objective, so its convergence behavior is slower over the same displayed interval.

Overlapping lines can make exact comparisons difficult, especially when two curves lie close together. The tabulated values provide an epoch-by-epoch check:

![Training loss values across 20 epochs for converted, surrogate-gradient, and STDP-based SNN models](/test-2/assets/source-visuals/2510-27379v1-page-10-table-t1-training-loss-values-across-20-epochs-for-conver.png)

*Training loss values across 20 epochs for converted, surrogate-gradient, and STDP-based SNN models* *(p. 10)*

Each row fixes the epoch, and the three model columns report the corresponding losses. Reading across a row answers, "Which strategy has the lowest loss at this particular stage of training?" Reading downward within one column answers, "How much does this strategy continue to improve as epochs pass?" This dual reading prevents a common visual error: treating a small gap between plotted lines as meaningless simply because the graph compresses the vertical scale. The table confirms the pattern carried by the graph-the surrogate-gradient values fall most strongly, the converted values decline at a moderate pace, and the STDP-based values change more slowly and settle higher.

**Question.** If the STDP loss curve becomes nearly flat, has the STDP-based SNN converged?

**Answer.** A nearly flat curve indicates stabilization under the measured loss: additional epochs within the displayed range produce little change. The level of that plateau also carries information. STDP stabilizes at a higher loss than the other strategies, so its curve represents slow improvement followed by limited further reduction. Convergence describes satisfying a stated criterion; the flat shape supplies evidence for a stability criterion, while a target-loss criterion would require checking whether the plateau actually reaches the chosen target.

Training accuracy asks a different question. It measures the proportion of training predictions that are correct, so higher accuracy represents better performance on that measure. Loss reflects the value of the training objective, while accuracy counts correct outcomes. Their curves often move in complementary directions-loss downward and accuracy upward-yet they should be read separately because a change in loss can occur without an equal-sized change in the number of correct predictions. A model may become more confident in predictions it already gets right, lowering loss while accuracy changes only slightly.

The accuracy learning curves reveal how quickly those improvements become correct training predictions:

![Training accuracy learning curves over 20 epochs for surrogate-gradient, converted, and STDP-based SNNs](/test-2/assets/source-visuals/2510-27379v1-page-11-graph-g1-training-accuracy-learning-curves-over-20-epochs.png)

*Training accuracy learning curves over 20 epochs for surrogate-gradient, converted, and STDP-based SNNs* *(p. 11)*

The surrogate-gradient SNN rises faster toward its later accuracy level. Its rapid early climb agrees with the steep reduction seen in its loss curve: training quickly improves both the objective value and the proportion of correct outcomes. By approximately epoch 20, its accuracy curve has approached a stable region, supporting the reported convergence description within the observed window. "Approximately epoch 20" should be read as an interpretation of this particular curve and criterion rather than as a fixed property of every surrogate-trained SNN.

The converted SNN's accuracy increases more moderately, matching its intermediate loss trajectory. The STDP-based curve rises more slowly, which reinforces the higher, earlier loss plateau seen in the loss comparison. Together, the two graphs provide mutually supporting evidence about relative convergence behavior: surrogate-gradient training improves fastest over the shown epochs, conversion produces moderate improvement, and STDP progresses more gradually.

A target-accuracy criterion makes the comparison precise. Choose a horizontal accuracy threshold, then locate the earliest epoch at which each curve reaches it. That epoch is the model's convergence time for the selected target. Raising the threshold can delay the qualifying epoch or leave a strategy without a qualifying point inside the 20-epoch range. Lowering it can make several strategies appear to converge earlier. Convergence therefore combines a curve with a declared rule; the visual impression of "looking stable" becomes a reproducible measurement.

**Question.** Can the model with the fastest-falling loss automatically be declared the most accurate?

**Answer.** Loss reduction shows rapid progress on the training objective, while accuracy records how many predictions are correct. The accuracy graph must supply the accuracy comparison. Here, the surrogate-gradient SNN combines the steepest loss decline with the fastest accuracy rise, so both measures support faster convergence over the displayed 20 epochs. The conclusion comes from reading the measures together while preserving the distinct meaning of each one.

A reliable mental model is to treat training as motion traced from two viewpoints. The loss curve shows how quickly disagreement with the learning objective shrinks; the accuracy curve shows how quickly correct predictions accumulate. Curve slope reveals the rate of change, a plateau reveals stabilization, and a target threshold turns convergence into an earliest qualifying epoch. Read this way, the reported curves form a coherent picture: surrogate-gradient training approaches its settled performance rapidly by about epoch 20, converted SNN training follows a moderate path, and STDP-based training advances more slowly and stabilizes at a less favorable loss level within the same observation window.
