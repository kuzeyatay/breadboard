## Training Loss, Accuracy, and Convergence

The three training paradigms developed earlier-ANN-to-SNN conversion, surrogate-gradient training, and STDP-based learning-produce models that change at different rates during training. Imagine checking each model after every epoch, where one epoch means one complete pass through the training data. A single final score would reveal where each model finished, yet it would hide the route taken to get there. To understand whether learning was rapid, gradual, smooth, or stalled, we need a record of performance throughout training. That record is called a **learning curve**.

Training loss provides one such curve. Loss is a numerical measure of how poorly the model's current outputs match the desired behavior. A large loss indicates substantial error under the chosen training objective; a smaller loss indicates that the model has adjusted its internal parameters toward outputs rewarded by that objective. Plotting loss after every epoch creates a training-loss trajectory. Its slope shows the pace of improvement, while its shape reveals whether progress continues steadily or begins to level off.

Consider a loss curve that falls sharply during the opening epochs and then becomes nearly horizontal. The steep early fall means the training process quickly found parameter changes that reduced error. The later flattening means each additional epoch produces a smaller improvement. This flattening is often described as stabilization or a plateau. A plateau at a low loss suggests that the model has settled into a comparatively effective solution. A plateau at a higher loss suggests that learning has stabilized while leaving more of the training objective unsatisfied.

The three trajectories below make these patterns visible across the full 20-epoch run.

![Training loss over 20 epochs for converted, surrogate-gradient, and STDP-based SNN models](/test-2/assets/source-visuals/2510-27379v1-page-10-graph-g1-training-loss-over-20-epochs-for-converted-surr.png)

*Training loss over 20 epochs for converted, surrogate-gradient, and STDP-based SNN models* *(p. 10)*

The surrogate-gradient SNN shows the steepest and most consistent decline. Its loss drops rapidly in the early epochs and continues moving toward a lower level as training proceeds, stabilizing by about the twentieth epoch. That combination-strong early reduction followed by continued progress-indicates high **optimization speed**, meaning that the training method efficiently finds parameter updates that improve its objective.

Surrogate gradients enable this behavior by supplying a usable training signal around spike-generation events. The resulting updates can coordinate many parameters toward the classification objective. The curve's smooth, sustained descent suggests that these updates remain productive over much of the run rather than exhausting their useful direction after only a few epochs.

The converted SNN follows a more moderate trajectory. Conversion begins from a conventionally trained network whose activations have been translated into spiking behavior, so the resulting SNN already inherits useful structure. Its loss still changes across the epochs because the spiking representation and simulation behavior must settle into effective operation. The curve declines more slowly than the surrogate-gradient curve, showing that useful improvement occurs while requiring more of the 20-epoch interval to approach stability.

The STDP-based SNN improves at the slowest rate and reaches a plateau earlier at a higher loss. STDP changes connections from the relative timing of spikes, so its updates respond to local temporal relationships rather than directly following the classification error. The early plateau shows that these local adjustments quickly reach a state where later epochs produce limited additional reduction in the measured training loss. Its curve therefore represents slower optimization under this objective and less complete reduction of the error by the end of the observed run.

Each row associates an epoch with three loss values: one for the converted SNN, one for the surrogate-gradient SNN, and one for the STDP-based SNN. Reading down a single model column reveals how its error changes with training time. Reading across a row compares all three models at the same stage of training. The values verify the shapes visible in the graph: surrogate-gradient loss falls fastest and most consistently, converted-SNN loss decreases at a moderate pace, and STDP-based loss changes more slowly before remaining near a higher level.

This distinction between early and final performance is essential. At an early epoch, one model may already have reduced much of its loss while another still has substantial improvement ahead. By epoch 20, both may appear stable, but their stable loss values can differ. Fast convergence describes how quickly a curve approaches its settled region; final performance describes the level reached there. A model can learn quickly and finish well, learn slowly and eventually finish well, or stabilize quickly at a less favorable value.

**Question.** If a loss curve becomes flat, has the model learned as well as it can?

**Answer.** A flat curve means the current training process is producing little additional loss reduction from one epoch to the next. The height of the plateau determines how favorable that settled state is. Here, the surrogate-gradient SNN stabilizes after a larger reduction in loss, whereas the STDP-based SNN plateaus earlier at a higher loss. Both curves stabilize, but they stabilize at different levels of training error.

Accuracy gives a second view of the same learning process. Training accuracy is the fraction of training examples classified correctly. If $C$ examples are classified correctly out of $N$ training examples, then
$$
\text{training accuracy}=\frac{C}{N}
$$
Here, $C$ is the number of correct predictions and $N$ is the total number of evaluated training examples. Multiplying the result by $100\%$ expresses it as a percentage. The formula lets us compute how often the model chooses the correct class, while loss measures the objective used to guide its updates.

The accuracy trajectories show how increasing correctness accompanies decreasing loss.

![Training accuracy learning curves over 20 epochs for surrogate-gradient, converted, and STDP-based SNNs](/test-2/assets/source-visuals/2510-27379v1-page-11-graph-g1-training-accuracy-learning-curves-over-20-epochs.png)

*Training accuracy learning curves over 20 epochs for surrogate-gradient, converted, and STDP-based SNNs* *(p. 11)*

The surrogate-gradient curve rises most rapidly, matching its steep loss reduction. The converted SNN improves more gradually, consistent with its moderate loss decline. The STDP-based SNN gains accuracy more slowly and settles lower, echoing its early, higher-loss plateau. Seen together, the two graphs provide a reliability check: the method with the strongest reduction in training error also shows the strongest growth in correct classifications.

Loss and accuracy need not move in perfectly mirrored steps. Loss can improve when a model becomes more confident in correct predictions or less confident in incorrect ones, even if the number of correct classifications remains unchanged. For example, an example already assigned to the correct class may receive a stronger correct score after an update. Accuracy stays the same because the predicted class has not changed, while loss can still decrease because the output better satisfies the training objective. This is why both trajectories are useful: accuracy reports discrete correctness, and loss captures finer changes that prepare or strengthen those decisions.

**Question.** Which curve should I inspect to decide which paradigm learns fastest?

**Answer.** Inspect loss and accuracy together across the early, middle, and late epochs. A rapid loss decline indicates efficient optimization, while a rapid accuracy rise shows that the improvement is translating into more correct predictions. The surrogate-gradient SNN displays both patterns most strongly over these 20 epochs. The converted SNN progresses at a moderate rate, and the STDP-based SNN shows slower improvement with an earlier plateau.

A useful mental model is to picture each paradigm traveling downhill toward a stable region while accuracy records how many classification boundaries it crosses successfully along the way. Surrogate-gradient training descends quickly and keeps making useful progress, conversion follows a steadier middle path, and STDP settles sooner on higher ground. The complete learning curves reveal pace, smoothness, and destination together, allowing the 20-epoch results to describe how reliably each paradigm learns rather than merely where each one ends.
