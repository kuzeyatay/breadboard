## Accuracy and Energy Across Training Strategies

You already know that an ANN, a converted SNN, a surrogate-gradient SNN, and an STDP-based SNN can all perform classification while using different forms of computation and learning. Imagine choosing among these models for a device with a fixed energy budget. Classification accuracy tells you how often each model predicts the correct class, while energy consumption tells you how costly each prediction is. A useful choice has to consider both quantities together, because each model occupies a different **operating point**: a particular combination of predictive performance and energy use.

The accuracy number is the fraction of test examples classified correctly, usually expressed as a percentage. An accuracy of $98\%$, for example, means that the model correctly classifies about 98 out of every 100 test examples. The reported results come from five independent runs with different random seeds, and the accuracy is presented as a mean together with a standard deviation. The mean estimates typical performance across those runs, while the standard deviation describes how much the result changes from run to run. A model with a strong mean and a small variation provides both high performance and consistency.

Energy can be reported directly in millijoules per inference or expressed as **normalized energy consumption**. An inference is one use of the trained model to classify one input. Normalization places the energy values on a common relative scale, making proportional comparisons easier. If model A has normalized consumption $E_A$ and model B has normalized consumption $E_B$, their relative consumption can be computed as
$$
R=\frac{E_A}{E_B}
$$
Where $R$ is the energy ratio, $E_A$ is model A's normalized energy consumption, and $E_B$ is model B's normalized energy consumption. If $R=4$, model A consumes four times as much energy per inference as model B under the reported comparison. Lower normalized consumption corresponds to greater normalized energy efficiency, provided that the accuracy remains suitable for the task.

The table places the MNIST accuracy, CIFAR-10 accuracy, and normalized energy consumption beside each model or training strategy. Reading across a row reveals one complete operating point. Reading down an accuracy column compares models on the same dataset. Reading down the energy column compares their computational cost.

The ANN row establishes the high-accuracy, high-energy end of the comparison. ANN-to-SNN conversion retains competitive classification performance while moving to a much lower-energy operating point. Direct surrogate-gradient training places the SNN close to ANN accuracy-within roughly one to two percentage points in the reported results-while reducing energy further. STDP produces the lowest-energy operating point and the lowest classification accuracy among the compared approaches. Its sparse event-driven activity therefore buys a large energy reduction at the cost of additional classification errors.

MNIST and CIFAR-10 must remain separate when interpreting these rows. An MNIST accuracy and a CIFAR-10 accuracy belong to different classification problems, so comparing them directly would mix dataset difficulty with model quality. The meaningful comparison holds the dataset fixed: compare all four MNIST entries with one another, then repeat the process within the CIFAR-10 column. A model's row can therefore be understood as a **dataset-specific operating point**. Its accuracy coordinate changes with the dataset, while the accompanying energy result describes the reported computational cost of that model strategy.

Suppose two models differ by one percentage point of accuracy, while one consumes a small fraction of the other's energy. A system that places very high value on every correct classification may prefer the more accurate point. A system governed by a tight energy budget may prefer the lower-energy point. The data provide the operating points; the application supplies the priority assigned to each axis.

**Question.** Why can't the model with the highest MNIST accuracy automatically be called the best model?

**Answer.** "Best" requires a criterion. MNIST accuracy measures correctness on MNIST, while normalized energy consumption measures the cost of producing each inference. The highest-accuracy model occupies one end of the trade-off, and a lower-energy SNN may preserve most of that accuracy while consuming far less energy. CIFAR-10 also requires its own within-column comparison. A defensible choice therefore combines the relevant dataset, the required accuracy, and the acceptable energy cost.

The graph makes the energy side of these operating points concrete. The ANN consumes approximately $200\ \text{mJ}$ per inference. Conversion reduces that value to about $20\ \text{mJ}$, surrogate-gradient training reaches about $15\ \text{mJ}$, and STDP reaches about $5\ \text{mJ}$.

![Performance and energy comparison of ANN, converted SNN, direct surrogate-gradient SNN, and STDP-based SNN](/test-2/assets/source-visuals/2510-27379v1-page-7-graph-g1-performance-and-energy-comparison-of-ann-conver.png)

*Performance and energy comparison of ANN, converted SNN, direct surrogate-gradient SNN, and STDP-based SNN* *(p. 7)*

The shape of this comparison shows a steep energy reduction as computation moves from the ANN toward increasingly sparse spiking strategies. Converted SNN energy is about one tenth of ANN energy because $20/200=0.10$. Surrogate-gradient SNN energy is about $7.5\%$ of ANN energy because $15/200=0.075$. STDP energy is about $2.5\%$ of ANN energy because $5/200=0.025$. These ratios correspond to reductions of about $90\%$, $92.5\%$, and $97.5\%$, respectively.

The graph also links lower energy with fewer generated spikes: approximately 20,000 spikes for the converted SNN, 12,000 for the surrogate-gradient SNN, and 4,000 for the STDP-based SNN. Within these reported models, sparser activity accompanies lower energy consumption. The surrogate-gradient model sits in the middle: it approaches ANN accuracy while consuming less energy than the converted SNN. STDP pushes farther toward sparse activity and minimum energy, accompanied by a larger accuracy reduction.

**Question.** Does the lowest energy value make STDP the preferred strategy for both MNIST and CIFAR-10?

**Answer.** The $5\ \text{mJ}$ result makes STDP the most energy-efficient reported operating point, while its lower accuracy affects how useful that point is for a particular dataset and requirement. A choice favoring minimum energy may select it. A choice requiring accuracy close to the ANN may favor surrogate-gradient training, while conversion offers another competitive operating point. The dataset filter and the relative priorities assigned to accuracy and energy determine which point fits the task.

A reliable mental model is a map with accuracy on one axis and energy on the other. Each training strategy places a point on that map for MNIST and another for CIFAR-10. The ANN reaches the high-accuracy, high-energy region; conversion preserves competitive performance with a large energy reduction; surrogate-gradient training balances near-ANN accuracy with still lower energy; and STDP reaches the sparsest, lowest-energy region with reduced accuracy. Model selection becomes the act of choosing the dataset-specific operating point that matches the required balance between correct classifications and energy spent per inference.
