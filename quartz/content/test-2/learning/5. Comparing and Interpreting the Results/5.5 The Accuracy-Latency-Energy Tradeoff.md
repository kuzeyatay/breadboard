## The Accuracy-Latency-Energy Tradeoff

Now that event-driven sparsity has been established, the next question is how to measure whether it actually helps. A single accuracy number hides the costs that make spiking networks worthwhile, so evaluation has to weigh several quantities at once. Consider comparing two models that reach similar accuracy at very different energy and latency costs.

A single-metric comparison hides this combined behavior. Selecting solely by accuracy favors whichever model produces the greatest fraction of correct classifications, even when its decisions arrive too late for real-time control. Selecting solely by energy may favor a highly sparse model whose accuracy is insufficient for the task. Selecting solely by latency may reward fast decisions that are less reliable. Responsible selection therefore uses **multi-metric evaluation**: judging each training paradigm through the same set of measurements and under a consistent evaluation protocol.

The relevant measurements answer different practical questions. Accuracy asks, "How often is the classification correct?" Latency asks, "How long does one inference take before a decision becomes available?" Energy per inference asks, "How much energy is consumed for one decision?" Total spike count records how much event activity occurs during inference. Convergence time describes how quickly training reaches stable performance, such as how performance develops over a shared twenty-epoch training period. Looking at all five creates a performance profile rather than a single score.

Consider surrogate-gradient training. The evaluated surrogate-trained SNNs approach ANN accuracy within roughly one to two percentage points, converge rapidly by the twentieth epoch, and can reach inference latency as low as 10 milliseconds. That profile makes surrogate training attractive when a deployment needs strong accuracy and rapid decisions. A real-time controller, for example, benefits when the model can recognize an event quickly and with high reliability. The same choice still requires checking its energy and spike activity against the device's power budget. Strong accuracy and low latency establish two desirable properties; the remaining metrics determine whether the complete system can sustain them.

ANN-to-SNN conversion produces a different profile. Converted SNNs can preserve competitive classification performance, but they may require longer simulation windows and higher spike counts. A simulation window is the period during which spikes are accumulated before the network settles on an output. Extending that window gives the converted network more time to represent the continuous activations of the original ANN through spike events. The practical cost appears as later decisions and more event activity. For a task where accuracy dominates and response time remains flexible, this exchange may be acceptable. For a tightly timed edge device, the same exchange may exceed the available latency or energy budget.

STDP-based learning shifts the balance again. The evaluated STDP models converge more slowly, yet they exhibit the lowest spike counts and energy consumption, reaching energy use as low as 5 millijoules per inference in the reported comparison. Sparse spiking activity means that relatively few events travel through the network during an inference. In event-driven hardware, lower activity can support lower energy use because fewer computational events need to be processed. This profile suits ultra-low-power, unsupervised settings where conserving energy carries greater weight than reaching the strongest accuracy or fastest training behavior.

These profiles reveal an **accuracy-latency-energy tradeoff**. Improving the metric with the highest priority may involve accepting weaker values elsewhere. Longer simulation windows can support competitive accuracy in converted networks while increasing latency and spike count. Sparse activity can lower energy consumption in STDP models while accompanying slower convergence. Direct surrogate training can combine near-ANN accuracy with low latency, while its energy profile still needs to be checked against applications where every millijoule is tightly constrained.

**Question.** Why can't we combine all the metrics into one score and choose the largest value?

**Answer.** A combined score requires weights that state how valuable each metric is. Those weights come from the deployment, rather than from the measurements themselves. Giving accuracy twice the weight of energy may make sense for one safety-sensitive classifier and fail for a remote sensor expected to operate for months on a small battery. A combined score can support a decision after priorities have been chosen, but choosing the weights already involves the central design judgment: deciding which costs the application can tolerate.

A useful way to reason about such comparisons is **Pareto-style tradeoff reasoning**. Place two candidate models side by side and ask whether one is at least as good on every relevant metric and strictly better on one or more. If model A has higher accuracy, lower latency, lower energy use, fewer spikes, and faster convergence than model B, then A dominates B for those measurements. The decision is straightforward. More commonly, one model has higher accuracy while another uses less energy. Neither dominates the other because each offers a benefit the other lacks. Both remain reasonable candidates until the application supplies priorities or hard limits.

This reasoning prevents a universal ranking of training paradigms. Surrogate training, conversion, and STDP produce different combinations of benefits. Their suitability changes with the task. A real-time robotics system may impose a maximum acceptable latency and then seek the greatest accuracy among models that meet it. An always-on edge sensor may impose a strict energy ceiling and then compare the accuracy of the surviving options. An experimental workflow with limited training time may place greater emphasis on convergence by the twentieth epoch. Metric priority turns a broad comparison into an application-specific decision.

Scale can also alter the balance. As tasks grow from MNIST-scale classification toward CIFAR-10-scale classification, the reported accuracy gap can widen: conventional ANNs reach about 92% while direct SNNs reach about 85% in the cited comparison. At the same time, surrogate-trained SNNs operate at less than 10% of ANN energy costs. The accuracy difference and energy saving pull the decision in different directions. A designer must decide whether the application gains more from the additional correct classifications or from the substantial reduction in energy use.

Hardware deployment can further change the practical meaning of the metrics. On neuromorphic platforms, event-driven execution can magnify latency and energy advantages. Gesture-recognition results on DVS128, for example, place surrogate SNN inference around 5-10 milliseconds, compared with roughly 20-50 milliseconds for ANN counterparts. For real-time interaction, those ranges describe how promptly a system can react after a gesture occurs. A latency reduction becomes operationally valuable when it moves the response inside the timing budget of a robot or edge device.

**Question.** Is spike count simply another name for energy consumption?

**Answer.** Spike count measures event activity, while energy per inference measures the energy used to complete a decision. The evaluated results connect low spike counts with low-energy STDP behavior and higher spike counts with converted models requiring more activity, so spike count helps interpret why energy profiles differ. Energy remains the deployment-facing measurement because hardware execution, simulation duration, and platform behavior also shape the final cost.

The complete mental model is a set of connected constraints. Accuracy measures decision quality, latency measures decision speed, energy measures operating cost, spike count helps explain event activity, and convergence measures learning behavior. A training paradigm becomes suitable when its whole profile fits the intended system. Multi-metric evaluation exposes that profile, Pareto-style reasoning identifies candidates with meaningful strengths, and metric priority converts the application's needs into a defensible choice.
