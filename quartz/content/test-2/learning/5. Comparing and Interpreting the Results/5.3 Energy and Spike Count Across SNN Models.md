## Energy and Spike Count Across SNN Models

Earlier comparisons treated total spike count as a measure of how much event-driven activity an SNN produces during one inference. Energy estimates add a second view of the same computation: they approximate how much electrical work the model requires to process one input. Reading these measurements together reveals an **energy-per-inference operating point**-the combination of spike activity and energy consumption at which a trained model performs. This operating point helps distinguish ANN-to-SNN conversion, surrogate-gradient training, and spike-timing-dependent plasticity, because each strategy produces a different pattern of activity.

Imagine that each spike is a small message traveling between neurons. Sending and processing a message consumes energy, so a model that generates many messages generally requires more energy than one that stays quiet. **Total spike count** records the number of spikes generated throughout the network while classifying a single input. A count of 20,000 therefore means that one inference caused approximately 20,000 discrete events across the measured network.

**Estimated inference energy** expresses the corresponding computational cost in millijoules, abbreviated mJ. One millijoule is one-thousandth of a joule. The estimate combines the activity generated by the model with the cost of carrying out that activity on the assumed computing system. Spike count strongly influences this estimate, while the operations attached to each event, memory movement, simulation duration, and system overhead can also contribute. Two models with different spike counts should therefore be compared using both columns rather than treating spike count as a complete energy measurement.

The energy values descend sharply as the comparison moves from the conventional ANN CNN to the three SNN strategies. The ANN CNN sits near 200 mJ per inference. Its computation uses continuous-valued operations, so it provides an energy reference even though a spike-count comparison does not describe its activity in the same way as it describes an SNN. Each SNN occupies a substantially lower-energy region, showing the computational advantage associated with event-driven processing in this comparison.

Among the SNNs, the converted model occupies the highest-activity operating point. **ANN-to-SNN conversion** begins with a trained ANN and translates its continuous activations into spike-based activity. The converted SNN uses about 20 mJ per inference and generates roughly 20,000 spikes. Its relatively dense activity follows from representing activation strength through spike rates: a stronger activation is expressed through more spikes over a simulation window. Reproducing the ANN's activation values can consequently require repeated events and a longer period of spike accumulation.

A concrete interpretation helps connect the two measurements. Suppose one converted neuron must represent a strong activation. During the simulation window, it may fire repeatedly so that its firing rate approximates the original ANN value. When many neurons behave this way, their events accumulate into a large total spike count. The converted model still consumes far less energy than the ANN CNN reference, yet it uses the most spikes and energy among the three reported SNN strategies.

The surrogate-gradient SNN moves to a lower-activity point: approximately 12,000 spikes and 15 mJ per inference. Surrogate-gradient training adjusts the spiking network directly while providing a usable gradient during learning. Because training shapes the network's spike patterns for the task, the model can reach its decision with fewer events than the converted SNN. The reduction from about 20,000 to 12,000 spikes is approximately 8,000 spikes per inference, or 40% of the converted model's count. Energy falls at the same time from about 20 mJ to 15 mJ, a reduction of 5 mJ per inference.

These paired reductions make the surrogate-gradient result an efficiency compromise. Its operating point combines moderate spike activity with moderate energy consumption, while the wider reported evaluation places its accuracy close to the ANN and its latency around 10 milliseconds. The energy graph alone cannot select a universally preferred model, because model choice depends on how energy, accuracy, latency, and training behavior are weighted. Within the energy-and-spike comparison, however, surrogate training clearly improves on the converted SNN along both measured axes.

The lowest operating point belongs to the SNN trained with **spike-timing-dependent plasticity**, usually shortened to **STDP**. STDP changes connections using the relative timing of spikes: the order and temporal proximity of activity provide the learning signal. The reported STDP-based model generates roughly 4,000 spikes per inference and reaches an estimated energy value as low as 5 mJ per inference. That value describes the reported operating point; the energy of another STDP implementation can vary with its network, activity, hardware assumptions, and measurement procedure.

Reading across each row preserves the connection between training strategy and operating point. The converted SNN pairs about 20 mJ with about 20,000 spikes; the surrogate-gradient SNN pairs about 15 mJ with about 12,000 spikes; and the STDP-based SNN pairs about 5 mJ with about 4,000 spikes. From the converted model to the STDP model, spike activity falls by about 16,000 events, an 80% reduction. Estimated energy falls by about 15 mJ, a 75% reduction. Relative to the roughly 200 mJ ANN reference, the 5 mJ STDP result represents about a 97.5% reduction in estimated inference energy.

The similar downward movement of spike count and energy shows that sparse event activity is a major contributor to the reported efficiency. Their relationship is not perfectly proportional: the surrogate model uses about three times as many spikes as the STDP model, while its estimated energy is also about three times as large, but the converted model uses five times as many spikes as STDP and consumes about four times the energy. Computational costs therefore depend on the complete operating process, including how spikes are handled and how long the network runs.

**Question.** Does the STDP model's smaller spike count mean that it produces the best predictions?

**Answer.** Spike count measures event activity, while accuracy measures how often predictions are correct. The reported STDP model has the sparsest activity and lowest estimated energy, together with slightly reduced accuracy and slower convergence. The surrogate-gradient model uses more spikes and energy while providing a stronger balance when competitive accuracy is also required. A low spike count establishes an efficiency result rather than an accuracy ranking.

**Question.** Why does the converted SNN still save substantial energy when it generates about 20,000 spikes?

**Answer.** Its spikes are discrete events, allowing computation to occur through event-driven operations rather than the ANN CNN's continuous-valued processing. The converted model therefore reaches about 20 mJ per inference compared with roughly 200 mJ for the ANN reference. Its rate-coded representation generates more activity than the directly trained SNNs, which explains why surrogate-gradient and STDP models reach still lower operating points.

The three strategies can now be pictured as positions on a map whose horizontal and vertical directions represent activity and energy. Conversion remains closest to dense ANN-like activation, giving roughly 20,000 spikes at 20 mJ. Surrogate-gradient training shapes spike patterns more directly, moving toward roughly 12,000 spikes at 15 mJ. STDP uses timing-based local adaptation and reaches the sparsest reported point, roughly 4,000 spikes at as little as 5 mJ. Following both coordinates preserves the full mental model: spike count describes how much event traffic the network creates, energy per inference describes the estimated cost of completing the computation, and their pairing reveals the practical efficiency trade-off created by each training strategy.
