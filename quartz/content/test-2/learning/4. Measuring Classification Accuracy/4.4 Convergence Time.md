## Convergence Time

Once classification accuracy is measured after each training epoch, those measurements can answer a practical question: how long does training take to reach a chosen level of performance? Imagine recording accuracy at the end of every epoch. Early measurements may be low because the network has had few opportunities to adjust. Later measurements may rise as training continues. A long sequence of accuracy values is useful, but comparing training runs becomes easier when that sequence is reduced to one clearly defined milestone: the earliest epoch whose measured accuracy reaches a selected target.

An **epoch** is one complete pass through the training data. Epochs give training a discrete clock: epoch 1, epoch 2, epoch 3, and so on. At a chosen evaluation point for each epoch, the trained network is used to make classifications, and its **measured accuracy** is recorded. Accuracy is the fraction of evaluated examples classified correctly, often expressed as a percentage. An accuracy of $0.84$, for example, means that 84% of the evaluated examples received the correct predicted class.

To turn these measurements into a convergence criterion, begin by selecting a **target accuracy**. The target is the accuracy level that a run must reach to count as having achieved the desired performance. Suppose the target is 90%. Each epoch can then be checked with a simple question: has the measured accuracy reached at least 90%? Epochs below 90% do not qualify. The earliest epoch at or above 90% becomes the convergence time.

This rule can be written compactly as
$$
T_{\mathrm{conv}}=\min \left\{e \mid A(e)\geq A_{\mathrm{target}}\right\}
$$
Here, $T_{\mathrm{conv}}$ is the **convergence time**, measured in epochs. The symbol $e$ represents a training epoch. The function $A(e)$ is the measured classification accuracy at epoch $e$. The quantity $A_{\mathrm{target}}$ is the selected target accuracy. The vertical bar means "such that," and $\min$ chooses the smallest qualifying epoch number. The formula therefore computes the earliest epoch whose measured accuracy is equal to or greater than the target.

The formula grows directly from the measurement process. Training produces an ordered list of epochs,
$$
1,2,3,\ldots
$$
And evaluation produces a corresponding ordered list of accuracies,
$$
A(1),A(2),A(3),\ldots
$$
The target-accuracy criterion filters the epochs by keeping those for which $A(e)\geq A_{\mathrm{target}}$. Taking the minimum of the remaining epoch numbers identifies when the target was reached for the earliest time. Convergence time is therefore an "earliest qualifying epoch" measurement.

Consider a run with a target accuracy of 90%. Suppose its measured accuracies are 72% at epoch 1, 81% at epoch 2, 88% at epoch 3, 91% at epoch 4, and 93% at epoch 5. Epochs 1 through 3 fall below the target. Epochs 4 and 5 satisfy the criterion because their accuracies are at least 90%. Among those qualifying epochs, 4 is the smallest epoch number, so
$$
T_{\mathrm{conv}}=4
$$
The 93% measurement at epoch 5 may describe stronger later performance, while convergence time answers a narrower question: when did the run first reach the selected target? That focus makes the metric useful for comparing how quickly different runs attain the same required accuracy level.

The phrase **minimum qualifying epoch** captures the procedure precisely. "Qualifying" means satisfying $A(e)\geq A_{\mathrm{target}}$, while "minimum" means choosing the earliest such epoch. Equality counts. If the target is 90% and an epoch records exactly 90%, that epoch qualifies. If epoch 6 is the earliest qualifying epoch, then the convergence time is 6 epochs even when later epochs also exceed the target.

A run may also finish without ever reaching the selected target. In that case, the qualifying set
$$
\left\{e \mid A(e)\geq A_{\mathrm{target}}\right\}
$$
Contains no epochs, so the minimum qualifying epoch is unavailable within the observed training period. The result should be reported as the target remaining unreached over the measured epochs rather than assigning the final epoch as the convergence time. For example, if training ends at epoch 20 and the highest measured accuracy is 88% for a 90% target, the run has no convergence time under that target-accuracy criterion during the 20 observed epochs.

**Question.** Does reaching a higher accuracy later change the convergence time?

**Answer.** No. Once the earliest qualifying epoch has been identified, later measurements do not move that milestone. If a run first reaches a 90% target at epoch 7 and later reaches 95% at epoch 15, its convergence time for the 90% target remains 7 epochs. The later value describes subsequent accuracy, while the convergence metric records the first target crossing.

**Question.** Can two runs have different convergence times even if they eventually reach the same accuracy?

**Answer.** Yes. Suppose both runs eventually reach 92% accuracy with a 90% target. If one first reaches 90% at epoch 5 and the other first reaches it at epoch 12, their convergence times are 5 and 12 epochs. The shared target keeps the comparison consistent, and the smaller convergence time identifies the run that reached the required accuracy earlier.

The complete mental model is a race toward a fixed accuracy threshold. Each epoch advances the training clock, each evaluation supplies the current accuracy, and the target defines the finish line. Scanning the measurements from the earliest epoch forward, the first value that reaches or exceeds that line determines convergence time.
