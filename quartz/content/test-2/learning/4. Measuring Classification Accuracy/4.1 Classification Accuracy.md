## Classification Accuracy

A spiking neural network has already produced a prediction for each input: one gesture, sound, or other event is assigned to one class. The immediate evaluation question is simple: how often did the predicted class match the correct class? Classification accuracy turns those individual correct and incorrect outcomes into a single ratio that describes the network's overall success across a set of evaluated inputs.

Imagine evaluating a network on ten inputs. For each input, compare the predicted class with the known correct class. Record a correct outcome when the two labels match and an incorrect outcome when they differ. Suppose eight predictions match their correct labels while two predictions differ. Looking only at the count of eight correct predictions gives an incomplete picture because the meaning of that count depends on how many predictions were evaluated. Eight correct predictions out of ten indicate strong performance, while eight correct predictions out of one hundred indicate weak performance. A ratio places the count in context.

Let $C$ represent the number of correct predictions and let $N$ represent the total number of predictions evaluated. Dividing the correct-prediction count by the total-prediction count gives the fraction of predictions that were correct:
$$
\text{Classification accuracy}=\frac{C}{N}
$$
Here, $C$ is the **correct-prediction count**, meaning the number of evaluated inputs whose predicted class matches the correct class. $N$ is the **total-prediction count**, meaning every prediction included in the evaluation, whether correct or incorrect. Classification accuracy is the resulting fraction $C/N$. It lets you compute the share of evaluated predictions that the network classified correctly.

For the ten-input example, $C=8$ and $N=10$, so
$$
\text{Classification accuracy}=\frac{8}{10}=0.8
$$
The decimal $0.8$ can be expressed as a percentage by multiplying by $100\%$:
$$
0.8\times 100\%=80\%
$$
An accuracy of $80\%$ means that 80 out of every 100 evaluated predictions would be correct if the observed ratio continued at the same rate. For the actual ten-input evaluation, the precise interpretation remains eight correct predictions out of ten. The percentage provides a convenient scale for comparing evaluations with different total counts.

The denominator deserves careful attention. Every evaluated prediction contributes once to $N$. If a network processes 500 inputs, then $N=500$, regardless of how many outcomes are correct. If 465 predictions match their correct labels, then $C=465$, giving
$$
\text{Classification accuracy}=\frac{465}{500}=0.93=93\%
$$
The calculation combines all 500 outcomes into one number. Each correct outcome contributes one to the numerator, while each incorrect outcome contributes zero. Another way to see the same calculation is to assign a score $x_i$ to prediction $i$, where $x_i=1$ for a correct prediction and $x_i=0$ for an incorrect prediction. For $N$ predictions, the correct-prediction count is
$$
C=x_1+x_2+\cdots+x_N
$$
And the accuracy is the average of these zero-or-one scores:
$$
\text{Classification accuracy}
=\frac{x_1+x_2+\cdots+x_N}{N}
$$
This interpretation explains why accuracy lies between $0$ and $1$, or between $0\%$ and $100\%$. A value of $0\%$ means every evaluated prediction was incorrect. A value of $100\%$ means every evaluated prediction was correct. Values between those endpoints describe the fraction of successful classifications.

**Question.** If two networks each make 90 correct predictions, do they have the same classification accuracy?

**Answer.** Their accuracies are equal only when they were evaluated on the same total number of predictions. A network with 90 correct predictions out of 100 has $90/100=90\%$ accuracy. A network with 90 correct predictions out of 120 has $90/120=75\%$ accuracy. The correct-prediction count gains meaning through the total-prediction count.

Accuracy answers a focused question about predictive outcomes. It leaves the network's computational behavior to separate measurements. Two spiking networks can achieve the same accuracy while requiring different inference times, consuming different amounts of energy, or generating different numbers of spikes. For example, suppose two networks each classify 95 of 100 inputs correctly. Both have $95\%$ accuracy. One may reach each prediction quickly with sparse spiking activity, while the other may take longer and generate many more spikes. Their classification success is equal under the accuracy calculation, while their latency, energy consumption, and spike activity differ.

The reverse pattern can also occur. A network may use very little energy or produce relatively few spikes while making fewer correct predictions. Accuracy captures that loss in classification success through a smaller numerator. Energy and spike count capture other properties using their own units: energy can be measured per inference, latency can be measured as elapsed inference time, and spike count records the number of generated events. Combining these measurements mentally would blur distinct questions. Accuracy asks, "How often was the predicted class correct?" Latency asks, "How long did prediction take?" Energy asks, "How much energy did prediction consume?" Spike count asks, "How much spiking activity occurred?"

**Question.** Does an accuracy of $93\%$ mean that every class is predicted correctly $93\%$ of the time?

**Answer.** The overall ratio alone gives the fraction of all evaluated predictions that were correct. It leaves the distribution of errors across classes unanswered. The correct predictions could be spread evenly across classes, or the network could perform better on some classes than on others. Additional class-level counts would be needed to distinguish those possibilities.

Classification accuracy therefore works like a success rate for a completed set of predictions. Begin with one outcome per input, mark whether each predicted class matches the correct class, count the matches, and divide by the total number evaluated. The resulting ratio compresses many outcomes into a clear measure of classification success, while latency, energy, spike activity, and the pattern of errors remain separate dimensions of the network's behavior.
