## Decision Latency

Once a classifier can produce a class decision, its performance can be examined along another dimension: how long the decision takes. Imagine that a gesture begins at a known instant and an SNN processes the incoming events until it commits to a label. A correct label tells us whether the classification succeeded, while the interval between the gesture's beginning and the commitment tells us how quickly the result became available. That interval is called **decision latency**.

Measuring this interval requires two observable moments. The **stimulus onset time** is the time at which the input stimulus begins. The **decision time** is the time at which the SNN produces the response counted as its classification decision. Both times must be recorded against the same clock. Once those two timestamps are available, the elapsed time between them follows directly.

Suppose a laboratory clock reads 10.000 seconds when a stimulus begins and 10.012 seconds when the network produces its decision. The clock reading 10.012 seconds is the decision time, but it does not by itself reveal how long the decision took. The clock may have been running long before the stimulus appeared. The useful measurement comes from removing the time that had already passed before the stimulus began.

Start with the timeline from the clock's origin to the decision event. Its total length is the decision time. Part of that timeline runs from the clock's origin to stimulus onset, so subtracting that earlier portion leaves exactly the interval from stimulus onset to the decision:
$$
L = t_{\mathrm{decision}} - t_{\mathrm{onset}}
$$
Here, $L$ is the **decision latency**, meaning the elapsed time required to produce the decision after the stimulus begins. The symbol $t_{\mathrm{decision}}$ is the **decision time**, the timestamp at which the classification response occurs. The symbol $t_{\mathrm{onset}}$ is the **stimulus onset time**, the timestamp at which the input begins. This formula lets us compute the response interval from two timestamps.

All three quantities use units of time. If both timestamps are recorded in milliseconds, the latency is measured in milliseconds. If they are recorded in seconds, the subtraction initially gives a result in seconds. Unit consistency keeps the interpretation reliable: subtracting 10 milliseconds from 25 milliseconds gives 15 milliseconds, whereas timestamps expressed in different units must first be converted to a common unit.

Consider a stimulus that begins at $t_{\mathrm{onset}}=2{,}500\ \text{ms}$. The SNN produces its classification decision at $t_{\mathrm{decision}}=2{,}514\ \text{ms}$. Substituting these values gives
$$
L = 2{,}514\ \text{ms} - 2{,}500\ \text{ms}
  = 14\ \text{ms}
$$
The network therefore requires 14 milliseconds from the beginning of the stimulus to the decision event. The value 2,514 milliseconds locates the decision on the experiment's clock; the value 14 milliseconds describes the duration of the classification response. Keeping these meanings separate prevents a common beginner confusion: **decision time** is an absolute timestamp within the measurement timeline, while **decision latency** is the elapsed interval calculated from two timestamps.

A simple analogy is a runner using a clock mounted at a track. The clock reads 3:00 when the runner starts and 3:12 when the runner finishes. The finish timestamp is 3:12, while the run lasts 12 seconds. In the same way, an SNN's decision event has a position on the clock and a duration measured from stimulus onset. Latency captures the duration.

The definition of the decision event must remain consistent across measurements. A response counted at one stage in one trial and at a different stage in another would make the resulting latencies describe different intervals. A clear measurement therefore identifies the input event used as stimulus onset and the response event used as the decision, records both timestamps, and subtracts them in the same order for every example. With this setup, a smaller positive value of $L$ means that less time passed between input onset and response.

**Question.** Why can the decision timestamp alone not serve as the latency?

**Answer.** A timestamp gives the decision's location on a clock whose origin may be unrelated to the stimulus. For example, a decision at 8,020 milliseconds could have followed a stimulus beginning at 8,000 milliseconds or one beginning at 7,900 milliseconds. Those cases have latencies of 20 milliseconds and 120 milliseconds, respectively. Subtracting the corresponding stimulus onset time reveals the actual response interval.

Latency is especially useful when an SNN handles inputs whose timing carries meaningful information, such as changing visual events or time-dependent auditory signals. The measurement begins when the stimulus begins, continues while the system processes the incoming events, and ends when the chosen decision event occurs. It therefore translates a sequence of timed activity into one interpretable duration.

**Question.** What happens if the stimulus onset is defined too early?

**Answer.** The measured interval becomes longer because the calculation includes time before the intended input actually begins. If the true onset is 500 milliseconds and the recorded onset is 490 milliseconds, a decision at 520 milliseconds produces a reported latency of $520-490=30$ milliseconds instead of the intended $520-500=20$ milliseconds. Accurate onset marking is therefore part of accurate latency measurement.

A useful mental model is a marked segment on a timeline. One endpoint is the arrival of the stimulus, and the other is the SNN's classification decision. The clock readings identify those endpoints; subtraction measures the segment between them. Decision latency is that segment's length, expressed in a unit of time, and it answers the practical question: after the input begins, how much time passes before the SNN produces its decision?
