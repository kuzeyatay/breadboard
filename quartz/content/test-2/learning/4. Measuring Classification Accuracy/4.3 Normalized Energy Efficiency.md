## Normalized Energy Efficiency

Once classification accuracy and estimated inference energy have been measured separately, a practical comparison becomes possible. Imagine two classifiers processing the same stream of inputs. One predicts slightly more labels correctly, while the other uses much less energy for every prediction. Accuracy alone favors the first classifier, and energy alone favors the second. A combined measure helps answer a more focused question: how much correct classification performance is obtained from each joule of energy?

Begin with classification accuracy. If a model classifies $N_{\text{correct}}$ examples correctly out of $N_{\text{total}}$ examples, its accuracy as a fraction is
$$
A=\frac{N_{\text{correct}}}{N_{\text{total}}}
$$
Here, $A$ is the classification accuracy, $N_{\text{correct}}$ is the number of correct predictions, and $N_{\text{total}}$ is the total number of predictions evaluated. A model that correctly classifies 978 out of 1,000 examples has
$$
A=\frac{978}{1000}=0.978
$$
Which is usually reported as $97.8\%$.

Now include the energy required to produce one prediction. Let $E$ be the estimated inference energy measured in joules per inference. An inference is one pass through the model to produce a prediction. If a model consumes $15$ millijoules per inference, the energy must first be expressed in joules. Because one millijoule equals one-thousandth of a joule,
$$
15\ \text{mJ}=15\times 10^{-3}\ \text{J}=0.015\ \text{J}
$$
The desired measure should increase when accuracy increases and decrease when energy consumption increases. Division creates exactly that relationship. Dividing accuracy by inference energy gives the **normalized energy efficiency**:
$$
\eta_{\text{NEE}}=\frac{A}{E}
$$
In this formula, $\eta_{\text{NEE}}$ is the normalized energy efficiency, $A$ is classification accuracy written as a fraction between $0$ and $1$, and $E$ is the estimated energy consumed per inference in joules. The ratio computes the classification accuracy obtained per joule. Its unit can be written as $\text{J}^{-1}$, because accuracy is a dimensionless fraction.

Consider a surrogate-gradient spiking neural network with an accuracy of approximately $97.8\%$ and an estimated inference energy of $15$ mJ. Convert both quantities into the forms required by the formula:
$$
A=0.978,\qquad E=15\ \text{mJ}=0.015\ \text{J}
$$
The normalized energy efficiency is therefore
$$
\eta_{\text{NEE}}
=\frac{0.978}{0.015}
=65.2\ \text{J}^{-1}
$$
This value means that the model delivers $65.2$ units of fractional classification accuracy per joule under the measurement assumptions used to estimate its inference energy. The number becomes especially useful when every model is evaluated with the same accuracy convention, energy-estimation method, and inference workload.

A second model reveals how the ratio captures a tradeoff. An STDP-based spiking neural network has an accuracy of approximately $95.5\%$ and consumes about $5$ mJ per inference. Its accuracy fraction is $0.955$, and its energy in joules is
$$
5\ \text{mJ}=0.005\ \text{J}
$$
Its normalized energy efficiency is
$$
\eta_{\text{NEE}}
=\frac{0.955}{0.005}
=191\ \text{J}^{-1}
$$
The surrogate-gradient model has the higher classification accuracy: $97.8\%$ compared with $95.5\%$. The STDP-based model has the higher normalized energy efficiency because it reaches its accuracy using one-third as much estimated energy per inference. Its ratio is approximately $191/65.2\approx 2.93$ times as large. In this comparison, a small reduction in accuracy accompanies a large reduction in energy, so the quotient rises sharply.

The scale used for accuracy must remain consistent. If accuracy is entered as $97.8$ rather than $0.978$, the result becomes $6{,}520$ percentage points per joule instead of $65.2\ \text{J}^{-1}$. Both conventions preserve the same ordering because one is exactly one hundred times the other. Writing accuracy as a fraction keeps the calculation compact and makes comparisons easier to reproduce.

**Question.** Does a higher normalized energy efficiency always mean that a model has higher classification accuracy?

**Answer.** A higher ratio means the model obtains more accuracy per joule. The ratio can rise through higher accuracy, lower energy consumption, or both. In the worked comparison, the STDP-based model reaches $191\ \text{J}^{-1}$ despite having lower raw accuracy than the surrogate-gradient model, because its estimated energy falls from $15$ mJ to $5$ mJ per inference. The ratio describes how the two quantities interact.

Energy units also require careful attention. A value expressed in millijoules is numerically one thousand times larger than the same energy expressed in joules. Dividing by $15$ while labeling the result "per joule" would produce a unit-conversion error. The denominator must be converted before calculating the ratio:
$$
E_{\text{J}}=\frac{E_{\text{mJ}}}{1000}
$$
Here, $E_{\text{mJ}}$ is energy measured in millijoules and $E_{\text{J}}$ is the same energy measured in joules. Substituting this conversion into the efficiency formula gives
$$
\eta_{\text{NEE}}
=\frac{A}{E_{\text{mJ}}/1000}
=\frac{1000A}{E_{\text{mJ}}}
$$
This equivalent form lets you compute accuracy per joule directly from a millijoule measurement while keeping the conversion visible.

**Question.** What does it mean when normalized energy efficiency increases?

**Answer.** An increase means that each joule supports a larger amount of classification accuracy under the same evaluation conditions. For a fixed energy value, improved accuracy raises the ratio. For a fixed accuracy value, reduced inference energy also raises it. When both quantities change, the ratio shows their combined effect.

The complete mental model is a simple exchange: classification accuracy describes the predictive result, estimated inference energy describes the energetic cost of producing that result, and normalized energy efficiency divides the result by the cost. Reading the ratio as "accuracy obtained per joule" keeps its meaning concrete and makes the direction intuitive: larger values represent more classification performance from each unit of energy.
