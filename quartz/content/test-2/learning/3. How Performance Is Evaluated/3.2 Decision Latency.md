Once an SNN produces an inference decision, its accuracy tells us whether the decision is correct, while **decision latency** tells us how long the decision took. Imagine a gesture-recognition sensor receiving the beginning of a hand movement. The network processes incoming spikes over successive time steps and eventually selects a gesture class. A system intended to react while the gesture is happening needs to reach that decision promptly, so evaluation must measure the elapsed time from the arrival of the gesture to the network's response.

A latency measurement begins by identifying two moments on the same timeline. The **stimulus onset time** is the moment when the input stimulus begins. We write this time as $t_{\text{onset}}$. For a visual task, it could mark the beginning of an observed gesture. For a time-dependent auditory task, it could mark the beginning of a sound input. Establishing this starting point gives the measurement a consistent reference: the clock begins when the information to be processed starts arriving.

The second moment is the **decision time**, written as $t_{\text{decision}}$. This is the time at which the network produces its inference decision. Between stimulus onset and decision time, spikes travel through the network and contribute to its output. When the network reaches the point at which it produces the decision being evaluated, the corresponding time is recorded as $t_{\text{decision}}$.

The elapsed time between these two moments is the decision latency. Starting at $t_{\text{onset}}$, the timeline advances until $t_{\text{decision}}$. Subtracting the starting time from the ending time leaves exactly the duration spent reaching the response:
$$
L_{\text{decision}} = t_{\text{decision}} - t_{\text{onset}}
$$
Here, $L_{\text{decision}}$ is the **decision latency**, $t_{\text{decision}}$ is the time when the network produces its decision, and $t_{\text{onset}}$ is the time when the stimulus begins. The formula computes the network's response interval for a particular inference. A smaller value means that less time passes between receiving the stimulus and producing the decision, which makes the network more suitable for situations requiring rapid responses.

Consider a simple timeline measured in milliseconds. Suppose a stimulus begins at $12\ \text{ms}$, and the network produces its decision at $22\ \text{ms}$. The latency is
$$
L_{\text{decision}} = 22\ \text{ms} - 12\ \text{ms} = 10\ \text{ms}
$$
The network therefore requires $10\ \text{ms}$ from stimulus onset to decision. The value $22\ \text{ms}$ alone would describe the decision's position on the overall clock. Subtracting the onset time isolates the portion of that clock associated with responding to the stimulus.

Latency can also be expressed in **time steps**. A time step is one discrete update in the SNN's temporal processing sequence. Imagine that the stimulus begins at time step 20 and the decision appears at time step 35. Applying the same definition gives
$$
L_{\text{decision}} = 35 - 20 = 15\text{ time steps}
$$
This result says that the network advances through 15 updates between the beginning of the stimulus and its decision. Reporting latency in time steps is useful when evaluation follows the network's discrete computational timeline. Reporting it in milliseconds describes the elapsed physical time. Both units answer the same question, provided that the onset and decision are measured using the same unit.

If each time step represents a known duration, a timestep latency can be converted into physical time. Let $\Delta t$ be the duration of one time step and let $N_{\text{steps}}$ be the measured latency in time steps. Then the physical latency is
$$
L_{\text{time}} = N_{\text{steps}}\Delta t
$$
Where $L_{\text{time}}$ is the elapsed latency in a physical time unit such as milliseconds. For example, a latency of 15 time steps with a duration of $1\ \text{ms}$ per step corresponds to $15\ \text{ms}$. This conversion allows results recorded on a discrete timeline to be interpreted as real-time response durations.

**Question.** Why do we subtract the stimulus onset time instead of reporting the decision time directly?

**Answer.** Decision time identifies where the response occurs on the measurement clock, while latency measures how long the response takes after the stimulus begins. If one trial starts at $5\ \text{ms}$ and reaches a decision at $15\ \text{ms}$, its latency is $10\ \text{ms}$. Another trial could start at $40\ \text{ms}$ and reach a decision at $50\ \text{ms}$; it also has a latency of $10\ \text{ms}$. Subtraction removes the arbitrary clock position and preserves the response duration.

**Question.** Can two reported latency values be compared when one uses milliseconds and the other uses time steps?

**Answer.** They can be compared after the duration represented by each time step is known. A result of 10 time steps describes ten network updates, but its physical duration depends on $\Delta t$. Multiplying the number of steps by the duration per step converts it to milliseconds or another shared unit.

Decision latency can therefore be pictured as a marked interval on the SNN's processing timeline. The left boundary is stimulus onset, the right boundary is the inference decision, and the distance between them is the latency. Measuring that distance in milliseconds or time steps turns the general idea of a "fast response" into a precise performance metric that can be recorded and evaluated.
