## Classification Accuracy

Once a spiking neural network has produced a predicted class label, evaluation begins by comparing that prediction with the target class label-the correct answer attached to the input. Imagine an SNN receiving 100 recordings of hand gestures. For each recording, the network selects a label such as "wave," "clap," or "point." An evaluator then checks each selection against the known gesture. Every match contributes one correct prediction; every mismatch contributes zero. Classification accuracy turns those individual checks into a single fraction that describes how often the network chose the correct class.

The counting process can be built from one example at a time. Suppose the network predicts "wave" for an input whose target label is "wave." The prediction is correct, so the correct-prediction count increases by one. Suppose the next input is a "clap," but the network predicts "point." The total-prediction count still increases because the network completed another inference, while the correct-prediction count stays unchanged. After all examples have been evaluated, the two counts provide everything needed to calculate accuracy.

The relationship is
$$
\text{Classification accuracy}
=
\frac{\text{number of correct predictions}}
{\text{total number of predictions}}
$$
Here, **classification accuracy** is the fraction of evaluated inputs assigned the correct class. The **number of correct predictions** counts how many predicted labels match their target labels. The **total number of predictions** counts every evaluated input, including both correct and incorrect results. The formula computes the share of predictions that were correct.

Consider an SNN evaluated on 200 inputs. If its predicted label matches the target label for 186 of them, then
$$
\text{Classification accuracy}
=
\frac{186}{200}
=
0.93
$$
The decimal $0.93$ means that 93 out of every 100 evaluated predictions were correct on average. Accuracy is therefore often expressed as a percentage:
$$
0.93 \times 100\% = 93\%
$$
The percentage form and the fractional form describe the same result. A reported classification accuracy of $93\%$ means that the model correctly classified $93\%$ of the examples included in that evaluation. Results above $93\%$, such as those reported for a spiking convolutional network on dynamic gesture recognition, indicate that more than 93 of every 100 evaluated gestures received the correct label.

Accuracy depends on the collection of inputs used for evaluation. If a model is tested on $N$ examples, each correct prediction contributes $1/N$ to the final fraction. With 100 examples, one correct prediction contributes one percentage point. With 1,000 examples, one correct prediction contributes one tenth of a percentage point. The size of the evaluation set therefore determines how much influence each individual prediction has on the reported value.

Accuracy also records outcomes rather than the process that produced them. Two SNNs can reach the same accuracy while behaving very differently during inference. One may require many spikes and a longer inference time before selecting a label. Another may reach its decision with sparse spiking activity and lower latency. Their correct-prediction counts can match even though their computational behavior differs substantially. Accuracy leaves energy consumption, spike count, and latency unanswered because none of those quantities appears in its formula.

Training behavior creates another dimension that accuracy leaves open. A directly trained SNN may eventually reach a strong classification result while requiring more training epochs or sensitive hyperparameter adjustment to converge. Another model may converge differently while producing a similar final fraction of correct predictions. The final accuracy value records the endpoint on the evaluated examples; convergence measurements describe how the model approached that endpoint.

**Question.** If two SNNs both achieve $95\%$ accuracy, are their performance characteristics equivalent?

**Answer.** They correctly classify the same fraction of their respective evaluation examples, provided the results were calculated under comparable conditions. Their latency, energy use, spike count, and convergence behavior can still differ. One model might deliver decisions in less time, while another might consume less energy by producing fewer spikes. Accuracy supports comparison of correct classification rates, while the other metrics reveal the computational tradeoffs behind those rates.

Variation across repeated training runs also affects how an accuracy result should be interpreted. Training can begin from different random conditions, so separate runs may finish with slightly different prediction counts. Running an experiment several times with different random seeds produces a collection of accuracy values. Reporting the mean gives the average accuracy across those runs, while the standard deviation describes how much the results vary around that average. A result written as "mean ± standard deviation" therefore communicates both typical correctness and run-to-run stability.

**Question.** Why report several runs when the accuracy formula already gives an exact answer?

**Answer.** The formula gives an exact fraction for one set of predictions from one trained model. Repeating the experiment asks whether a similar result appears when training begins under different random conditions. If five runs produce closely grouped accuracy values, the model's result is relatively stable. If the values spread widely, a single run may give an unreliable impression of typical performance. The average and standard deviation preserve information that one accuracy percentage would leave hidden.

A useful mental model is to picture accuracy as a scorekeeper standing at the end of inference. The scorekeeper observes each predicted label, compares it with the target, and records a match or mismatch. Dividing the matches by all evaluated predictions produces a clear measure of classification success. The scorekeeper does not observe how many spikes were emitted, how long each decision took, how much energy was consumed, or how training converged. Those questions require their own measurements. Classification accuracy supplies the correctness coordinate within a broader performance picture, allowing an SNN's successful predictions to be counted precisely while additional metrics describe the cost and behavior behind them.
