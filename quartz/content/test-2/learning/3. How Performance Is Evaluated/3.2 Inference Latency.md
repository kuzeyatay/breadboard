## Inference Latency

Once a model can turn an input stimulus into a decision, evaluation gains a new question: how long does that transformation take? Imagine a gesture-recognition system receiving a hand movement. The system eventually identifies the gesture, but a useful evaluation must distinguish a response produced almost immediately from one produced after a noticeable delay. Both decisions may be correct, yet they behave differently when an application must react while the event is still unfolding.

Measuring that delay requires two clearly defined moments. The **stimulus time** is the timestamp at which the input becomes available to the model. Depending on the evaluation setup, the stimulus might be an event stream, a sound sample, or another input presented for inference. We can denote this timestamp by $t_{\text{stimulus}}$. It establishes the starting point of the measurement.

The **decision time** is the timestamp at which the model produces the response being evaluated. We denote it by $t_{\text{decision}}$. A decision must be defined consistently before timing begins. For example, an evaluator might treat the first emitted class prediction as the decision, or require the model's output to satisfy a fixed decision rule. Using the same rule for every measured sample ensures that the ending timestamp has the same meaning throughout the evaluation.

With a start and an end, the required response time is their difference:
$$
L = t_{\text{decision}} - t_{\text{stimulus}}
$$
Here, $L$ is the **inference latency**, $t_{\text{decision}}$ is the time at which the model produces its decision, and $t_{\text{stimulus}}$ is the time at which the input stimulus is presented. The subtraction computes elapsed time: the duration spent moving from the arrival of the input to the production of the decision. When both timestamps are recorded in milliseconds, the resulting latency is also measured in milliseconds.

Suppose an input stimulus arrives at $125\text{ ms}$, and the model produces its decision at $134\text{ ms}$. The inference latency is
$$
L = 134\text{ ms} - 125\text{ ms} = 9\text{ ms}
$$
The model therefore required $9\text{ ms}$ to respond. The value $134\text{ ms}$ alone cannot reveal the response duration because it is an absolute timestamp tied to the evaluator's clock. Starting the same inference at $500\text{ ms}$ and finishing at $509\text{ ms}$ gives the same $9\text{ ms}$ latency. Subtracting the stimulus time removes the arbitrary clock position and preserves the interval that describes the model's behavior.

This is why stimulus time makes the behavior measurable. Without a recorded starting moment, the decision timestamp has no reference point. It resembles saying that a runner crossed the finish line at 10:15 without saying when the race began. Recording both moments turns two clock readings into a duration that can be interpreted and compared.

**Question.** If a model makes a decision at $40\text{ ms}$, is its inference latency $40\text{ ms}$?

**Answer.** The decision timestamp by itself is insufficient. If the stimulus arrived at $32\text{ ms}$, the latency is $40\text{ ms}-32\text{ ms}=8\text{ ms}$. If the stimulus arrived at $5\text{ ms}$, the latency is $35\text{ ms}$. Inference latency always measures the interval from stimulus to decision rather than the decision time alone.

For a collection of inputs, the same subtraction is performed for each sample. If three stimuli arrive at $10$, $30$, and $50\text{ ms}$, while their corresponding decisions occur at $17$, $41$, and $58\text{ ms}$, their latencies are $7$, $11$, and $8\text{ ms}$. These per-sample values reveal that response time can vary even when one model and one decision rule are used. Any reported aggregate latency therefore depends on those measured intervals and should preserve a consistent definition of stimulus and decision across the set.

Latency describes speed along one evaluation axis. A shorter measured interval means the decision arrived sooner within the reported comparison. Correctness remains a separate property: a rapid incorrect decision still has low latency, while a slower correct decision may have higher latency. Energy consumption and other deployment costs also require their own measurements. Establishing stimulus time creates a precise boundary for the timing measurement, allowing response speed to be examined separately from model quality and deployment cost rather than blending all three into a vague idea of performance.

In spiking neural networks, the decision may emerge while spikes are being processed over time. The evaluator still applies the same structure: mark when the stimulus begins, define when the system has produced its decision, and measure the interval between them. If a decision requires a longer observation period before it can be produced, $t_{\text{decision}}$ moves farther from $t_{\text{stimulus}}$, and the measured latency increases. The metric therefore captures the full waiting period implied by the chosen decision rule.

**Question.** Can two evaluations of the same model report different latency values?

**Answer.** Yes. Different inputs can produce different decision times, and different evaluation procedures can define the decision moment differently. A fair interpretation checks that the stimulus boundary, decision rule, time unit, and measurement procedure are consistent. Under matched conditions, a lower latency represents a faster response.

A useful mental model is a stopwatch attached to inference. The arrival of the stimulus starts the watch, the model's defined decision stops it, and the elapsed reading is inference latency. The two timestamps make the measurement reproducible, their difference removes the arbitrary position on the clock, and the resulting interval isolates how quickly the model responds while accuracy, energy, and other performance properties remain available for separate evaluation.
