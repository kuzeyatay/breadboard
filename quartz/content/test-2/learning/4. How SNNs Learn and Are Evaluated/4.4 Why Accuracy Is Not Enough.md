## Why Accuracy Alone Leaves the Decision Unfinished

Imagine a small camera on a battery-powered robot watching a hallway. Most of the time, the scene barely changes. A person steps into view, the robot must recognize the event quickly, and the battery must last long enough for the robot to keep working. If two models both identify the person correctly, the accuracy number treats them as equal. The robot experiences them very differently: one may spend far more energy, wait longer before deciding, or produce many more spikes before reaching the same answer.

Accuracy measures how often a model's output matches the desired answer. During inference, the model receives an input and produces a decision, such as "gesture detected," "digit is 7," or "obstacle ahead." If the model answers correctly on 98 out of 100 test examples, its accuracy is 98%. That number is useful because every deployed system needs correct decisions. A medical sensor, robot, or edge device cannot be called successful if it frequently chooses the wrong class.

A spiking neural network adds another layer to evaluation because its computation unfolds through events over time. A neuron collects incoming spikes, its membrane potential rises or leaks away, and it emits a spike when the potential crosses a threshold. The model's answer comes from a pattern of spikes rather than a single pass of continuous numerical activations. That event-based style creates the possibility of saving energy: silence can mean no work is needed, and sparse spikes can reduce computation. It also creates new questions. How many spikes were required? How long did the model need to observe the input? How much energy did the inference consume?

A deployment metric is a measurement that describes how a model behaves in the situation where it will actually be used. Accuracy is one deployment metric, but an SNN often needs several: energy per inference, latency, spike count, and convergence behavior. Energy per inference means the amount of energy used to produce one decision. Latency means the time from receiving the input to producing a usable output. Spike count means the number of spike events generated while processing one input. Convergence behavior describes how quickly learning settles into useful performance during training.

Think of these metrics as different readings on the same machine. A car's top speed tells you one thing, fuel use tells you another, braking distance tells you another, and reliability over time tells you another. A single number can be true while leaving out the property that determines whether the car suits the trip. In the same way, an SNN with high accuracy can still be a poor choice for a tiny edge device if it burns too much energy or waits too long before deciding.

The accuracy-energy-latency tradeoff appears because improving one behavior can put pressure on another. A converted SNN can inherit strong accuracy from an artificial neural network, but it may need a longer simulation window and a larger number of spikes to represent values through firing rates. More spikes usually mean more event processing, and more event processing tends to raise energy use. A surrogate-gradient SNN can learn spike patterns more directly and may reach high accuracy with lower latency, such as around 10 milliseconds in the reported comparison, while using moderate energy. An STDP-based SNN can produce very sparse activity and energy as low as about 5 millijoules per inference, while giving up some accuracy and converging more slowly.

A concrete comparison makes the tradeoff easier to feel. An ANN-style CNN may consume about 200 millijoules per inference because it relies on continuous-valued operations throughout the network. A converted SNN can reduce energy to about 20 millijoules, but may still generate around 20,000 spikes because rate-coded information needs many events to express a value reliably. A surrogate-gradient SNN can reduce energy further to about 15 millijoules with roughly 12,000 spikes by learning spiking patterns more efficiently. An STDP-based SNN can reach about 5 millijoules and roughly 4,000 spikes, giving the sparsest activity among the compared models. Those numbers show why accuracy alone gives an incomplete picture: the same task can be solved with very different amounts of time-dependent event activity and energy cost.

**Question.** If an ANN has the highest accuracy, should it automatically be chosen over an SNN?

**Answer.** The choice depends on the deployment goal. If the system runs in a setting where power, latency, and event activity barely matter, the highest raw accuracy may be preferred. If the system runs on a battery-powered or real-time edge device, a slightly less accurate SNN may be better because it can answer with far lower energy and shorter latency. The practical decision comes from comparing accuracy together with energy per inference, latency, spike count, and training behavior.

Latency deserves special attention because SNNs process information over time. A model may eventually reach a correct answer, but a robot, gesture interface, or event camera often needs the answer quickly. If a model requires a long simulation window before enough spikes have arrived, it may be accurate on a benchmark while feeling sluggish in the real task. Surrogate-trained SNNs can support low-latency targets because learning shapes when spikes occur, helping the network reach a decision earlier. In gesture-recognition settings, reported SNN latencies of about 5-10 milliseconds compare favorably with ANN counterparts around 20-50 milliseconds, which means the timing difference can affect whether the system feels responsive.

Spike count connects timing and energy to the internal behavior of the network. Each spike is a communication event. A sparse network sends fewer messages, and fewer messages can reduce energy use on hardware designed for event-driven computation. A beginner may wonder whether fewer spikes always means a better model. The useful picture is that spikes are costly but also carry information. Too many spikes waste energy, while too few useful spikes can make the decision unreliable. Evaluation asks whether the network uses enough spikes to decide accurately and quickly, while avoiding unnecessary activity.

Convergence adds the training side of the story. A model that eventually performs well may still require more training time or may improve more slowly under its learning rule. Surrogate-gradient methods can converge faster by using gradient-based updates adapted for spiking behavior. STDP-based learning follows local timing relationships between spikes and can be attractive for low-power unsupervised settings, while often showing slower convergence and somewhat lower accuracy. Comparing convergence with deployment metrics keeps the evaluation connected to both building the model and using the model.

**Question.** Why include spike count if energy is already measured directly?

**Answer.** Energy gives the final cost, while spike count explains part of the behavior that produces that cost. Two models can have similar accuracy, but the one with many more spikes is doing more event communication during inference. Spike count helps reveal whether the network is using sparse spiking efficiently or relying on heavy activity that may reduce the practical advantage of an SNN.

A unified comparison prevents one metric from carrying more meaning than it can support. Saying "this model is best" after looking only at accuracy can hide a slow decision. Saying "this model is best" after looking only at energy can hide a damaging accuracy drop. Saying "this model is best" after looking only at latency can hide unstable training or excessive spike activity. A practical comparison lines the models up across accuracy, latency, energy, spike count, and convergence, then chooses according to the application.

The mental model is a set of connected dials rather than a scoreboard with one winner. Accuracy tells whether the answer is right. Latency tells when the answer arrives. Energy tells what the answer costs. Spike count tells how much event activity the network used to get there. Convergence tells how the learning process behaved on the way to that deployed model. An SNN becomes valuable when those dials fit the task: a fast surrogate-trained network for low-latency accuracy targets, a sparse STDP-based network for ultra-low-power settings, or another design when the balance calls for it. Evaluation needs more than a single accuracy number because an SNN is meant to be judged as a working time-and-energy system, not only as a classifier that eventually produces the right label.
