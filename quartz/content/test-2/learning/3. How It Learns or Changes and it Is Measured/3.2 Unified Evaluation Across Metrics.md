### Unified Evaluation Across Metrics

After seeing how spiking neural networks learn through different training methods, the natural question becomes how to judge the result. Imagine choosing a model for a small vision sensor on a drone. The drone must recognize an obstacle correctly, react in a few milliseconds, and preserve battery power while doing it. A model with excellent accuracy can still be a poor choice if it takes too long to respond or burns too much energy per decision. A useful comparison therefore has to ask several questions at once: How often is the answer correct? How fast does the model respond? How much energy does one inference consume? How many spikes does the network produce? How smoothly does training improve over time?

Accuracy is the easiest metric to understand because it matches the everyday idea of being right. If a model classifies 1,000 images and gets 978 correct, its accuracy is 97.8%. That number is valuable because the model still has to solve the task. In a spiking neural network, though, accuracy only describes the final answer. It does not describe the path the network took to get there. Two SNNs can reach similar accuracy while behaving very differently inside: one may fire many spikes over a long simulation window, while another may fire fewer spikes and settle quickly. Those internal differences become visible when the model runs on neuromorphic or edge hardware, where spikes correspond to actual communication events and energy use.

Latency measures how long the model needs to produce its answer. In a spiking system, time is part of the computation because neurons accumulate input and emit spikes across discrete moments. If a surrogate-gradient SNN reaches about 97.8% accuracy with roughly 10 ms latency, it is doing more than classifying correctly; it is producing a useful answer quickly enough for time-sensitive settings. A gesture-recognition system, for example, gains practical value when it reacts in 5-10 ms rather than 20-50 ms, because the delay is felt directly in control loops, robotics, and interactive edge devices. The learner can think of latency as the model's reaction time: accuracy says whether the answer is right, while latency says whether the answer arrives soon enough to act on.

Energy measures the cost of producing one inference. In conventional artificial neural networks, computation often relies on dense continuous-valued operations, so many units update even when the input changes only slightly. Spiking networks use discrete events, so silent neurons can avoid unnecessary work. This is why energy has to be measured beside accuracy. An ANN may consume around 200 mJ per inference, while converted SNNs can reduce this to around 20 mJ, surrogate-gradient SNNs to around 15 mJ, and STDP-based SNNs to around 5 mJ. These numbers change the interpretation of "best." A method that trails slightly in accuracy may become preferable when the device has a tiny battery or must run continuously.

Spike count helps explain where the energy difference comes from. A spike is an event sent from one neuron to another, and each event has a communication cost. A converted SNN can still produce a relatively high number of spikes, around 20,000 per inference, because it often represents information through firing rates over a simulation window. A surrogate-gradient SNN can learn more efficient spiking patterns, reducing activity to about 12,000 spikes while keeping high accuracy. An STDP-based SNN can become even sparser, around 4,000 spikes per inference, which helps explain its very low energy consumption. A helpful analogy is a group chat: if every person sends repeated updates, communication becomes expensive; if only the necessary messages are sent at the right moments, the same group can coordinate with far less traffic.

Convergence measures how learning changes over training. A model that reaches strong performance by the 20th epoch is easier to train and tune than one that improves slowly or unstably. An epoch is one full pass through the training data. If two methods end near similar accuracy but one reaches that level earlier, the faster-converging method can save training time and make experimentation more predictable. Surrogate-gradient training often has this advantage because it uses gradient-based updates adapted for spiking neurons. STDP, which changes connections based on the relative timing of spikes, can produce very sparse and energy-efficient activity, but it may converge more slowly and reach lower final accuracy.

A unified evaluation protocol means every method is compared using the same set of measurements rather than being praised for whichever metric makes it look strongest. Surrogate-gradient SNNs balance several goals at once: high accuracy around 97.8%, low latency around 10 ms, and moderate energy around 15 mJ per inference. STDP-based SNNs achieve the lowest energy, around 5 mJ, and the sparsest spike activity, but their accuracy may be closer to 95.5% and their learning may take longer to settle. Converted SNNs can preserve competitive performance after translating an ANN into spikes, yet their higher spike counts and longer simulation windows can reduce their efficiency advantage. A single leaderboard would flatten these differences into one rank, while a multi-metric view preserves the actual engineering choice.

**Question.** If one SNN has higher accuracy and another uses much less energy, which one is better?

**Answer.** The better model is the one that fits the deployment goal. A medical image classifier running on a powerful workstation may prioritize accuracy. A battery-powered sensor that wakes up thousands of times per day may prioritize energy and spike sparsity. A robot avoiding obstacles may prioritize latency because a late correct answer can still fail the task. Multi-metric comparison keeps the choice tied to the real constraint.

The tradeoff becomes sharper as tasks scale. On larger image problems, accuracy gaps can widen: an ANN may reach about 92% while a direct SNN may fall near 85%. At the same time, energy savings can become more pronounced, with surrogate-trained SNNs operating at less than 10% of the ANN energy cost. This creates a practical tension. Larger tasks demand stronger representation power, yet edge deployment rewards sparse, low-energy computation. A model selection process that only asks "Which method is most accurate?" misses the reason SNNs are attractive in the first place: they can move useful computation closer to sensors while spending far less energy.

**Question.** Why include spike count if energy is already measured?

**Answer.** Energy is the final cost, while spike count helps explain the behavior that creates that cost. Fewer spikes usually mean fewer communication events between neurons, which often lowers energy on spike-based hardware. Spike count also reveals whether a method is using the temporal code efficiently. Two models with similar energy numbers may still differ in spike activity, giving clues about how they might scale to larger networks or different hardware.

A strong mental model is to treat evaluation as a control panel rather than a single score. Accuracy checks whether the model understands the task. Latency checks whether it answers in time. Energy checks whether the answer is affordable. Spike count checks how much event traffic the network creates. Convergence checks how efficiently learning reaches a useful state. Once these measurements are viewed together, an SNN method is no longer judged as universally best or worst. It becomes a design choice: surrogate-gradient training fits low-latency accuracy targets, STDP fits ultra-low-power sparse operation, and converted SNNs fit cases where an existing ANN can be carried into a spiking form with acceptable efficiency costs.
