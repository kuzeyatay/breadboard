## Unified Evaluation Across Metrics

By this point, an SNN has several moving parts in view: a training method changes the network, accuracy tells how often its answers are correct, latency tells how quickly it responds, and energy tells how much work the hardware spends to produce an answer. Imagine choosing a spiking model for a small camera on a battery-powered robot. The robot needs to recognize a hand gesture, decide quickly, and avoid draining its battery. A model that wins by accuracy alone may still be a poor choice if it takes too long to respond or burns too much energy each time it thinks.

Accuracy is the most familiar measurement because it answers a clean question: out of all test examples, what fraction did the model classify correctly? If a model sees 10,000 test images and labels 9,780 correctly, its accuracy is 97.8%. That number is useful because learning should improve the model's predictions. But an SNN is usually chosen because spikes can make computation sparse in time. Sparse means the model can stay mostly quiet and only spend effort when events occur. A measurement system that only asks "Was the final answer right?" misses how much activity was needed to reach that answer.

That is why a multi-metric comparison measures several quantities under one evaluation protocol. A metric is one measurable property of performance. A protocol is the shared testing procedure that keeps the comparison fair: the same task, the same kind of inputs, the same reporting rules, and the same set of metrics for each method. Without a shared protocol, one model might look strong because it was tested only for accuracy, while another looks weaker because its energy and latency were also exposed. A unified evaluation makes each method answer the same set of practical questions: How correct is it? How fast is it? How much energy does it use? How many spikes does it generate? How quickly does training settle into good performance?

The reason spike count belongs beside energy is that spikes are the events that drive work in an SNN. In conventional dense neural computation, layers often process continuous numerical activations whether or not the input has changed much. In a spiking system, a neuron sends a discrete event when its internal state reaches a firing condition. Fewer spikes usually mean fewer event-driven operations, so spike count gives a visible trace of computational activity. For example, a converted SNN may reduce energy compared with an ANN, yet still generate around 20,000 spikes per inference because rate-coded propagation represents values through many spikes over time. A surrogate-gradient SNN can learn more efficient spiking patterns, using around 12,000 spikes per inference with about 15 mJ of energy. An STDP-based SNN can be even sparser, around 4,000 spikes and about 5 mJ per inference, because its local timing-based learning favors economical activity. The spike count helps explain why the energy number changes instead of leaving energy as a mysterious hardware measurement.

Latency measures a different kind of cost: time. If a model needs a long simulation window to accumulate enough spikes before deciding, it may be accurate but sluggish. A converted SNN can be competitive in accuracy because it inherits behavior from a trained ANN, yet it may need more spikes and a longer window to represent continuous values through firing rates. A surrogate-gradient SNN can reach strong accuracy while responding in roughly 10 milliseconds because training directly shapes the timing and pattern of spikes. In a real-time setting, such as gesture recognition or robotics, that difference changes the system's behavior. A 5-10 ms response can feel immediate to a controller, while 20-50 ms may limit how smoothly the machine reacts to a changing scene.

Energy, latency, and accuracy often pull against one another, and that pull is called a tradeoff. A tradeoff appears when improving one goal tends to make another goal harder to satisfy. The word does not mean one method is bad; it means the best method depends on what the system is trying to do. Surrogate-gradient SNNs can balance high accuracy, around 97.8%, with low latency around 10 ms and moderate energy around 15 mJ per inference. STDP-based SNNs can achieve much lower energy, around 5 mJ, and very sparse activity, while giving up some accuracy, around 95.5%, and converging more slowly. If the device is a battery-powered sensor that only needs a rough classification, STDP may be attractive. If the device is a responsive edge system that needs high accuracy quickly, surrogate-gradient training may be the better fit.

Convergence adds the training-time side of the story. A model's final score shows where it ended up, but convergence shows how it got there. During training, accuracy or loss can be tracked across epochs, where one epoch means one full pass through the training data. If one method reaches useful performance by the 20th epoch while another is still improving slowly, the faster-converging method may be easier to tune and deploy. Surrogate-gradient training often has an advantage here because it uses gradient-based optimization adapted to spikes, allowing the model to adjust toward a task objective more directly. STDP changes weights through local timing relationships between pre-synaptic and post-synaptic spikes, so it can be energy-efficient and biologically inspired while following a slower path to high task accuracy.

**Question.** If one SNN has 97.8% accuracy and another has 95.5% accuracy, should the higher-accuracy model always be chosen?

**Answer.** The higher-accuracy model is the better choice only when accuracy is the main constraint. If the lower-accuracy model uses far less energy and produces far fewer spikes, it may be better for a tiny battery-powered or always-on device. A difference of 2.3 percentage points in accuracy may be acceptable when the energy cost drops from about 15 mJ to about 5 mJ per inference. The evaluation has to match the job the model will actually perform.

A beginner confusion often comes from treating "best model" as a single fixed title. In SNN evaluation, "best" is a relationship between the model and the deployment goal. A model for a medical monitoring device, a mobile robot, and an offline image classifier can be judged differently because their failure costs, response-time needs, and energy budgets differ. Accuracy remains part of the decision, but it shares the decision with physical constraints.

The same reasoning becomes stronger as tasks scale up. On simpler image tasks, several methods may appear close in accuracy. On larger tasks, such as moving from MNIST-scale problems to CIFAR-10-scale problems, accuracy gaps can widen; ANNs may reach around 92% while direct SNNs may drop closer to 85%. Yet energy savings can also become more dramatic, with surrogate-trained SNNs operating at less than 10% of ANN energy costs. A larger task therefore makes the evaluation more practical, because it asks whether a small loss in accuracy buys a large gain in efficiency.

**Question.** Why measure spike count if energy is already measured directly?

**Answer.** Energy tells the total cost per inference, while spike count helps explain the source of that cost. Since spikes trigger event-driven computation, a model that produces fewer spikes usually performs fewer operations on neuromorphic hardware. Spike count also reveals whether a method is genuinely using sparse spiking behavior or merely translating dense computation into many spike events.

A unified evaluation is the habit of looking at an SNN as a working system rather than a single score. Accuracy asks whether the answer is right. Latency asks whether the answer arrives in time. Energy asks whether the answer is affordable to compute. Spike count asks how much event activity produced the answer. Convergence asks how efficiently learning reaches useful behavior. Together, these measurements form a mental model of SNN selection: each training method shapes both what the network knows and how it spends time, spikes, and energy to express that knowledge.
