After tracking whether an SNN makes the correct decision, the natural follow-up is how quickly that decision appears. Imagine a small vision sensor on a robot facing a doorway. Most of the scene stays still, then a person steps into view. The sensor delivers a stimulus: an input pattern that begins at a particular moment. The SNN responds through spike timing, where neurons communicate by sending brief events rather than continuously updating every value. The robot can only act after the network has gathered enough spike evidence to choose a class, such as "person," "empty doorway," or "moving object." Latency measures the waiting time between the stimulus arriving and the decision becoming available.

A decision in an SNN is the moment when the network's output can be read as an answer. In a simple classifier, several output neurons may represent possible classes. The class whose output neuron reaches the required evidence level, fires strongly enough, or accumulates the clearest spike pattern becomes the prediction. The exact decision rule can vary by model, but the timing idea stays the same: the stimulus starts the response, spikes carry evidence forward, and the decision time marks when the model has enough information to choose.

The formula records that relationship directly:
$$
\text{Latency} = t_{\text{decision}} - t_{\text{stimulus}}
$$
Here, $\text{Latency}$ is the elapsed response time of the model. The symbol $t_{\text{stimulus}}$ means the time when the input stimulus begins or arrives at the network. The symbol $t_{\text{decision}}$ means the time when the model produces its prediction. Subtracting the start time from the decision time gives the amount of time spent turning input into output. When time is measured in milliseconds, abbreviated ms, a latency of $10\text{ ms}$ means the model takes ten thousandths of a second to respond.

A concrete timeline makes the formula feel less abstract. Suppose a gesture appears at $t_{\text{stimulus}} = 100\text{ ms}$. The SNN receives the early spikes from the input, internal neurons integrate those events, and an output neuron reaches the decision condition at $t_{\text{decision}} = 108\text{ ms}$. The latency is
$$
108\text{ ms} - 100\text{ ms} = 8\text{ ms}
$$
That value says the model needed $8\text{ ms}$ to convert the gesture stimulus into a prediction. If another model receives the same stimulus at $100\text{ ms}$ and decides at $130\text{ ms}$, its latency is $30\text{ ms}$. Both models may eventually choose the same correct class, but the first one supports faster action.

The connection to spike timing gives latency a special role in SNNs. A spike carries information by occurring at a particular time. Early spikes can provide fast evidence, while later spikes can refine or strengthen the decision. A network that waits for many spikes may gather a richer signal, but it also delays the answer. A network that uses a small number of well-timed spikes can decide quickly, which is useful for real-time settings such as gesture recognition, robotics, and edge devices that must react while the world is still changing.

Latency also helps explain why accuracy alone gives an incomplete picture of model behavior. Accuracy counts how often the prediction is correct across many examples, while latency measures how long each prediction takes. A model with high accuracy and high latency may be acceptable for offline analysis, where waiting is harmless. A model with slightly lower accuracy and very low latency may be better for a moving robot, where a late correct answer can arrive after the useful moment has passed. For SNNs, reported response times as low as about $10\text{ ms}$, and gesture-recognition latencies around $5\text{-}10\text{ ms}$ for surrogate-trained SNNs, show how fast spike-based decisions can become practical in real-time systems.

**Question.** If two models both classify 970 out of 1000 examples correctly, why compare their latency?

**Answer.** The equal correct count means their accuracy is the same, but their usefulness can still differ. If one model usually decides in $10\text{ ms}$ and the other usually decides in $50\text{ ms}$, the faster model can support quicker responses. In real-time tasks, the delay between stimulus and decision affects whether the prediction arrives soon enough to guide action.

Low latency often interacts with energy use and spike count. An SNN saves energy partly because silence is allowed: neurons send spikes only when events need to be communicated. Pushing a model to decide faster can encourage earlier or more concentrated activity, depending on the design. Waiting longer can sometimes allow sparse activity to accumulate gradually, which may reduce energy but delay the prediction. Energy-efficient models such as STDP-based SNNs can use very low spike counts and low energy per inference, while surrogate-gradient SNNs can offer a strong balance of high accuracy, low latency around $10\text{ ms}$, and moderate energy. The practical choice depends on whether the task values speed, energy savings, accuracy, or a balance among them.

A beginner confusion often comes from treating latency as the same thing as simulation length. A simulation window is the amount of time the model is allowed to run; latency is the time the model actually needs before the decision appears. If a model is allowed $50\text{ ms}$ but consistently decides by $10\text{ ms}$, the latency is $10\text{ ms}$ under that decision rule. The extra allowed time may provide room for later spikes, but the measured response speed comes from the stimulus-to-decision interval.

**Question.** If a model has lower latency, does that automatically mean it is the best SNN?

**Answer.** Lower latency means the model responds faster, but model choice depends on the whole performance profile. A very fast model may use more activity than an ultra-low-power model, or it may trade some accuracy for speed. Latency should be compared with accuracy, energy consumption, spike count, and convergence behavior so the chosen SNN fits the application.

The mental model is a stopwatch attached to a spike-based decision process. The stopwatch starts when the stimulus enters the network. Spikes then move evidence through the system, with their timing shaping how quickly output evidence builds. The stopwatch stops when the model's prediction is ready. The latency formula, $\text{Latency} = t_{\text{decision}} - t_{\text{stimulus}}$, simply names that elapsed time, but the number carries practical meaning: it tells whether an SNN can turn event-based input into action quickly enough for the task while still being judged alongside accuracy and energy.
