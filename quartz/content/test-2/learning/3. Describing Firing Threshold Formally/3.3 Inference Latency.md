## Inference Latency

Once a firing threshold turns accumulated input into an output event, timing becomes part of the model's behavior. Imagine a gesture sensor receiving its first event when a hand begins to move. The network processes the incoming activity until it commits to a gesture label. A fast decision may support immediate control of a robot or wearable device, while a delayed decision leaves the application waiting even if the final label is correct. Measuring that delay requires two clearly defined moments: when the stimulus begins and when the model produces its decision.

The **stimulus onset time** is the timestamp assigned to the beginning of the input being evaluated. For an event-based sensor, this can be the arrival time of the first event in a sample. For an input presented as a complete item, it can be the moment that item becomes available to the model. We write this timestamp as $t_{\text{onset}}$, where $t$ represents time and the subscript identifies the event being timed.

The **decision time** is the timestamp at which the model produces the output accepted as its decision. We write it as $t_{\text{decision}}$. A decision rule must be applied consistently during a comparison. One evaluation might record the time when an output neuron first crosses a firing threshold; another might wait until accumulated output activity identifies a stable class. Either convention can define a decision time, provided every model is measured from the same starting event to an equivalent decision event.

A timestamp tells us where an event lies on a clock. Latency tells us how much time passes between two events. Starting from the stimulus onset, the model remains in its inference interval until the decision appears. The duration of that interval is therefore the ending timestamp minus the starting timestamp:

$$
L_{\text{inference}} = t_{\text{decision}} - t_{\text{onset}}
$$
Here, $L_{\text{inference}}$ is the **inference latency**, the elapsed time required to produce a decision. The quantity $t_{\text{decision}}$ is the decision time, and $t_{\text{onset}}$ is the stimulus onset time. When both timestamps are measured in milliseconds, their difference is also measured in milliseconds. The formula lets us compute a duration even when the stimulus begins at a clock reading other than zero.

Suppose a sensor begins presenting a gesture at $125\ \text{ms}$, and the model commits to a class at $137\ \text{ms}$. Substituting the two timestamps gives
$$
L_{\text{inference}} = 137\ \text{ms} - 125\ \text{ms} = 12\ \text{ms}
$$
The model therefore spends $12\ \text{ms}$ moving from stimulus onset to decision. If the same stimulus begins at $400\ \text{ms}$ and the model decides at $412\ \text{ms}$, the latency remains $12\ \text{ms}$. The clock readings changed, while the elapsed interval stayed the same. If the onset remains fixed at $125\ \text{ms}$ and the decision shifts to $145\ \text{ms}$, latency grows to $20\ \text{ms}$. A later decision increases latency whenever the starting time stays fixed.

**Question.** Why subtract the stimulus onset time instead of reporting the decision timestamp by itself?

**Answer.** The decision timestamp depends on when the measurement clock happened to start. A decision at $500\ \text{ms}$ could represent a $10\ \text{ms}$ response to a stimulus that began at $490\ \text{ms}$, or a $100\ \text{ms}$ response to one that began at $400\ \text{ms}$. Subtracting $t_{\text{onset}}$ removes that arbitrary clock offset and isolates the interval during which the model processed the stimulus.

Latency comparisons become especially useful when model families represent and process information differently. An artificial neural network, or ANN, typically moves continuous-valued activations through its layers. A converted spiking neural network begins from an ANN representation and expresses its activity through spikes, often over a simulation window long enough to represent activation strength through spike rates. A surrogate-gradient SNN is trained directly with a gradient-based approximation that supports learning useful spike patterns. An STDP-based SNN learns through changes associated with spike timing and can develop its response over evolving spike activity.

Latency answers a focused question: how long does the model take to decide after the stimulus begins? Accuracy answers how often those decisions are correct, while energy per inference measures the computational energy used to produce them. A model with a shorter decision delay may have a different accuracy or energy profile, so choosing a model requires reading these measurements as separate coordinates of performance. The surrogate-gradient SNN's short bar establishes a low reported decision delay; its accuracy must be evaluated with the accuracy results. The STDP-based SNN's latency describes response speed; its sparse spiking and low energy belong to separate measurements.

**Question.** If one model has half the latency of another, does that establish that it is the better model?

**Answer.** It establishes that the model reaches its decision in half the elapsed time under the stated measurement conditions. A complete choice also considers whether the decisions meet the required accuracy, whether energy use fits the available budget, and whether both models were tested with comparable decision rules and hardware conditions. Latency provides the speed coordinate of that comparison.

A useful mental model is a stopwatch attached to inference. Stimulus onset presses start, the accepted model decision presses stop, and the difference between those timestamps is inference latency. Firing thresholds and accumulated activity determine when the stop event occurs, while the latency equation turns that event timing into a comparable duration. Graphs then reveal relative delays at a glance, tables attach those delays to specific model families, and the surrounding metrics complete the practical picture of how each model behaves.
