## Spike Count and Energy per Inference

Once a neuron's membrane potential reaches its firing threshold, the neuron emits a spike during a particular time step. Following one neuron for one time step tells us whether that single event occurred. Estimating the activity of an entire inference requires widening the view: we must count spike events across every included neuron and every simulated time step, then connect those events to the computations they trigger.

Imagine an SNN containing three neurons observed for four time steps. Each neuron produces a short binary sequence, where $1$ means that it emitted a spike and $0$ means that it remained silent. Suppose their sequences are $1,0,0,1$, $0,1,0,0$, and $0,0,1,0$. The network emitted four spikes altogether. That count describes how much firing occurred while processing one input, such as one image or one sensor sample.

We can formalize this counting process by assigning a **spike indicator** to every neuron-time pair:
$$
S_{\text{total}}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{t=1}^{T}s_i[t]
$$
Here, $i$ is the **neuron index**, identifying one of the $N$ included neurons. The symbol $t$ is the **time-step index**, identifying one of the $T$ simulated time steps. The quantity $s_i[t]$ is the spike indicator for neuron $i$ at time step $t$: it equals $1$ when that neuron emits a spike at that time and $0$ otherwise. Each value of $1$ represents one **spike event**. The result $S_{\text{total}}$ is the **total spike count** for the inference.

The double sum gives a precise instruction. For each neuron, add its spike indicators across time; then add those neuron totals across the network. In the three-neuron example,
$$
S_{\text{total}}=(1+0+0+1)+(0+1+0+0)+(0+0+1+0)=4
$$
This formula also reveals how the simulation window affects the metric. Extending the inference from $T$ time steps to a longer window creates more opportunities for neurons to fire. Comparing spike counts therefore requires the same counting boundary: the included neurons, input sample, and simulation duration must be clear.

A spike count measures emitted events, while an energy estimate must also account for the work associated with those events. Emitting or handling a spike has an energy cost, and transmitting activity through synaptic connections produces synaptic operations. A **synaptic operation** occurs when information is processed across a connection between neurons. One emitted spike may reach several downstream connections, so a single spike can lead to several synaptic operations.

For example, suppose one neuron spikes and sends that event to five connected neurons. The network records one emitted spike, while the fan-out from that spike can produce five synaptic operations. Another neuron with twenty outgoing connections can produce twenty synaptic operations from one spike. Network connectivity therefore influences computation even when two networks emit the same number of spikes.

Let $E_{\text{spike}}$ be the energy cost of one spike event and $E_{\text{synop}}$ be the energy cost of one synaptic operation. If an inference produces $S_{\text{total}}$ spikes and $N_{\text{synop}}$ synaptic operations, their estimated energy contributions can be added:
$$
E_{\text{inference}}
=
S_{\text{total}}E_{\text{spike}}
+
N_{\text{synop}}E_{\text{synop}}
$$
In this expression, $S_{\text{total}}$ is the total spike count, $E_{\text{spike}}$ is the **spike-event cost**, $N_{\text{synop}}$ is the **synaptic-operation count**, and $E_{\text{synop}}$ is the **synaptic-operation cost**. The result $E_{\text{inference}}$ is the **estimated energy per inference**, measured in joules when both per-operation costs are expressed in joules. A millijoule, abbreviated mJ, equals one-thousandth of a joule.

The two products represent two energy contributions. The term $S_{\text{total}}E_{\text{spike}}$ computes the energy associated with all spike events. The term $N_{\text{synop}}E_{\text{synop}}$ computes the energy associated with all synaptic operations. Adding them gives the model's total estimate for processing one input.

Consider an illustrative inference with $4{,}000$ spikes and $30{,}000$ synaptic operations. If each spike event costs $2$ energy units and each synaptic operation costs $0.5$ energy units, the spike contribution is $4{,}000\times2=8{,}000$ units and the synaptic contribution is $30{,}000\times0.5=15{,}000$ units. The estimated total is $23{,}000$ energy units. The example shows why the synaptic term can dominate even when the spike count appears small: each spike can activate multiple connections.

**Question.** If two SNNs each emit 10,000 spikes, must they have the same estimated energy per inference?

**Answer.** Their estimated energies can differ because the spike counts determine only the first term. Different connectivity can produce different synaptic-operation counts, and different operation costs change the energy assigned to both spike events and synaptic operations. Equal spike counts lead to equal spike-event contributions only when their per-spike costs are also equal.

**Question.** Why keep spike count as a separate metric when energy per inference already gives a total?

**Answer.** Spike count describes the network's firing activity directly and can be computed from neuron outputs across time. Energy per inference combines that activity with operation counts and per-operation costs. Keeping both measurements exposes whether a lower energy estimate accompanies fewer spikes, fewer triggered synaptic operations, lower operation costs, or some combination of these factors.

The complete mental model begins at the firing threshold: threshold crossings create spike indicators, spike indicators sum across neurons and time to produce total spike count, and emitted spikes drive work through synaptic connections. Spike-event cost prices the events themselves, while synaptic-operation cost prices the connection-level computation they cause. Energy per inference emerges by adding those two contributions, so spike count serves as one essential input to the estimate rather than the entire estimate.
