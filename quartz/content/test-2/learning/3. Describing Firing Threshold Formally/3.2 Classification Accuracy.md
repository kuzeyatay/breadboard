## Classification Accuracy

Once a spiking neural network has turned its firing activity into a predicted class, each evaluated prediction can be checked against the correct class. A gesture recognizer might predict "wave," for example, while the recorded label identifies the gesture that actually occurred. Agreement makes the prediction correct; disagreement makes it incorrect. Repeating this check across many inputs produces a collection of correct and incorrect classifications. Classification accuracy compresses those checks into one percentage that answers a focused question: what percentage of the evaluated predictions received the correct class?

Begin with a small batch of predictions. Suppose a network evaluates five gestures and assigns the correct class to four of them. Each correct prediction contributes one to the correct-prediction count, so the numerator becomes $4$. Every prediction that was checked contributes one to the total-prediction count, so the denominator becomes $5$. Dividing $4$ by $5$ gives $0.8$. Multiplying by $100\%$ expresses that proportion as a percentage, so the network classified $80\%$ of that batch correctly.

This counting process leads directly to the percentage-valued accuracy formula:
$$
\text{Classification accuracy}
=
\frac{\text{number of correct predictions}}
{\text{total number of evaluated predictions}}
\times 100\%
$$
Before converting to a percentage, the underlying proportion can be represented with the conceptual helper
$$
A=\frac{C}{N}
$$
Where:

- $A$ is the fraction of evaluated predictions that received the correct class;
- $C$ is the number of evaluated predictions assigned the correct class;
- $N$ is the total number of predictions evaluated.

The denominator $N$ must be greater than zero because a proportion requires at least one evaluated prediction. Since every correct prediction is also an evaluated prediction, $C$ can range from $0$ to $N$. The proportion $A$ therefore ranges from $0$ to $1$. A value of $0$ means none of the evaluated predictions were correct, while a value of $1$ means all of them were correct.

Multiplying the proportion by $100\%$ gives classification accuracy as a percentage:
$$
A_{\%}=\frac{C}{N}\times 100\%
$$
Where $A_{\%}$ is classification accuracy expressed as a percentage. For the five-gesture example,
$$
A_{\%}=\frac{4}{5}\times 100\%=80\%
$$
The fraction, decimal, and percentage all describe the same result:
$$
\frac{4}{5}=0.8=80\%
$$
The choice of form changes the presentation rather than the underlying quantity. The fraction preserves the visible counts, the decimal is convenient for calculations, and the percentage communicates how many predictions would be correct per hundred at the same rate.

Consider a larger worked interpretation. Suppose an SNN makes $240$ evaluated predictions and $216$ receive the correct class. Substituting these counts into the proportion gives
$$
A=\frac{216}{240}=0.9
$$
So
$$
A_{\%}=0.9\times 100\%=90\%
$$
This result says that $90\%$ of the evaluated predictions were correct. Equivalently, the observed rate corresponds to $90$ correct predictions per $100$ evaluated predictions. The original evaluation still contained $216$ correct predictions across $240$ cases; "per hundred" is a standardized interpretation of the ratio.

Reported classification accuracy should always be read together with the set on which it was calculated. A result above $93\%$ on dynamic gesture recognition means that more than $93$ out of every $100$ evaluated gesture predictions were correct at that observed rate. The percentage gives a compact measure of overall correctness for that evaluation. The identity of individual mistakes, the timing of the decisions, and the amount of spiking activity remain outside that single number.

The same compression that makes accuracy convenient also limits what it reveals. Imagine two evaluations that each contain $100$ predictions and $90$ correct answers. Both have
$$
\frac{90}{100}\times 100\%=90\%
$$
accuracy. One evaluation could scatter its ten errors across several kinds of inputs, while another could concentrate them on one kind of gesture. Accuracy records ten errors in each case and produces the same value. The error pattern requires additional inspection because the ratio retains only the total number correct and the total number evaluated.

**Question.** If a network makes more predictions, does its accuracy automatically increase?

**Answer.** Accuracy depends on the proportion that is correct rather than the number of predictions alone. A network with $9$ correct predictions out of $10$ has $90\%$ accuracy. A network with $900$ correct predictions out of $1{,}000$ also has $90\%$ accuracy. The larger evaluation provides more observations, while the calculated percentage remains the same.

**Question.** Can the number of incorrect predictions be recovered from $C$ and $N$?

**Answer.** Yes. Every evaluated prediction is either correct or incorrect, so the incorrect-prediction count is $N-C$. If $216$ of $240$ predictions are correct, then $240-216=24$ are incorrect. Accuracy uses the correct count directly, although the same result can be understood through the remaining errors: $24$ errors leave $216$ correct predictions, producing $(216/240)\times 100\%=90\%$.

A useful mental model is a container of evaluated predictions. Each prediction enters the container once, and every correct prediction receives a mark. The total number of marked items becomes $C$, the total number of items becomes $N$, and $C/N$ measures how densely the container is filled with correct results. Multiplying that density by $100\%$ expresses classification accuracy as a percentage. The arrangement of the unmarked items—the particular classes, inputs, or conditions associated with errors—requires information beyond the percentage.
