## Spike-Timing-Dependent Plasticity

A presynaptic neuron sends a spike across a connection, and a postsynaptic neuron emits its own spike a short time later. The two events may be separated by only a small interval, yet their order contains useful information: activity arrived at the connection, then the receiving neuron responded. If the postsynaptic spike occurred earlier, the timing relationship tells a different story. Spike-Timing-Dependent Plasticity, usually shortened to **STDP**, uses these relationships to adjust the connection strength between neurons.

The connection strength, often called its **weight**, determines how much influence a presynaptic spike has on the postsynaptic neuron. A stronger connection gives an arriving spike more influence over the receiving neuron's activity. A weaker connection gives it less influence. Learning therefore requires a rule for deciding when a weight should change and how large that change should be.

STDP makes this decision using information already available at the connection: the time of the presynaptic spike and the time of the postsynaptic spike. A **presynaptic spike** is the event arriving from the sending neuron. A **postsynaptic spike** is the event produced by the receiving neuron. Their **relative timing** includes both their order and their separation in time.

Imagine that the presynaptic spike occurs at 12 milliseconds and the postsynaptic spike occurs at 15 milliseconds. The presynaptic event leads by 3 milliseconds. Now imagine that the postsynaptic spike occurs at 9 milliseconds while the presynaptic spike still occurs at 12 milliseconds. The order has reversed, and the presynaptic event now follows by 3 milliseconds. The absolute clock readings are less useful than this relationship between the two events. STDP evaluates that relationship through a timing-dependent update rule.

The rule maps a timing relationship to a change in connection strength. Changing the order of the spikes can change the direction of the update, while changing their separation can change its size. Closely spaced events represent a different relationship from events separated by a long interval. The exact mathematical shape of this rule depends on the chosen STDP design, so the reliable mental model is a timing window: place the presynaptic spike and postsynaptic spike on the same timeline, observe which event comes first, measure how far apart they are, and use that pair of facts to determine the weight update.

Consider two connected neurons that repeatedly show the same pattern. The sending neuron spikes, and the receiving neuron spikes soon afterward. Each occurrence gives the connection a local timing observation. Repeated updates gradually shape the weight according to that recurring relationship. If the ordering reverses or the interval changes, the rule receives a different timing observation and can adjust the connection differently. Learning emerges from many small, event-driven changes rather than from one global decision made after all activity has finished.

This makes STDP a form of **local learning**. A connection needs the spike times associated with its two ends and the specified timing rule. The update is computed where the relevant events meet. A distant mechanism does not need to assign a detailed correction to every connection. Each connection adapts through the activity that directly reaches it and the activity produced by the neuron it helps drive.

Locality also explains how STDP can support **unsupervised adaptation**. In supervised learning, an external target identifies the desired answer and helps determine how the network should change. STDP can adapt from patterns within the spike stream itself. When some neurons tend to fire in consistent temporal relationships, their connections repeatedly encounter similar timing evidence. The network can therefore reorganize its weights around recurring event patterns without requiring a target label for every example.

Suppose a sensor repeatedly produces a short sequence of spikes when a familiar pattern appears. One neuron tends to spike early in that sequence, while another tends to spike shortly afterward. Their connection sees a stable order and a small time separation. STDP applies the same kind of local update each time this relationship returns. Over many presentations, the network's connection strengths come to reflect regularities in the timing of the input. The adaptation is unsupervised because the spike relationships themselves provide the learning signal.

**Question.** Does STDP learn from the number of spikes or from their timing?

**Answer.** STDP updates a connection according to the relative timing of presynaptic and postsynaptic spikes. Spike count can describe how active a network is, but the local learning signal here is the relationship between paired events: which spike occurred first and how much time separated them. Repeated spike pairs create repeated opportunities for timing-dependent updates.

A common beginner confusion is to picture the presynaptic spike as carrying a complete instruction such as "increase this weight." The spike is an event with a time. Its learning significance appears when that time is compared with the postsynaptic event. The timing rule performs the interpretation. The same presynaptic event can therefore lead to different updates when the postsynaptic timing changes.

Because learning is driven by discrete events, STDP fits naturally with sparse activity. A connection updates when relevant spikes occur, while periods without spikes require no event-based update. In the reported model comparison, the STDP-based SNN generated about 4,000 spikes per inference and consumed about 5 millijoules, the lowest spike count and energy consumption among the compared approaches. These values show the practical consequence of sparse event processing: fewer generated spikes can reduce the activity that consumes energy.

That efficiency comes with a training tradeoff. STDP-based networks converged more slowly in the comparison and showed slightly reduced accuracy, even while achieving the sparsest activity and lowest energy use. STDP is therefore especially suited to settings where unsupervised adaptation and very low energy consumption carry greater weight than rapid convergence or the highest possible accuracy. Application priorities determine whether that balance is desirable.

**Question.** Why can a local timing rule learn anything useful without a global error signal?

**Answer.** Repeated inputs create repeated temporal relationships among neurons. Each connection records those relationships through its own presynaptic and postsynaptic spike times. When a timing pattern occurs consistently, the corresponding local update also occurs consistently, gradually shaping the network around regularities in its activity. The learning signal comes from temporal structure rather than from a supplied answer.

The full mental model begins with two discrete events at opposite ends of a connection. Their order and separation form a timing relationship. A specific STDP rule translates that relationship into a weight change, and repeated local changes produce unsupervised adaptation. Because updates follow sparse spike activity, the resulting network can operate with low spike counts and low energy consumption, while its slower convergence reflects the gradual accumulation of many local timing decisions.
