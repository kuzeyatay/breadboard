## Surrogate-Gradient Training

A spiking neuron has already accumulated its inputs and reached the moment when it must decide whether to fire. Its output changes abruptly: below the threshold, no spike appears; at or above the threshold, a spike appears. That sharp event gives a spiking neural network its discrete communication style, yet it creates a difficulty during training. Gradient-based optimization needs to estimate how a small change in each weight would change the loss. Around a hard spike threshold, an extremely small change can leave the output unchanged or suddenly flip it from silence to a spike. The ordinary derivative therefore provides no useful gradual signal for adjusting the weights.

To see the difficulty concretely, imagine that a neuron's internal state finishes just below its firing threshold. Increasing one incoming weight by a tiny amount may still leave the neuron below threshold, so its spike output remains unchanged. Increasing the weight slightly further may push the neuron across the threshold, causing its output to jump immediately. A derivative expects a smooth local relationship between input and output, while the spike event creates a step. The learning process needs a usable estimate of sensitivity even though the forward computation contains this abrupt decision.

A **surrogate gradient** supplies that estimate. Training keeps the discrete spike event when the network processes an input: the neuron either emits a spike or remains silent according to its threshold rule. During the backward pass, when the loss is traced back through the network, the unusable derivative of that spike event is replaced with the derivative of a smooth approximation. The approximation is called a **surrogate derivative** because it stands in for the spike derivative only while gradients are being computed.

This creates two closely connected views of the same neuron. The forward view preserves the behavior being trained: inputs accumulate, the threshold is tested, and a discrete event may be emitted. The backward view treats the threshold region as if it had a smooth slope, allowing an error signal to pass through it. Piecewise-linear, sigmoid-shaped, and exponential-shaped approximations can provide such a slope. Each gives the optimizer a graded answer to a practical question: "If the neuron's state had moved slightly, how strongly might that have influenced the loss through the firing decision?"

Consider a neuron whose state lies very close to threshold and whose spike contributes to an incorrect prediction. A smooth surrogate typically assigns a relatively strong derivative near that threshold. The backward pass can then attribute part of the error to the weights that placed the neuron there. A neuron whose state lies far from threshold usually receives a weaker surrogate signal, because a small weight change is less likely to alter its firing decision. In this way, the approximation turns proximity to the spike boundary into a useful training signal.

The weight-adjustment rule follows the usual logic of gradient-based learning. The network first produces spikes and an output, and the output is compared with the desired result to obtain a loss. The backward pass then works from that loss toward earlier neurons. At every spike event, it uses the surrogate derivative to estimate how the loss responds to the neuron's internal state. That estimated sensitivity is combined with the influence of each incoming weight on the state. The optimizer finally changes the weight in the direction expected to reduce the loss. Repeating this procedure over training examples gradually shapes when and where spikes occur.

Suppose an input should produce activity associated with class A, but the network favors class B. The loss records the size of that error. During the backward pass, a neuron near its threshold may receive a signal indicating that firing more readily would have supported class A. Weights carrying useful evidence toward that neuron can then increase, moving its future state closer to or across threshold. Another neuron may receive the opposite signal, causing relevant weights to decrease so that an unhelpful spike becomes less likely. The surrogate derivative makes these targeted changes possible while the network continues to communicate through discrete events during its forward operation.

**Question.** Does the smooth approximation replace spikes throughout the network?

**Answer.** The network still uses discrete spike events during the forward pass. The smooth approximation is used during the backward pass to estimate a derivative where the hard threshold cannot provide a useful one. Training therefore evaluates the behavior of an actual spiking network while borrowing smooth sensitivity information for weight updates.

Because the loss can be propagated through successive spike-producing layers, the entire SNN can be optimized directly from input to output. This is **direct SNN optimization**: the weights and spiking patterns are learned together instead of beginning with a separately trained conventional network and converting it afterward. Direct training allows the optimization process to shape temporal and sparse activity as part of the task. Reported comparisons associate surrogate-trained SNNs with competitive accuracy, convergence by around the twentieth epoch, latency near 10 milliseconds, energy near 15 millijoules per inference, and roughly 12,000 spikes per inference. These values describe a balance among several goals rather than a guarantee for every network or dataset.

The choice of surrogate derivative influences that balance. A broad, gentle approximation can pass gradient information across a wider range of neuron states, which may make optimization easier, while also treating states farther from threshold as influential. A narrow, steep approximation concentrates learning around states most likely to change their firing decision, while fewer neurons receive substantial gradient signals. Computing and propagating these signals also adds training cost. The approximation is therefore chosen to provide enough gradient information for accurate, stable learning while preserving the efficiency sought from sparse spiking activity.

**Question.** If the surrogate derivative is only an approximation, why can it still improve the real spiking network?

**Answer.** The optimizer needs a direction that tends to reduce the loss, rather than a perfect description of every abrupt firing change. A well-chosen smooth slope links errors to neurons near their thresholds and then to the weights that control those neurons. Each update is evaluated later through another genuine forward pass of discrete spikes, so repeated training continually tests whether the approximate directions produce better spiking behavior.

The resulting mental model is a network with an exact event-driven path forward and a smooth learning path backward. Spikes carry the computation, the loss measures the outcome, the surrogate derivative carries responsibility through each threshold, and the optimizer adjusts the weights that determine future spike timing and activity. Surrogate-gradient training succeeds by joining those paths: discrete events define what the SNN does, while differentiable approximations provide the local guidance needed to improve it.
