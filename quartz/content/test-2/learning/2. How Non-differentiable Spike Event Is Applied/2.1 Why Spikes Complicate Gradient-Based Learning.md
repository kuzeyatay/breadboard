## Why Spikes Complicate Gradient-Based Learning

A firing threshold gives a spiking neuron a clear decision rule: accumulated input raises the neuron's internal state, and crossing the threshold produces a spike. A training loss then measures how far the network's output is from the desired output, while a gradient indicates how each adjustable parameter should change to reduce that loss. These ideas fit together smoothly until the learning calculation reaches the instant of spike generation. At that point, a continuously changing internal state becomes a discrete event, creating a break in the chain of gradients needed for learning.

Imagine a neuron whose internal state rises gradually from 0.7 to 0.8, then to 0.9, while its firing threshold is 1.0. Its output remains silent throughout those changes. When the state rises from 0.99 to 1.01, the output suddenly jumps from silence to a spike. A tiny change near the threshold can therefore cause a complete change in the neuron's output, while equally sized changes farther from the threshold can leave the output unchanged.

This behavior can be represented by a spike function. Let $u$ be the neuron's internal state, let $\theta$ be its firing threshold, and let $s$ be its output:
$$
S =
\begin{cases}
1, & u \geq \theta,\\
0, & u < \theta.
\end{cases}
$$
Here, $s=1$ means that the neuron emits a spike, and $s=0$ means that it remains silent. The formula lets us compute the neuron's event output from its state and threshold. It also reveals the difficulty for gradient-based learning: the output forms a step. Below the threshold it stays at zero, and at the threshold it jumps directly to one.

A gradient describes how a small change in one quantity produces a small change in another. For example, if increasing a connection weight slightly raises the loss, the gradient tells the optimizer to reduce that weight. Backpropagation performs this reasoning through many connected operations. It starts with the training loss and follows the chain of influence backward, asking how each operation affected the final error.

The spike function interrupts that chain. Away from the threshold, a small change in $u$ produces no change in $s$, so the ordinary derivative is zero. Exactly at the threshold, the output changes abruptly, so the ordinary derivative is undefined. Written schematically,
$$
\frac{\partial s}{\partial u} =
\begin{cases}
0, & u \neq \theta,\\
\text{undefined}, & u = \theta.
\end{cases}
$$
The symbol $\partial s/\partial u$ represents the sensitivity of the spike output $s$ to a change in the internal state $u$. A value of zero tells backpropagation that changing the state has no local effect on the spike. An undefined value provides no usable direction at the exact switching point. As a result, ordinary differentiation cannot carry a useful learning signal through spike generation.

Consider a connection weight $w$ that contributes to the internal state $u$, which then determines the spike $s$, which finally affects the loss $L$. Backpropagation needs the chain
$$
\frac{\partial L}{\partial w}
=
\frac{\partial L}{\partial s}
\frac{\partial s}{\partial u}
\frac{\partial u}{\partial w}
$$
Here, $L$ is the training loss, $w$ is the adjustable connection weight, $\partial L/\partial s$ measures how the spike affects the loss, and $\partial u/\partial w$ measures how the weight affects the neuron's state. The middle term, $\partial s/\partial u$, carries the learning signal across the firing decision. When that term is zero or undefined, the backward chain cannot provide a useful update for $w$.

This creates a credit-assignment problem. Credit assignment means deciding which earlier parameters contributed to a later success or error. Suppose a neuron remained just below threshold when producing a spike would have reduced the loss. The learning process needs to assign some responsibility to the weights that shaped the neuron's state. The exact spike derivative supplies zero below threshold, even at $u=0.999\theta$, so those weights receive no guidance through that route. The mathematical description of the event is accurate for forward computation, yet its derivative is unhelpful for backward learning.

**Question.** Why can the optimizer simply wait until the neuron reaches the threshold and use the gradient there?

**Answer.** The derivative at the threshold is undefined, and reaching one exact value offers no stable numerical direction for adjusting many parameters. Training needs useful sensitivity information across a neighborhood of states so that a neuron approaching the threshold can receive guidance before its output switches.

Surrogate-gradient learning supplies that guidance by separating the forward and backward roles of the spike function. During the forward run, the neuron keeps its discrete firing rule: it emits either a spike or silence. During the backward run, the learning algorithm substitutes a smooth, differentiable approximation for the spike derivative. Common choices include piecewise-linear, sigmoid-shaped, and exponential approximations.

A surrogate derivative usually gives its largest values near the threshold and smaller values farther away. This shape expresses a practical idea: a small change in a neuron close to firing can alter the event outcome, while the same change has less immediate influence when the state lies far from the threshold. The surrogate therefore creates a usable path for gradients without changing the discrete spikes used in the forward computation.

For example, suppose two neurons are silent. One has $u=0.99\theta$, while the other has $u=0.20\theta$. The exact derivative assigns zero to both. A surrogate derivative assigns a stronger learning signal to the first neuron because its state lies close to the firing boundary. The optimizer can then adjust the contributing weights so that future inputs move the neuron toward or away from firing, depending on which change reduces the loss.

**Question.** Does a surrogate derivative mean that the neuron emits partial spikes during training?

**Answer.** The neuron still emits discrete events in the forward run. The smooth approximation operates only during the backward calculation, where it estimates how sensitive the firing decision should be to nearby changes in the neuron's state.

The resulting mental model has two coordinated paths. Information moves forward through threshold-triggered spike events, preserving the discrete behavior that defines the network. Learning signals move backward through smooth surrogate derivatives, allowing the training loss to assign credit to the weights that shaped those events. The firing threshold creates the event, the event creates the differentiation obstacle, and the surrogate derivative bridges that obstacle so gradient-based learning can proceed.
