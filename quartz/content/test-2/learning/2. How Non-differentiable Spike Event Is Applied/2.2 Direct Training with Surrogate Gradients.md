## Direct Training with Surrogate Gradients

Once a spiking network produces discrete events and a training loss measures how far its output is from the desired result, learning requires one more link: the loss must guide each synaptic weight toward a better value. Imagine that a neuron's membrane potential sits just below its firing threshold. A tiny increase in an incoming weight may push the potential across the threshold and create a spike, while a slightly smaller increase may leave the output unchanged. The forward computation therefore contains an abrupt transition. Gradient-based training, however, depends on measuring how a small weight change affects the loss. Surrogate gradients provide a practical estimate of that sensitivity.

Consider a neuron with membrane potential $u$ and firing threshold $\vartheta$. Its spike output $s$ can be written as
$$
S = H(u-\vartheta)
$$
Where $H$ is a step function, $u$ is the neuron's membrane potential, $\vartheta$ is the firing threshold, and $s$ is the resulting spike value. When $u$ reaches or exceeds $\vartheta$, the neuron emits a spike; otherwise, it remains silent. This hard threshold preserves event-based behavior during the forward pass, which is the stage where the network processes an input and produces a prediction.

Training proceeds in the opposite direction. The loss $L$ measures prediction error, and the optimizer needs the gradient of that loss with respect to a synaptic weight $w$. The chain rule expresses the required gradient as
$$
\frac{\partial L}{\partial w}
=
\frac{\partial L}{\partial s}
\frac{\partial s}{\partial u}
\frac{\partial u}{\partial w}
$$
Where $L$ is the training loss, $w$ is a synaptic weight, $s$ is the neuron's spike output, and $u$ is its membrane potential. The factor $\partial L/\partial s$ describes how the spike output influences the loss. The factor $\partial u/\partial w$ describes how the weight influences the membrane potential. Between them lies $\partial s/\partial u$, the sensitivity of the spike to the membrane potential.

The hard threshold makes this middle factor unusable for ordinary gradient descent: its derivative is zero away from the threshold and undefined exactly at the threshold. A learning signal passed through that derivative would usually disappear before reaching earlier weights. Surrogate-gradient training keeps the hard spike in the forward pass while using the derivative of a smooth approximation during the backward pass.

If $\tilde{s}(u)$ is a smooth approximation to the spike function, the gradient calculation becomes
$$
\frac{\partial L}{\partial w}
\approx
\frac{\partial L}{\partial s}
\frac{\partial \tilde{s}}{\partial u}
\frac{\partial u}{\partial w}
$$
Where $\tilde{s}$ is the smooth surrogate and $\partial \tilde{s}/\partial u$ supplies a usable local slope. The approximation affects the learning signal rather than the spike emitted during forward computation. Common choices include piecewise-linear, sigmoid-shaped, and exponential surrogate functions. Each gives membrane potentials near the threshold a meaningful gradient, allowing the optimizer to estimate which weight changes would make useful spikes more likely and harmful spikes less likely.

A piecewise-linear surrogate offers a concrete picture. Suppose the threshold is $1.0$, and a neuron reaches a membrane potential of $0.98$. The forward pass records silence because the threshold has not been crossed. During the backward pass, the surrogate assigns a positive slope around the threshold. If the loss indicates that a spike would have improved the prediction, the weight update can raise the inputs that contributed to the membrane potential. On a later presentation, the potential may reach $1.01$, producing a real spike. The surrogate therefore turns "almost fired" into actionable training information.

The same mechanism works in the other direction. If a neuron fires at an unhelpful time, the gradient can reduce the weights that pushed its membrane potential through the threshold. Across many examples, these small updates shape both which neurons fire and when they fire. Timing can participate directly in the learned representation because the network is optimized through its sequence of internal spike events.

**Question.** If the backward pass uses a smooth function, does the trained network produce smooth-valued outputs instead of spikes?

**Answer.** The forward pass continues to use the hard threshold and produces discrete spike events. Smoothness appears only in the derivative used to estimate weight updates. After training, the network still computes with spikes because the surrogate serves as a training tool rather than a replacement for event-based activity.

Applying this approximation throughout a multilayer network creates direct SNN optimization. "Direct" means that the spiking network itself is trained end to end: an input drives spike dynamics, those dynamics produce an output, the output determines a loss, and surrogate gradients carry corrective signals back through the same network. The resulting weights are adapted to spiking behavior from the beginning. This avoids a separate conversion stage in which a continuously activated network is trained first and then translated into a spiking form.

End-to-end optimization also lets the training objective coordinate activity across layers and across time. A weight can influence the present membrane potential, alter whether a spike occurs, and thereby change later activity. Training follows these dependencies backward so that early synapses receive credit or blame for later output errors. Multilayer methods such as SuperSpike demonstrate how this approach can support competitive accuracy and generalization on spatiotemporal classification tasks, while directly trained networks can learn temporal structure in auditory signals.

The intended performance profile combines competitive predictive accuracy with low latency and relatively sparse activity. Reported surrogate-trained SNNs can approach conventional-network accuracy within roughly one to two percentage points, while convergence can become strong by around the twentieth epoch in the evaluated setting. Latency can reach approximately $10$ milliseconds. These values describe an observed operating profile rather than a universal guarantee: neuron dynamics, input encoding, surrogate shape, and training hyperparameters all influence the result.

Surrogate training also aims for a practical balance between rapid decisions and efficient event generation. One reported comparison places a surrogate-gradient SNN near $15$ millijoules per inference and about $12{,}000$ spikes, indicating lower activity and energy than a converted SNN in that evaluation. The optimization process can learn economical spiking patterns because the weights are adjusted within the spiking network itself. Ultra-sparse learning rules may consume even less energy, while surrogate gradients are suited to settings that prioritize low-latency supervised accuracy.

**Question.** Why can surrogate-gradient training still require careful tuning if it supplies usable gradients?

**Answer.** The surrogate determines where the backward pass assigns sensitivity and how large that sensitivity becomes. A very narrow surrogate may pass useful gradients through only a small range of membrane potentials, while a broad or steep surrogate may produce updates that poorly reflect threshold-crossing behavior. Learning rate, threshold dynamics, encoding, and the number of simulated time steps interact with that choice, so stable and efficient convergence depends on matching these elements.

A useful mental model is a hard event pathway paired with a smooth learning pathway. The hard pathway determines the spikes the network actually emits. The smooth pathway estimates how nearby changes in membrane potential would affect those events and the final loss. Repeating forward spikes, backward surrogate gradients, and weight updates allows the network to organize its own event timing and activity directly, targeting accurate predictions, fast convergence, low latency, and efficient spike use within one trainable system.
