### The Leaky Integrate-and-Fire Neuron

A stream of spike events becomes useful only when a neuron can combine those events into a decision. Suppose several upstream neurons send spikes during a short sequence of simulation timesteps. Each arriving spike carries a weighted input: a larger positive weight gives the receiving neuron a stronger push, while a smaller positive weight gives it a gentler push. The receiving neuron needs some memory of these pushes because a single event may be too weak to trigger a response. It also needs a way for old input to fade, so that events separated by a long quiet interval do not accumulate forever. The leaky integrate-and-fire neuron, usually shortened to **LIF neuron**, supplies both abilities with one changing internal quantity.

That quantity is the **membrane potential**, a number representing the neuron's current accumulated drive. Imagine it as the water level in a container. Incoming weighted spikes pour water into the container, raising the level. Between arrivals, a small drain lowers the level. A marked line near the top determines when the container produces an output event. This picture captures the three operations that shape the neuron's behavior: input is integrated, stored potential leaks away, and a spike is emitted when the potential reaches a firing threshold.

Consider a neuron beginning with a low membrane potential. During one simulation timestep, it receives a spike through a connection with a positive weight. The membrane potential rises by an amount determined by that weight. If another weighted spike arrives soon afterward, its contribution is added to the remaining potential. This accumulation is called **integration**. Integration gives the neuron a short-term memory: several modest events can combine into a response even when none of them is strong enough by itself.

For example, imagine that each of three nearby input spikes raises the membrane potential by a similar amount. The first spike creates a small rise. The second arrives while much of that rise remains, so the potential climbs higher. The third adds another push and may carry the potential to the firing level. The output spike therefore reflects a recent pattern of input, rather than merely copying each incoming event. Input timing matters because closely spaced events have more opportunity to accumulate.

The memory provided by the membrane potential gradually weakens through **leakage**. At each timestep, the stored potential decays toward its resting level. Returning to the container analogy, the drain remains open while water is being added. A rapid sequence of pours can raise the level despite the drain, while widely separated pours may disappear before they can combine. Leakage therefore creates a time window over which past inputs influence the present state.

Suppose two input spikes deliver equal weighted pushes. When they arrive in neighboring timesteps, the second builds on most of the potential left by the first. When a long interval separates them, leakage removes much of the earlier contribution, and the second spike begins from a lower level. Equal spike counts can consequently produce different outcomes. The temporal arrangement of those spikes determines how much accumulated drive survives.

The neuron needs a definite rule for turning this changing internal state into an output event. A **firing threshold** provides that rule. The threshold is a fixed level against which the membrane potential is compared. While the potential remains below it, the neuron stores and leaks its accumulated input. When incoming input pushes the potential to the threshold, the neuron emits a spike. That event can then become an input to neurons in the following layer or stage of processing.

The trajectory below places these operations in chronological order. Follow the membrane-potential curve from left to right. Input causes upward movement, while leakage pulls the curve downward or limits how quickly it rises. The horizontal threshold marks the firing condition. At the moment the curve reaches that level, the neuron emits a spike. The sharp change afterward represents a **reset**, which begins a fresh accumulation cycle.

The downward behavior in the graph has two different causes depending on when it occurs. A gradual decline below threshold comes from ordinary leakage: stored influence fades as time passes. The abrupt return immediately after threshold crossing comes from the reset rule: firing deliberately moves the membrane potential back to its starting level. Keeping these processes separate makes the trajectory easier to read. Leakage operates throughout the accumulation period, whereas reset follows spike emission and prepares the neuron for another cycle.

Imagine replaying the same input pattern while changing one condition at a time. Stronger input weights create larger upward changes, so threshold may be reached earlier. More closely spaced events leave less time for leakage, again favoring an earlier spike. Faster leakage removes accumulated potential more quickly and may prevent firing. A higher threshold demands more surviving input before emission. The output spike time is therefore determined jointly by input timing, input strength, leakage, and threshold.

**Question.** If several input spikes arrive but the neuron never fires, where did their influence go?

**Answer.** Each weighted spike raised the membrane potential when it arrived, but leakage reduced that stored potential between events. If the arrivals were too weak or too widely separated, their contributions never accumulated enough to reach the firing threshold. Their influence appeared temporarily in the membrane-potential trajectory and then faded toward the resting level.

A beginner may wonder whether reaching the threshold means the neuron sends out all the potential it has accumulated. A spike is a discrete output event announcing that the firing condition has been reached. The membrane potential is the internal state used to make that decision. After emission, the reset changes that state so later inputs are evaluated in a new accumulation cycle. The spike carries the occurrence and timing of the threshold crossing onward through the network.

**Question.** Why can the same number of input spikes produce an output spike in one trial and silence in another?

**Answer.** Spike count alone leaves out timing and weight. Closely spaced or strongly weighted inputs can raise the membrane potential faster than leakage removes it, allowing threshold crossing. Widely spaced or weakly weighted inputs may decay before they combine. The neuron responds to the evolving balance between incoming drive and leakage across time.

This mechanism also explains how sparse activity emerges. A LIF neuron emits an event only when accumulated input overcomes leakage and reaches threshold. Many timesteps may therefore pass with no output spike, especially when stimulation is weak. Silence records the fact that the firing condition has not been met, while each emitted spike marks a specific threshold-crossing time. The complete mental model is a repeating cycle: weighted events raise the membrane potential, leakage continuously weakens stored input, threshold crossing triggers spike emission, and reset prepares the neuron to integrate the events that arrive next.
