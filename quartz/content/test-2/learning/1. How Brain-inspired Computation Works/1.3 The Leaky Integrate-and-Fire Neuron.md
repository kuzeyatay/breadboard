## The Leaky Integrate-and-Fire Neuron

A spike event happens at a particular time, but a neuron needs a way to decide when that event should occur. Imagine that several incoming spikes arrive during a short interval. Each arrival carries a small influence, and the neuron must combine those influences across time. A single weak arrival may be insufficient to produce an output, while several closely spaced arrivals may collectively become strong enough. The leaky integrate-and-fire neuron turns this recent history of incoming activity into a clear decision: remain silent for now, or emit a spike at this moment.

The neuron keeps track of that history through a changing internal quantity called the **membrane potential**. You can picture the membrane potential as a running level that rises when the neuron receives input. The level summarizes how strongly recent activity has pushed the neuron toward firing. Incoming activity therefore has an effect that can persist beyond its exact arrival time, allowing multiple inputs to contribute to one later output event.

This accumulation is called **integration**. Suppose three input spikes arrive in rapid succession, and each one raises the membrane potential by a small amount. The first raises the level, the second adds to what remains from the first, and the third adds again. Their combined effect may become large even though each spike is individually weak. Integration gives the neuron a short-term memory of recent input: its present state depends on what has arrived over a stretch of time rather than only on what arrives at the current instant.

A useful analogy is a cup placed beneath a dripping tap. Each drop raises the water level slightly. Drops that arrive close together build upon one another, so the level can climb quickly. In the neuron, incoming spikes play the role of the drops, while the membrane potential plays the role of the water level. The analogy becomes more accurate when the cup has a small hole in its base, because the accumulated level also gradually falls.

That gradual loss is called **leakage**. When incoming activity pauses, the membrane potential decays over time. Earlier inputs therefore have less influence as they recede into the past. Leakage gives recent activity greater weight than distant activity and prevents weak, widely separated inputs from accumulating indefinitely. The state of the neuron becomes a fading record of its input history.

Consider two patterns containing the same number of incoming spikes. In one pattern, five spikes arrive close together. Their effects overlap before much potential leaks away, allowing the membrane potential to rise rapidly. In the other pattern, the same five spikes are spread across a longer interval. More potential disappears between arrivals, so the level may remain low. The total number of inputs is identical, yet their timing produces different outcomes. The leaky dynamics make the neuron sensitive to both how much activity arrives and when it arrives.

The neuron needs a boundary that converts this continuously changing internal level into a discrete output event. That boundary is the **firing threshold**. As integration raises the membrane potential and leakage pulls it downward, the neuron continually evolves toward or away from the threshold. When the potential reaches or crosses that level, the neuron emits a spike. The threshold therefore marks the point at which accumulated input becomes sufficient for an output event.

The sharp event marks a specific time, turning the preceding trajectory into a discrete signal. Immediately afterward, the membrane potential drops to its starting level through a **reset**, preparing the neuron to evaluate later input.

The reset separates one firing decision from the next. Without clearing the accumulated state after a spike, the potential could remain near the threshold and allow the previous input history to dominate later decisions. Resetting begins a fresh cycle of accumulation and leakage. New inputs can then build another trajectory, potentially producing another threshold crossing at a later time.

Imagine a neuron receiving a steady sequence of equally strong inputs. Each input raises the membrane potential, leakage removes part of that rise between arrivals, and the remaining potential gradually approaches the threshold. Stronger or more frequent input makes accumulation outpace leakage, so the threshold is reached sooner. Faster leakage removes more of each earlier contribution, delaying the spike or preventing a crossing during the observed interval. A lower threshold requires less accumulated influence and usually produces an earlier spike, while a higher threshold requires a longer or stronger input history.

These three influences-input strength, leakage, and threshold-jointly determine spike timing. Input pushes the membrane potential upward. Leakage pulls it back toward its resting level as time passes. The threshold sets the level required for spike emission. A spike emerges when the upward effect of sufficiently concentrated input overcomes the ongoing loss strongly enough for the trajectory to cross the boundary.

A common beginner question arises here because the output is a discrete event while the internal state changes gradually.

**Question.** If the neuron either spikes or stays silent, where is the information from weaker inputs stored?

**Answer.** Weaker inputs change the membrane potential even when they do not immediately cause a spike. Their effects accumulate with nearby inputs and fade through leakage. The resulting trajectory stores a short-lived record of recent activity. That record influences whether a later input causes a threshold crossing and exactly when the crossing occurs.

The timing of the emitted spike therefore reflects more than the final input that pushed the potential across the threshold. It reflects the whole recent sequence: how strong the inputs were, how closely they arrived, and how much accumulated potential leaked away between them. Two neurons can receive different input histories and still emit one spike each, yet emit those spikes at different times because their membrane trajectories reached threshold at different moments.

**Question.** Why can several closely spaced inputs cause a spike when the same inputs spread apart may produce silence?

**Answer.** Closely spaced inputs arrive before much membrane potential has leaked away, so their effects add together efficiently. Widely spaced inputs allow more decay between arrivals, leaving less accumulated potential for the next input to build upon. The clustered pattern can cross the firing threshold, while the spread-out pattern can remain below it.

The leaky integrate-and-fire neuron can be understood as a cycle driven by time. Incoming activity raises an internal level through integration; the level gradually falls through leakage; a fixed threshold identifies when the accumulated influence is sufficient; crossing that threshold emits a spike; and reset begins the cycle again. Through this sequence, membrane potential acts as the bridge between a continuous history of incoming activity and a precisely timed, discrete event.
