### Reading Training Accuracy Curves

Imagine choosing an SNN for a small edge device that must recognize events quickly while spending only a few millijoules per inference. Earlier energy and spike-count results tell you how expensive each model is once it runs: an ANN-style CNN sits around 200 mJ per inference, a converted SNN around 20 mJ with about 20,000 spikes, a surrogate-gradient SNN around 15 mJ with about 12,000 spikes, and an STDP-based SNN around 5 mJ with about 4,000 spikes. Those numbers describe the cost of using the model, but they do not yet tell you how the model reached its final behavior during training. A training accuracy curve fills that gap by showing how well a model classifies examples after each pass through the training data.

An epoch is one full pass through the training set. If a model trains for 20 epochs, it receives 20 repeated chances to adjust its internal parameters using the same overall collection of examples. Accuracy is the fraction of predictions that are correct, usually written as a percentage. A training accuracy curve places epoch number on the horizontal axis and accuracy on the vertical axis, so the shape of the curve becomes a record of learning over time. A curve that rises steeply early shows rapid improvement. A curve that flattens shows stabilization, meaning extra training is producing smaller gains. A curve that remains lower than another curve shows an accuracy gap, which is the distance between their achieved accuracies at the same epoch or at the end of training.

The reason this curve belongs beside energy and spike count is practical. A low-energy model that learns slowly or settles at a much lower accuracy may suit one application and fail another. A slightly more expensive model that reaches high accuracy quickly may be the better choice for real-time recognition. The curve turns a final score into a learning profile: how fast the method improves, how high it climbs, and whether it has mostly stabilized by the chosen training budget.

*Training accuracy learning curves over 20 epochs for surrogate-gradient, converted, and STDP-based SNNs* *(p. 11)*

The three curves show method-specific learning profiles. The surrogate-gradient SNN rises quickly and approaches a high final accuracy by about the 20th epoch. That shape supports the claim that surrogate-trained SNNs can come within roughly 1-2% of ANN accuracy while converging within this 20-epoch window. The visual evidence is the combination of a steep early climb and a high plateau: the model gains useful classification ability rapidly, then stabilizes near the top of the plotted range. In plain terms, the surrogate method learns spike patterns through a gradient-based training process that gives it a strong signal about how to improve, even though spikes themselves are discrete events.

A converted SNN follows a different path. It begins from an ANN that has already learned continuous-valued activations, then translates that behavior into spikes. Because the converted model must approximate activation levels through spike rates over a simulation window, it can reach competitive accuracy while using more spikes and often requiring longer temporal processing. On the accuracy curve, this kind of method can look strong because it inherits much of the ANN's learned structure, yet its cost profile differs sharply from the surrogate method. The earlier cost values make that visible: the converted SNN uses about 20 mJ per inference and about 20,000 spikes, while the surrogate-gradient SNN uses about 15 mJ and about 12,000 spikes. When two methods appear close in accuracy, the spike count and millijoule cost decide whether "close" is operationally meaningful.

STDP-based learning has its own curve shape because it learns through spike-timing relationships. STDP stands for Spike-Timing Dependent Plasticity. The plain idea is that the timing between incoming spikes and outgoing spikes changes connection strength. If a presynaptic spike helps trigger a postsynaptic response at the right time, that connection can strengthen; if the timing relationship is less useful, it can weaken. This local rule produces sparse activity and very low energy use, around 5 mJ per inference with about 4,000 spikes, but the accuracy curve tends to rise more slowly and settle lower than the surrogate-gradient curve. The curve therefore matches the tradeoff: STDP is attractive when ultra-low power or unsupervised adaptation is the priority, while its classification accuracy and convergence speed are less aggressive than the surrogate approach.

A beginner might wonder why the curve uses training accuracy rather than only final accuracy. Final accuracy is like seeing the finishing position in a race; the training curve shows the whole run. If one model reaches high accuracy by epoch 10 and barely changes afterward, it has converged quickly. If another model is still climbing at epoch 20, it may need a longer training budget or a different training rule. Convergence time is the amount of training needed before the curve mostly stabilizes. In this comparison, the 20-epoch window gives a common ruler for surrogate-gradient, converted, and STDP-based SNNs, so their learning behavior can be compared under the same training horizon.

**Question.** If the surrogate-gradient SNN and converted SNN both reach competitive accuracy, why favor the surrogate-gradient SNN for some edge applications?

**Answer.** The surrogate-gradient SNN combines high accuracy with lower latency and fewer spikes than the converted SNN. The reported comparison places the surrogate-gradient model at about 15 mJ per inference and about 12,000 spikes, while the converted SNN uses about 20 mJ and about 20,000 spikes. Since spikes are the events that drive computation in an SNN, fewer spikes usually mean less activity to process. The accuracy curve shows that the surrogate method also converges quickly by about the 20th epoch, so it offers a strong balance between learning speed, final accuracy, and operating cost.

The accuracy gap becomes easier to interpret once it is tied to the task goal. Suppose a gesture-recognition device must respond within a few milliseconds and run on a small battery. A method that saves large amounts of energy while staying close to ANN accuracy is valuable because every inference happens repeatedly in the field. The surrogate-gradient curve supports this role: it climbs quickly, reaches a high level, and pairs that learning behavior with low-latency operation around 10 ms and moderate spiking activity. Suppose instead the device is a tiny always-on sensor where saving power dominates precise classification. The STDP curve's lower final accuracy may be acceptable because the method uses the fewest spikes and the least energy.

The curve also prevents a common confusion: "more brain-like" does not automatically mean "most accurate." In this comparison, STDP has the sparsest spike activity and lowest energy, while surrogate-gradient training produces the stronger accuracy profile. The correct mental picture is a set of linked axes rather than a single ranking. Accuracy, latency, energy, spike count, and convergence each measure a different pressure on the model. The training accuracy curve covers the learning-pressure part: how rapidly each method turns training examples into correct classifications.

**Question.** What does it mean when a training accuracy curve flattens near epoch 20?

**Answer.** A flattening curve means the model's accuracy is changing only slightly from one epoch to the next. The model has mostly stabilized under the current training setup. For the surrogate-gradient SNN, flattening near a high accuracy by about the 20th epoch supports the idea that it converges quickly. For STDP-based learning, a flatter curve at a lower accuracy means the method has also settled, but around a lower classification level.

Reading these curves well means reading them together with the operating costs. The surrogate-gradient SNN's curve tells a story of fast improvement and high final accuracy, which explains why it is positioned as the balanced choice for low-latency, accuracy-sensitive SNN applications. The converted SNN remains competitive in accuracy but carries a heavier spike burden because rate-coded propagation needs more events and longer simulation windows. The STDP-based SNN learns more gradually and reaches a lower classification level, while its sparse spiking makes it the most energy-frugal option. The mental model is simple: the curve shows how the method learns across 20 epochs, and the millijoules-per-inference and spike-count numbers show what that learned behavior costs when the model is used.
