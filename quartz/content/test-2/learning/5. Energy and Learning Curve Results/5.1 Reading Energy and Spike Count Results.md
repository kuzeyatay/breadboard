## Reading Energy and Spike Count Results

Imagine a small camera at the edge of a road watching for movement. Most of the time, the scene changes only a little: a parked car stays parked, the background stays still, and only a pedestrian or bicycle creates new information. A conventional neural network still pushes dense numerical activations through many layers for each input. Energy is spent again and again on continuous-valued operations, even when much of the scene carries little new signal. A spiking neural network uses a different habit: quiet moments can remain quiet, and meaningful changes can be carried by discrete spike events.

That makes energy and spike count natural partners when reading SNN results. Total energy tells us how much power the model spends to make one decision. Total spikes tell us how many discrete events the model generates while making that decision. A model that produces fewer spikes usually gives the hardware fewer events to route, store, and process. The phrase **millijoules per inference** puts the energy number into a concrete unit: one inference means one forward decision by the model, and one millijoule is a small unit of energy. If a method uses 5 millijoules per inference, it spends 5 mJ to classify or respond to one sample.

The energy-spike relationship is useful because a spike is both a message and a cost. A spike carries information about activity at a particular time, but each spike also asks the system to do work. The system may need to move the event, update neuron states, and trigger downstream computation. Reading spike count without energy misses the actual cost of those events. Reading energy without spike count hides the reason the cost changes across models. The strongest interpretation comes from reading the two numbers together.

![SNN energy efficiency summary across ANN, converted SNN, surrogate gradient SNN, and STDP-based SNN](/test-2/assets/source-visuals/2510-27379v1-page-9-table-t1-snn-energy-efficiency-summary-across-ann-conver.png)

*SNN energy efficiency summary across ANN, converted SNN, surrogate gradient SNN, and STDP-based SNN* *(p. 9)*

The table gives a compact way to compare four model families. The ANN row sits at the high-energy end, around 200 mJ per inference. That large value fits the dense-computation picture: an ANN carries information through continuous activations rather than sparse event timing, so the model keeps doing numerical work across many units. The converted SNN row lowers energy sharply, to about 20 mJ per inference, because the computation has moved into a spiking form. Its spike count remains relatively high, around 20,000 spikes per inference, because converted models often approximate ANN activations through rates of spikes. A stronger activation becomes more spikes over a simulation window, so the method saves energy compared with the ANN while still sending many events.

Surrogate-gradient SNNs reduce both numbers further. Their reported energy is about 15 mJ per inference, with roughly 12,000 spikes. The training method directly shapes spiking behavior, so the model can learn patterns that use fewer events than a rate-heavy converted SNN while still keeping competitive performance. This is why the surrogate-gradient row belongs between converted SNNs and STDP-based SNNs in the efficiency story: it balances learned spike control with practical accuracy and convergence behavior.

STDP-based SNNs sit at the lowest-energy, lowest-spike end of the comparison. STDP means **Spike-Timing Dependent Plasticity**, a learning rule where the timing relationship between spikes helps adjust connections. When this kind of model learns sparse spike timing patterns, it can make decisions with far fewer events. The reported result is about 5 mJ per inference and roughly 4,000 spikes, making it the low-spike-count model in this comparison. That energy advantage comes with a tradeoff already seen in the broader model comparison: STDP prioritizes energy-efficient sparse activity over fast convergence and top classification accuracy.

A beginner might wonder whether the smallest spike count automatically means the best model. The correct reading is more careful: the smallest spike count means the model uses the fewest event messages during inference, and the energy number tells whether that sparsity actually turns into lower cost. Here, the two measurements point in the same direction for STDP-based SNNs. The spike count is low, and the energy per inference is also low. That agreement makes the energy advantage convincing.

**Question.** If converted SNNs already use spikes, why do they still produce so many spikes compared with STDP-based SNNs?

**Answer.** Converted SNNs often inherit the behavior of an ANN by representing continuous activation strength through spike rates. A stronger activation may need many spikes across time to approximate the original ANN value. STDP-based SNNs learn through spike timing itself, so they can settle into sparser event patterns. In the reported comparison, that difference appears as about 20,000 spikes for converted SNNs versus about 4,000 spikes for STDP-based SNNs.

The graph makes the coupled pattern easier to see because the energy and spike-count trends move together across the model families.

![Comparison of energy consumption and spike count per inference across neural network models](/test-2/assets/source-visuals/2510-27379v1-page-9-graph-g1-comparison-of-energy-consumption-and-spike-count.png)

*Comparison of energy consumption and spike count per inference across neural network models* *(p. 9)*

The left-to-right pattern shows a steep drop from ANN energy to SNN energy. The ANN is near 200 mJ per inference, while the converted SNN falls to around 20 mJ, the surrogate-gradient SNN to around 15 mJ, and the STDP-based SNN to around 5 mJ. The spike-count pattern follows the same general descent across the SNN methods: converted SNNs are around 20,000 spikes, surrogate-gradient SNNs around 12,000, and STDP-based SNNs around 4,000. The graph therefore supports a paired interpretation: lower event activity accompanies lower energy consumption in these reported results.

The size of the energy drop is large enough to change how the models are judged. Moving from about 200 mJ to 20 mJ is a tenfold reduction. Moving from about 200 mJ to 5 mJ is a fortyfold reduction. Another way to read the STDP number is that one ANN inference at 200 mJ costs about as much energy as forty STDP-based SNN inferences at 5 mJ each, using these reported values. That comparison turns an abstract efficiency claim into a practical budget: with the same energy allowance, the low-spike STDP model can make many more decisions.

Surrogate-gradient SNNs also deserve a careful reading. Their energy is higher than STDP-based SNNs, but still far below the ANN number. Their spike count is higher than STDP-based SNNs, but lower than converted SNNs. That middle position reflects their role as a balance point: they are trained to produce useful spiking patterns while preserving strong performance and faster stabilization during training. When an application needs both efficiency and reliable task performance, the 15 mJ and 12,000-spike result can be more attractive than judging by minimum energy alone.

STDP-based SNNs become most compelling when the application values ultra-low-power behavior. Their low spike count means fewer event transmissions per inference, and their 5 mJ energy number shows that this sparseness is reflected in the reported cost. The slower convergence tradeoff means the model may take longer to settle during training, and its classification accuracy may be slightly reduced compared with accuracy-focused alternatives. For energy-constrained settings, that tradeoff can be reasonable because the operating cost per decision is so small.

**Question.** Why should energy be reported per inference instead of only as one total energy number?

**Answer.** Millijoules per inference makes models comparable at the level of one decision. A total energy number can depend on how many samples were processed, how long the model ran, or how the evaluation was arranged. Energy per inference answers a cleaner question: how much energy does this model spend each time it produces one result? That lets the ANN value of about 200 mJ, the converted SNN value of about 20 mJ, the surrogate-gradient value of about 15 mJ, and the STDP-based value of about 5 mJ be read on the same scale.

The mental model is a chain from representation to activity to cost. Dense continuous activations keep many computations active, so the ANN lands at high energy. Rate-coded conversion uses spikes but still sends many of them, so energy drops while spike count remains high. Surrogate-gradient training shapes spike patterns more directly, reducing both event count and energy while keeping a strong balance with performance. STDP-based learning pushes the chain furthest toward sparse timing-based activity, producing the fewest reported spikes and the lowest reported millijoules per inference. Energy and spike count together show why low-power SNN behavior is more than a label: it is visible in the number of events the model emits and in the energy spent to turn those events into a decision.
