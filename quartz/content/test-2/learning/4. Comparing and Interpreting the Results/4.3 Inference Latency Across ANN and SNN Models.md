## Inference Latency Across ANN and SNN Models

Once simulation windows and the three SNN training paradigms are familiar, latency becomes a practical question: how long does a trained model take to produce one inference? Imagine a gesture-controlled device waiting to recognize a hand movement. A delay of a few milliseconds feels immediate, while a longer delay can make the device feel sluggish. Inference latency measures that waiting time, usually from the arrival of an input until the model produces its prediction. Lower latency supports applications that must react quickly, such as interactive sensing, event-driven control, and real-time recognition.

An ANN typically processes an input through a fixed sequence of layers. Its latency therefore depends heavily on model complexity and the hardware executing those layers. CNN-based ANNs in the reported event-data benchmarks require roughly 20-50 milliseconds per sample. The range is wide because a larger network or a less suitable processor takes longer to complete the same general kind of computation.

An SNN adds a temporal dimension to inference. Input events arrive as spikes, neurons update over time, and the model observes activity across a simulation window before deciding. Each additional simulation step extends the opportunity to collect useful evidence, while also adding computation. This added work is the **temporal inference cost**: the latency associated with evolving and observing the network through time. A short window can support a rapid prediction when informative spikes arrive early. A longer window gives spike activity more time to represent the input, but delays the final answer.

Read the table by comparing each model type with its reported inference latency rather than treating "ANN" and "SNN" as two uniform categories. The ANN CNN occupies the higher-latency region, while the SNN results spread across different values according to how each model was trained and how long it must simulate spike activity. The surrogate-gradient SNN reaches about 10 milliseconds, the STDP-based SNN stabilizes below 15 milliseconds under optimized conditions, and the converted SNN is around 20 milliseconds. These values place surrogate-gradient training at the low-latency end of this comparison.

Consider 100 consecutive samples processed one at a time. At about 10 milliseconds per sample, a surrogate-gradient SNN would spend roughly one second on inference across all 100 samples. At about 20 milliseconds per sample, a converted SNN would spend roughly two seconds under the same simple interpretation. This multiplication gives an intuitive sense of how a small per-sample difference can accumulate in a continuous workload. Actual throughput can also depend on whether a system processes samples in parallel, so per-sample latency should remain the focus when judging response time.

The surrogate-gradient SNN achieves its low reported latency because its spike dynamics are trained directly toward the task. During learning, surrogate gradients provide a usable training signal around spike events, allowing the network to develop temporally effective responses. Informative spike patterns can therefore emerge within a relatively short period. A prediction around 10 milliseconds indicates that the model gathers enough evidence quickly, making this paradigm attractive when fast reactions and competitive predictive performance must coexist.

The STDP-based SNN follows a different temporal pattern. Spike-Timing Dependent Plasticity adjusts connections through the relative timing of spikes, and its early inference behavior can involve gradual spike adaptation. That behavior makes initial responses slightly slower than those of the surrogate-gradient model. Under optimized conditions, however, its latency stabilizes below 15 milliseconds. The interpretation is application-dependent: a sub-15-millisecond response can still satisfy many low-latency requirements even when another SNN reaches approximately 10 milliseconds.

ANN-to-SNN conversion produces another latency profile. A converted network begins with learned ANN activations and represents those activations through spike activity. The network may need a longer simulation window so that accumulated spikes approximate the original activation values reliably. Its latency can consequently rise to around 20 milliseconds. The conversion process can preserve useful behavior from an ANN, while the required temporal observation creates a larger temporal inference cost than in the directly trained surrogate-gradient case.

**Question.** Does spike-based computation guarantee that an SNN will have lower inference latency than an ANN?

**Answer.** Latency depends on how the SNN was trained, how spikes encode information, how long the simulation runs, and which hardware executes the model. A directly trained surrogate-gradient SNN can respond in about 10 milliseconds in the reported comparison, while a converted SNN may approach 20 milliseconds because it needs more simulation time. ANN latency also varies with architecture and hardware, reaching roughly 20-50 milliseconds in the cited benchmark setting. Model type alone therefore provides too little information; the simulation duration and deployment platform complete the comparison.

Low-latency deployment begins by setting a response-time requirement. If an interactive system needs an answer within roughly 15 milliseconds, the surrogate-gradient result fits comfortably and the optimized STDP-based result may also fit. A converted SNN near 20 milliseconds would require a looser deadline or a reduction in its required simulation duration. For a task that tolerates tens of milliseconds, all four approaches may remain plausible, allowing other performance requirements to guide the choice.

**Question.** Why can extending an SNN's simulation window improve its representation while worsening latency?

**Answer.** Each simulation step gives neurons another opportunity to receive spikes, update their internal state, and contribute evidence to the prediction. More steps can make a spike-based estimate more stable, especially when spike counts approximate continuous ANN activations. Those steps also take time to execute, so the prediction arrives later. The window length therefore controls a practical exchange between the amount of temporal evidence collected and the speed of the response.

A useful mental model is a timed evidence-gathering process. The ANN completes a fixed computational path, while the SNN listens to evolving spike activity until its simulation window provides enough evidence for a decision. Surrogate-gradient training produces useful evidence quickly, STDP-based learning reaches a slightly slower but still responsive regime, and ANN-to-SNN conversion often waits longer for spike counts to represent inherited activations. Latency comparisons become meaningful when each reported time is connected to that underlying temporal process and then judged against the response deadline of the intended deployment.
