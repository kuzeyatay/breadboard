## Accuracy and Energy Across MNIST and CIFAR-10

Suppose you must choose a neural network for a small device with a limited energy budget. You already know how to read classification accuracy and normalized energy consumption, and you have seen how ANN-to-SNN conversion, surrogate-gradient training, and STDP produce different kinds of spiking networks. The decision now requires reading those measures together. A model that recognizes the most images may consume far more energy per inference, while a model with extremely sparse activity may give up some accuracy. The useful choice depends on how much predictive performance the application needs and how much energy it can afford.

MNIST provides the gentler part of this comparison. Its images are small handwritten digits with relatively simple visual structure, so all four model categories can reach strong accuracy. The conventional artificial neural network, or ANN, leads in raw predictive performance. The converted SNN begins with an ANN and translates its continuous activations into spike activity, allowing it to retain much of the original model's accuracy. The directly trained SNN uses surrogate gradients to learn useful spiking patterns during training and approaches ANN accuracy closely, generally within about one to two percentage points. The STDP-based SNN learns through changes driven by spike timing and reaches lower accuracy than the other categories, while operating with especially sparse activity.

These MNIST results reveal how an easier dataset can compress the visible accuracy differences between methods. When several models classify most digits correctly, a small accuracy gap may be acceptable if it accompanies a large reduction in energy. Imagine that two models process 100 handwritten digits: one classifies only one or two additional digits correctly but consumes roughly an order of magnitude more energy. The extra correct predictions still have value, yet their value must be judged against the device's battery, heat, and operating constraints.

CIFAR-10 puts greater pressure on the models. Its color images contain more complex objects and backgrounds, so scaling from MNIST-level recognition to CIFAR-10-level recognition widens the accuracy gaps. The ANN reaches about 92% accuracy on CIFAR-10, while the direct SNN falls to roughly 85%. Converted and surrogate-trained SNNs occupy the region between the accuracy-focused ANN and the energy-focused STDP model. The widening spread means that the training method and representation have greater consequences as the visual task becomes harder.

Read each model across the graph before comparing models within one bar group. The ANN's profile combines the strongest accuracy with the highest normalized energy consumption. The converted SNN preserves competitive accuracy while sharply reducing energy, although its rate-coded spike propagation still produces relatively dense activity. The direct, surrogate-gradient SNN moves farther toward efficiency while keeping accuracy near the ANN on MNIST and remaining competitive on CIFAR-10. The STDP-based SNN reaches the lowest energy level and accepts the largest accuracy reduction.

Normalized energy consumption expresses each model's energy relative to a reference level, with the ANN serving as the high-consumption baseline in this comparison. The measure supports proportional reasoning: a model operating below 10% of ANN energy uses less than one-tenth as much energy under the comparison conditions. Surrogate-trained SNNs reach this region as task scale increases, and STDP-based SNNs push energy lower still. Measurements reported per inference give the same ordering in physical units: the ANN consumes approximately 200 mJ, the converted SNN about 20 mJ, the surrogate-gradient SNN about 15 mJ, and the STDP-based SNN about 5 mJ. Thus, conversion reduces energy by roughly a factor of ten, surrogate training reduces it somewhat further, and STDP reaches roughly one-fortieth of the ANN level.

Spike count helps explain this energy ordering. The converted SNN generates about 20,000 spikes per inference because rate coding represents activation strength through repeated events. The surrogate-gradient SNN generates around 12,000 spikes because training can shape more efficient temporal patterns. The STDP-based SNN produces roughly 4,000 spikes, giving it the sparsest activity and lowest reported energy. Energy therefore follows an understandable computational pattern: fewer spike events generally require fewer event-driven operations.

**Question.** Why can the model ranking change when we move from MNIST to CIFAR-10?

**Answer.** CIFAR-10 demands recognition of more complex visual patterns, so differences in representation and training become more visible. Accuracy values that cluster closely on MNIST spread farther apart on CIFAR-10. The ANN retains the strongest raw accuracy, while direct and STDP-based spiking approaches experience larger reductions. Energy savings remain substantial, so the harder dataset changes the accuracy side of the decision more than the efficiency ordering.

**Question.** Which model should be chosen when accuracy and energy are considered together?

**Answer.** The choice follows the operating requirement. An accuracy-dominant setting favors the ANN. A setting seeking competitive accuracy with sharply lower energy favors the surrogate-gradient SNN, which stays close to ANN accuracy while using a small fraction of its energy. Conversion offers another accuracy-preserving route with greater spike activity and energy use than direct surrogate training. A severe energy constraint favors the STDP-based SNN because its sparse spikes produce the lowest energy consumption, accompanied by reduced accuracy.

The complete mental model is a set of profiles rather than a podium. MNIST shows how spiking methods can preserve strong performance on a simpler task, while CIFAR-10 exposes the accuracy cost more clearly as task complexity grows. Normalized energy then supplies the second axis needed to interpret that cost. ANN, converted SNN, surrogate-gradient SNN, and STDP-based SNN occupy different points along an accuracy-energy tradeoff, so model selection comes from matching the whole profile to the intended constraint.
