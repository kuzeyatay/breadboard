## Energy per Inference and Spike Count

Once energy per inference and spike count are understood separately, they can be read together to reveal how a model's activity pattern shapes its power profile. Imagine a battery-powered sensor classifying one input at a time. Each completed classification is an **inference**, and the energy required to complete it is the model's **energy per inference**, measured here in millijoules (mJ). A spiking neural network also produces a countable stream of discrete events during that inference. The total number of those events is its **spike count**. Comparing both measurements helps answer a practical question: how much activity does the model generate, and how much energy accompanies that activity?

A spike can trigger communication and computation as it travels through a network. A model that generates many spikes therefore creates many opportunities for energy-consuming operations. A sparse model sends fewer events, leaving more of the system inactive for more of the inference. This creates an **energy-spike relationship**: among the reported spiking models, lower spike counts accompany lower energy consumption. The relationship is a measured trend across complete models rather than a fixed price assigned to each individual spike. Different spikes can involve different pathways, layers, memory accesses, or hardware operations, so dividing total energy by total spikes would oversimplify the comparison.

*Comparison of energy consumption and spike count per inference across ANN and SNN models* *(p. 9)*

The conventional ANN/CNN sits at the high-energy end, consuming approximately 200 mJ for each inference. Its computation uses continuous-valued activations rather than a stream of spikes, so its energy bar belongs in the comparison while spike count serves as a spiking-model activity measure. The three SNN approaches occupy a much lower energy range. Within that group, the converted SNN has the greatest spike activity and energy use, the surrogate-gradient SNN has intermediate values, and the STDP-based SNN has the sparsest activity and lowest energy use. The descending movement in both measurements gives the graph its main meaning: models with lighter spiking activity show a lower-power profile.

The converted SNN uses about 20 mJ per inference while producing approximately 20,000 spikes. Conversion begins with a conventionally trained network and represents its activations through spike-based processing. In the reported model, rate coding carries information through repeated spikes over time. A stronger represented activation generally requires more spike events, so activity accumulates across the network. The result is a major energy reduction relative to the ANN/CNN, paired with the highest spike count among the three SNNs. Conversion therefore demonstrates that adopting spikes can lower energy while still leaving substantial **activity cost**, meaning the energy associated with generating, transmitting, and processing a large amount of event activity.

The surrogate-gradient SNN lowers both measurements further, using about 15 mJ per inference and generating approximately 12,000 spikes. Surrogate-gradient training adapts the spiking network's behavior directly, allowing it to learn useful spiking patterns rather than inheriting a rate-coded representation through conversion. In concrete terms, it completes an inference with about 8,000 fewer spikes than the converted SNN, alongside a 5 mJ reduction in energy. Those paired changes support an interpretation based on efficiency: the learned activity pattern performs the task with fewer events, and the model consumes less energy per inference.

The STDP-based SNN reaches the lowest reported values: about 5 mJ per inference and approximately 4,000 spikes. Spike-Timing Dependent Plasticity, or STDP, adjusts connections using the relative timing of spikes. Its reported activation pattern is highly sparse, so comparatively few events propagate during an inference. Relative to the converted SNN, the STDP model uses about one quarter of the energy and generates about one fifth as many spikes. This is the clearest example of a **low-power model profile** in the comparison: sparse event activity and low energy appear together. The accompanying performance tradeoff remains part of model selection, since the STDP-based model has slightly reduced accuracy while offering especially strong energy efficiency.

Read each row horizontally rather than ranking one column in isolation. The model column identifies the architecture or training approach. The energy-per-inference column reports the energy needed for one classification. The spike-count column reports how many discrete events the spiking model generates during that classification. Converted SNN pairs 20 mJ with roughly 20,000 spikes; surrogate-gradient SNN pairs 15 mJ with roughly 12,000 spikes; and STDP-based SNN pairs 5 mJ with roughly 4,000 spikes. Moving through these rows, energy decreases as spike activity becomes sparser. The ANN/CNN row provides the continuous-computation reference at approximately 200 mJ per inference, showing the much larger energy demand against which the SNN profiles are evaluated.

**Question.** Does the STDP-based SNN use less energy simply because every spike has the same energy cost and it generates fewer spikes?

**Answer.** The results support a model-level association between sparse activity and low energy. They do not establish one universal energy value for an individual spike. Total energy can include event processing, communication, memory access, and other operations that vary with the model and computing system. The careful interpretation is that the reported STDP model combines the lowest spike count with the lowest measured energy per inference.

**Question.** Why compare spike count and energy together instead of choosing the model with the smallest energy value immediately?

**Answer.** Energy gives the final resource cost, while spike count helps explain the activity pattern behind that cost. The converted SNN's roughly 20,000 spikes and 20 mJ reveal a relatively active rate-coded model. The surrogate-gradient model reduces both values, and the STDP model reduces them further. Reading the two measurements together shows how sparse event-driven processing contributes to the observed low-power behavior and helps distinguish models that all consume far less energy than the ANN/CNN reference.

A useful mental model is to picture each inference as a journey through the network. Energy per inference records the total fuel used, while spike count records how much event traffic moved along the way. The converted SNN carries heavy spike traffic and uses the most energy among the spiking models. Surrogate-gradient training produces a leaner traffic pattern with a smaller energy bill. STDP produces the quietest journey, with about 4,000 spikes and about 5 mJ per inference. Across these results, sparse spiking activity and low energy form a consistent model profile, making spike count a practical clue for interpreting where an SNN's energy advantage comes from.
