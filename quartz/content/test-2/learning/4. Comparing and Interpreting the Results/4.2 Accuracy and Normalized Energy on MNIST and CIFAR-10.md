A model that classifies handwritten digits well can still struggle when the images contain colored objects, varied backgrounds, and greater visual diversity. MNIST and CIFAR-10 therefore provide two different tests of the same design choice. MNIST contains relatively simple grayscale digit images, while CIFAR-10 contains small color images drawn from ten object categories. Reading both benchmarks together reveals whether a model's accuracy-energy balance survives as the recognition task becomes more demanding.

The comparison includes four model types. A conventional convolutional artificial neural network, or ANN, processes continuous-valued activations throughout the network. A converted spiking neural network begins as a trained ANN and translates its activations into spike rates. A direct surrogate-gradient SNN learns its spiking behavior during training by using a gradient approximation that makes discrete spikes trainable with gradient-based methods. An STDP-based SNN learns through spike-timing-dependent plasticity, where changes in connection strength depend on the relative timing of spikes.

Each model produces an **accuracy-energy profile**: a pairing of how reliably it classifies images with how much energy it consumes during inference. Accuracy alone favors the model that predicts the most labels correctly. Energy alone favors the model that performs each prediction using the least energy. A practical comparison reads the two quantities together because different applications value them differently. A battery-powered sensor may accept a modest loss in accuracy to extend operating life, while a high-stakes recognition system may spend more energy to preserve every available percentage point of accuracy.

Begin with the two accuracy columns. The ANN occupies the strongest raw-accuracy position, and the surrogate-gradient SNN remains close to it. Across the reported results, surrogate-gradient training brings SNN accuracy to within roughly one or two percentage points of ANN accuracy. Conversion also preserves competitive performance, although converting continuous activations into spike rates carries costs that become clearer in the energy comparison. The STDP-based SNN gives up more accuracy than the other model types.

Now compare the datasets vertically rather than reading one model row in isolation. MNIST presents the easier recognition problem, so all four approaches can form a stronger accuracy profile there. CIFAR-10 places greater demands on learned visual features, and the reported accuracies reflect that difficulty. The relative model pattern remains informative: the ANN provides the highest accuracy, direct surrogate-gradient training stays close, conversion remains competitive, and STDP occupies the lower-accuracy position. Seeing this pattern on both benchmarks gives stronger evidence than seeing it on MNIST alone. A model can appear highly capable when digits are clean and centered; CIFAR-10 tests whether that capability extends to varied objects and backgrounds.

The energy column uses **normalized energy consumption**, which expresses each model's energy relative to a common reference scale. Normalization makes ratios and relative savings easier to compare even when the raw measurement unit is omitted from the table. A smaller normalized value means that the model consumes a smaller fraction of the reference energy for an inference. The column should therefore be read as a relative ordering and magnitude comparison: the ANN is the energy-intensive baseline, converted SNNs reduce consumption substantially, surrogate-gradient SNNs reduce it further, and STDP-based SNNs reach the lowest level.

A concrete interpretation helps. Suppose two models have nearly the same CIFAR-10 accuracy, while one has a much smaller normalized-energy entry. The lower-energy model offers a stronger profile for an energy-constrained device because it buys almost the same classification performance with fewer computational resources. If another model uses even less energy but loses a larger amount of accuracy, choosing it depends on how costly classification errors are in the intended application.

The ANN sits at the high-accuracy, high-energy end of the comparison, consuming about 200 mJ per inference. The converted SNN lowers consumption to about 20 mJ, a large saving, while its rate-coded activity still generates roughly 20,000 spikes per inference. Direct surrogate-gradient training improves the joint profile: energy falls to about 15 mJ and activity falls to roughly 12,000 spikes, while accuracy remains close to the ANN. The STDP-based model reaches about 5 mJ and roughly 4,000 spikes, giving it the sparsest and lowest-energy profile, together with a more noticeable accuracy reduction.

The shape of this progression explains why surrogate-gradient SNNs often occupy the balanced middle. Moving from the ANN to the surrogate-gradient SNN produces a large energy reduction while preserving most of the accuracy. Moving onward to the STDP-based SNN saves additional energy, though the accuracy cost grows. Across the spiking approaches, the reported energy use can be about 90-97% lower than the ANN baseline. The best model therefore depends on where an application places its acceptable operating point along the accuracy-energy profile.

**Question.** Why can the converted SNN consume more energy than the directly trained surrogate-gradient SNN even when both use spikes?

**Answer.** Conversion represents previously continuous ANN activations through spike rates. Stronger activations may require many spikes over a simulation window, producing relatively dense activity. Direct surrogate-gradient training shapes the spiking patterns themselves, allowing the network to learn a sparser pattern that reaches competitive accuracy with fewer events and lower energy.

**Question.** Does the lowest normalized-energy value identify the best model?

**Answer.** It identifies the most energy-efficient model in this comparison. Model selection also includes the corresponding MNIST and CIFAR-10 accuracies. STDP provides the lowest-energy profile, surrogate-gradient training provides a stronger balance between accuracy and energy, and the ANN provides the strongest raw accuracy at the highest energy cost.

A useful mental model is to imagine each architecture as a point on a map whose horizontal direction represents energy and whose vertical direction represents accuracy. MNIST shows where those points fall on a simpler task, while CIFAR-10 tests how they move under greater visual complexity. The ANN remains near the accuracy-focused edge, STDP moves farthest toward minimal energy, conversion carries ANN performance into a substantially cheaper spiking form, and surrogate-gradient training occupies the region where large energy savings coexist with accuracy close to the ANN.
