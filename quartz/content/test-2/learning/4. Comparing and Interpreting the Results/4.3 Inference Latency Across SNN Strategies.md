## Inference Latency Across SNN Strategies

Once inference latency is understood as the waiting time between receiving an input and producing a decision, the practical question becomes sharper: which learning strategy reaches that decision soonest? Imagine a small event-driven system that receives a rapidly changing stream. Each input begins a short race. The model must accumulate enough evidence, settle on an output, and make that output available before the information loses its value. A difference of several milliseconds changes how quickly the surrounding system can react.

The comparison includes four model categories: a conventional convolutional artificial neural network (ANN CNN), an ANN converted into a spiking neural network, an SNN trained directly with surrogate gradients, and an SNN learned through spike-timing-dependent plasticity (STDP). Their **inference latency** is measured in milliseconds per sample. A lower value means that the model completes its decision sooner, so the ranking from lowest to highest latency also gives the ranking from fastest to slowest response.

The exact values provide the clearest starting point:

![Latency comparison of ANN, converted SNN, surrogate-gradient SNN, and STDP-based SNN models](/test-2/assets/source-visuals/2510-27379v1-page-8-table-t1-latency-comparison-of-ann-converted-snn-surrog.png)

*Latency comparison of ANN, converted SNN, surrogate-gradient SNN, and STDP-based SNN models* *(p. 8)*

The surrogate-gradient SNN has the lowest latency, at about **10 ms per inference**. The STDP-based SNN follows at about **15 ms**, the converted SNN takes about **20 ms**, and the ANN CNN takes about **30 ms**. Read as decision times, these values mean that the surrogate-gradient model produces an output after roughly one hundredth of a second, while the ANN requires roughly three hundredths of a second. For one isolated input, both intervals may sound brief. Across a continuous sequence of inputs, however, the shorter interval allows decisions to become available earlier and gives the system more time for any action that follows.

A simple latency ratio makes the comparison easier to interpret. If model A takes $L_A$ milliseconds and model B takes $L_B$ milliseconds, then
$$
R=\frac{L_A}{L_B}
$$
Where $L_A$ is the latency of model A, $L_B$ is the latency of model B, and $R$ is the ratio of their decision times. This ratio tells us how many times as long model A waits before producing a result. Comparing the ANN CNN with the surrogate-gradient SNN gives
$$
R=\frac{30\text{ ms}}{10\text{ ms}}=3
$$
In this comparison, the ANN's decision time is three times the surrogate-gradient SNN's decision time. Equivalently, the surrogate-gradient SNN returns its decision in one-third of the time required by the ANN. This interpretation concerns response time for the compared models; it does not by itself determine every aspect of overall model quality.

The bar chart makes the ranking visible as a sequence of decision windows:

![Bar chart comparing inference latency across ANN and SNN models](/test-2/assets/source-visuals/2510-27379v1-page-8-graph-g1-bar-chart-comparing-inference-latency-across-ann.png)

*Bar chart comparing inference latency across ANN and SNN models* *(p. 8)*

The ANN CNN has the tallest bar, representing the longest wait. The converted SNN shortens that bar to around 20 ms, while the STDP-based SNN reaches around 15 ms. The surrogate-gradient SNN has the shortest bar at around 10 ms. The overall shape therefore shows that all three spiking strategies produce decisions sooner than the compared ANN, with direct surrogate-gradient training giving the strongest latency result.

The differences arise from how each strategy develops and uses its temporal behavior. A converted SNN begins with a trained ANN and represents its continuous activations through spike activity. Reliable approximation can require observing spikes across a longer **simulation window**, meaning a longer span of simulated time steps. The model must wait long enough for the accumulated spike pattern to represent the original ANN activation adequately. That waiting period contributes to the converted SNN's roughly 20 ms latency.

This added waiting is called **simulation-time cost**: the decision depends on how many simulated time steps must pass before the output becomes dependable. Suppose one converted model needs evidence from twenty time steps while a directly trained model reaches a stable output after ten comparable steps. Even if each step is processed efficiently, the converted model carries a longer temporal path to its decision. Its 20 ms result still improves on the ANN's 30 ms result here, yet its longer simulation window places it behind the directly trained spiking approaches.

Surrogate-gradient training shapes the SNN's spike-based computation directly toward the task objective. In the comparison, that process produces useful output activity quickly enough to support a decision at about 10 ms. The result is a strong fit for **latency-sensitive inference**, where the usefulness of a prediction depends partly on how soon it arrives. Latency-sensitive inference treats decision time as an operational constraint: the model has a limited interval in which to process an input and return a usable result.

The STDP-based SNN reaches about 15 ms. Its learning is driven by the relative timing of spikes, and its early inference behavior can involve gradual spike adaptation before the response stabilizes. Once optimized, it remains below 15 ms in the reported conditions. Its position between surrogate-gradient and converted SNNs shows that spiking behavior alone does not fix a single SNN latency. The learning strategy and the time needed for activity to stabilize shape the final response speed.

**Question.** Why can a converted SNN be slower than a surrogate-gradient SNN even though both use spikes?

**Answer.** The converted SNN may need a longer simulation window so that spike rates approximate the continuous activations of its original ANN. The surrogate-gradient SNN learns its spiking dynamics directly and can form a usable output with a shorter temporal sequence. In this comparison, those different paths produce latencies of about 20 ms and 10 ms, respectively.

A beginner may wonder whether 10 ms means that every part of a larger system responds within 10 ms. Here, the number describes model inference per sample under the comparison conditions. Input preparation, communication, and actions performed after the prediction can add further delay. The model's inference latency therefore occupies one segment of the complete response path, while still setting a lower bound on how soon the model's own decision can become available.

**Question.** If an input arrives every 12 ms, which compared models can finish one inference within that interval?

**Answer.** The surrogate-gradient SNN, at about 10 ms, fits within a 12 ms interval. The STDP-based SNN at 15 ms, converted SNN at 20 ms, and ANN CNN at 30 ms each require more than 12 ms for one decision under the compared values. This worked interpretation shows how a latency limit can turn a bar-chart difference into an immediate deployment constraint.

The complete mental model is a timed accumulation of evidence. An input starts the clock; the network processes activity until its output is ready; the elapsed interval is the inference latency. Converted SNNs may spend extra simulated time gathering a rate-based approximation, STDP-based SNNs may wait for temporally learned activity to stabilize, and surrogate-gradient SNNs can learn spike patterns that support earlier decisions. In the presented ranking, the surrogate-gradient SNN responds fastest, followed by the STDP-based SNN, the converted SNN, and the ANN CNN. For latency-sensitive use, the shorter bars represent more than small numerical improvements: they represent decisions becoming available sooner within a limited response window.
