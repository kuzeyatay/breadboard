## Energy Efficiency and Convergence Time

Once classification accuracy tells us how often a model predicts correctly, a practical question follows: how much energy does the model spend to achieve that performance? Imagine two models processing the same set of inputs. One reaches slightly higher accuracy but consumes three times as much energy per inference. The better choice depends on whether the application values every additional correct prediction enough to justify the added energy. Accuracy and energy therefore become more useful when they are combined into a single efficiency measure.

Begin with one inference, meaning one pass from an input to a predicted class. Let the model's classification accuracy be written as a fraction between 0 and 1, and let its estimated energy consumption per inference be measured in joules. Dividing the useful predictive performance by the required energy gives the **accuracy per joule**:
$$
\eta_E=\frac{A}{E}
$$
Here, $A$ is the classification accuracy expressed as a fraction, $E$ is the energy consumed per inference in joules, and $\eta_E$ is the normalized energy efficiency. This ratio computes how much correct-prediction performance the model provides for each joule it consumes. A larger value means that more accuracy is obtained from the same energy budget.

The word *normalized* indicates that performance has been scaled by the energy used to produce it. Accuracy alone describes predictive quality, while energy alone describes resource consumption. Their ratio places both quantities on a shared basis: one joule. That common basis allows models with different energy demands to be compared directly.

The units require careful interpretation. Since $A$ is a fraction, $\eta_E$ has units of inverse joules, $\mathrm{J}^{-1}$, with the accuracy fraction understood as the useful output. If accuracy is entered as a percentage instead, such as $97.8$ rather than $0.978$, the numerical result becomes percentage points per joule. Either convention can work, provided every model uses the same convention. Using fractions keeps the metric naturally tied to the range from 0 to 1.

Consider a model with an accuracy of $97.8\%$ and an energy cost of $15\ \mathrm{mJ}$ per inference. Convert the quantities before using the formula:
$$
A=0.978
$$
And
$$
E=15\ \mathrm{mJ}=0.015\ \mathrm{J}
$$
The normalized energy efficiency is then
$$
\eta_E=\frac{0.978}{0.015}=65.2\ \mathrm{J}^{-1}
$$
Now consider a model with $95.5\%$ accuracy that consumes $5\ \mathrm{mJ}$ per inference:
$$
\eta_E=\frac{0.955}{0.005}=191\ \mathrm{J}^{-1}
$$
The second model produces a slightly smaller correct-prediction ratio, yet it provides far more accuracy per unit of energy because its energy requirement is much lower. The comparison reveals a tradeoff that raw accuracy leaves hidden. One model favors the highest predictive performance, while the other uses its energy budget more efficiently.

The ratio also explains why unit conversion deserves attention. Dividing by $15$ while treating millijoules as joules would make the first result one thousand times too small. Since
$$
1\ \mathrm{mJ}=0.001\ \mathrm{J}
$$
Every millijoule value must be divided by $1000$ before a per-joule metric is calculated.

**Question.** Can a model improve its normalized energy efficiency even when its accuracy stays unchanged?

**Answer.** Yes. If accuracy remains fixed while energy consumption decreases, the denominator of $\eta_E=A/E$ becomes smaller, so the ratio grows. For example, an accuracy of $0.96$ at $0.020\ \mathrm{J}$ gives $48\ \mathrm{J}^{-1}$. The same accuracy at $0.010\ \mathrm{J}$ gives $96\ \mathrm{J}^{-1}$. Halving the energy doubles the normalized efficiency.

Energy efficiency evaluates the cost of producing predictions. Training introduces a different time-related question: how long does the learning process take to reach an acceptable level of performance? Training proceeds in **epochs**, where one epoch represents one complete pass through the training data. Accuracy can be evaluated after each epoch, producing a sequence such as $A_1,A_2,A_3,\ldots$, where $A_t$ is the measured accuracy after epoch $t$.

To reduce this sequence to one convergence measure, choose a **target accuracy**, written $A_{\text{target}}$. The target represents the performance level considered sufficient for the comparison or task. The **convergence time** is the earliest epoch whose measured accuracy reaches or exceeds that target:
$$
T_{\text{conv}}
=
\min \left\{t \mid A_t \geq A_{\text{target}}\right\}
$$
In this expression, $t$ is an epoch number, $A_t$ is the accuracy measured at epoch $t$, $A_{\text{target}}$ is the designated target accuracy, and $T_{\text{conv}}$ is the convergence time measured in epochs. The condition $A_t \geq A_{\text{target}}$ identifies every qualifying epoch. The minimum operator, $\min$, selects the earliest one.

Suppose a hypothetical training run produces accuracies of $0.72$, $0.81$, $0.87$, $0.90$, and $0.92$ over epochs 1 through 5. With a target accuracy of $0.90$, epoch 4 is the earliest qualifying epoch, so
$$
T_{\text{conv}}=4
$$
If the target is changed to $0.92$, the measured convergence time becomes 5 epochs. The training run stayed exactly the same; the evaluation threshold changed. Convergence time therefore always carries an implicit pair of ingredients: a learning curve and a chosen target.

The target should reflect the performance requirement used for the comparison. A low threshold will usually be reached earlier, while a demanding threshold may require more epochs. Some training runs may never reach the selected target during the observed training period. In that case, convergence time remains unreached within that period rather than being assigned the final epoch automatically.

**Question.** What happens if accuracy reaches the target, drops below it, and rises again later?

**Answer.** The threshold-based definition uses the earliest epoch that reaches or exceeds the target, so the first crossing determines $T_{\text{conv}}$. If sustained performance is required, the evaluation rule can instead require accuracy to remain above the target for a specified number of epochs. That creates a different convergence criterion and should be stated explicitly.

Together, these metrics create a compact mental model of performance under constraints. Normalized energy efficiency asks how much correct-prediction performance each joule buys. Convergence time asks how many complete passes through the training data are needed before accuracy first satisfies a chosen requirement. One ratio connects predictive quality to operating cost, while one threshold crossing connects training progress to elapsed epochs. Reading them alongside accuracy reveals whether a model predicts well, spends energy effectively, and reaches an acceptable performance level within the available training time.
