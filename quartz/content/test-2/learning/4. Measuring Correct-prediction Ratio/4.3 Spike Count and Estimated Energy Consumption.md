## Spike Count and Estimated Energy Consumption

Once neurons, simulation timesteps, spike events, and synaptic operations are defined, computational activity can be measured by counting what happens during one inference. Imagine observing a small spiking network for four timesteps. Each neuron either emits a spike during a timestep or remains silent. Whenever a spike travels through a connection and contributes to a receiving neuron's computation, the network performs a **synaptic operation**. These two kinds of activity-emitted spikes and resulting synaptic operations-provide the counts needed to estimate how much computation the inference requires.

Begin with a single neuron observed at a single timestep. Its activity can be represented by a **spike indicator**, a variable that takes the value $1$ when the neuron emits a spike and $0$ when it remains silent. Let
$$
S_i(t)=
\begin{cases}
1, & \text{if neuron } i \text{ emits a spike at timestep } t,\\
0, & \text{if neuron } i \text{ remains silent at timestep } t.
\end{cases}
$$
Here, $i$ is the **neuron index**, which identifies one neuron in the measured network, and $t$ is the **timestep index**, which identifies one discrete moment in the simulation. The symbol $s_i(t)$ is the spike indicator for that neuron and timestep. Because its value is either zero or one, it also acts as a tiny counter: it contributes one to the total when a spike occurs and zero otherwise.

Suppose three neurons are observed for four timesteps. Their spike indicators might be
$$
\begin{array}{c|cccc}
 & t=1 & t=2 & t=3 & t=4\\
\hline
I=1 & 0 & 1 & 0 & 1\\
I=2 & 0 & 0 & 1 & 0\\
I=3 & 1 & 0 & 0 & 1
\end{array}
$$
Reading across the first row shows that neuron 1 spikes twice. Neuron 2 spikes once, and neuron 3 spikes twice. Counting every $1$ in the table gives five spike events during the observation window. The same procedure scales to a network containing many neurons and many timesteps: add every spike indicator included in the measurement.

If the network contains $N$ measured neurons and the inference lasts for $T$ timesteps, the **total spike count** is
$$
N_{\mathrm{spike}}
=
\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_{i=1}^{N}s_i(t)
$$
In this expression, $N_{\mathrm{spike}}$ is the total spike count, $N$ is the number of included neurons, $T$ is the number of included simulation timesteps, $i$ selects a neuron, $t$ selects a timestep, and $s_i(t)$ records whether that neuron spikes at that time. The inner sum counts spikes across all neurons at one timestep. The outer sum repeats that count across the entire simulation window. The formula therefore computes the number of discrete spike events generated during the measured inference.

The measurement boundary belongs to the definition of the count. A count across one layer describes activity in that layer, while a count across all included layers describes activity across the measured network. Likewise, an inference observed for ten timesteps has a different counting window from one observed for twenty timesteps. A meaningful spike count therefore accompanies a clearly specified set of neurons, layers, timesteps, and inferences.

**Question.** If two neurons spike during the same timestep, does that timestep contribute one spike or two spikes?

**Answer.** It contributes two spikes. The sum counts neuron-timestep pairs, so each neuron's event contributes separately. If $s_2(5)=1$ and $s_7(5)=1$, their combined contribution at timestep $5$ is $1+1=2$. The timestep identifies when the events occur; it does not merge simultaneous events into a single count.

A spike count measures emitted events, while an energy estimate also accounts for the work those events produce through network connections. When a spike is communicated across a synapse and processed at its destination, that event contributes a synaptic operation. Let $N_{\mathrm{syn}}$ denote the **synaptic-operation count**, meaning the number of included synaptic operations performed during the inference. A neuron with several outgoing connections can cause several such operations from one emitted spike. For example, if one spike is delivered through four counted connections, it contributes one spike event and four synaptic operations under this counting convention.

Counts become an estimated energy value by assigning an energy cost to each counted operation. Let $E_{\mathrm{spike}}$ be the **spike cost**, the assumed energy associated with one spike operation, and let $E_{\mathrm{syn}}$ be the **synaptic-operation cost**, the assumed energy associated with one synaptic operation. Multiplying each unit cost by the corresponding operation count gives the estimated energy contribution of that activity:
$$
\widehat{E}
=
E_{\mathrm{spike}}N_{\mathrm{spike}}
+
E_{\mathrm{syn}}N_{\mathrm{syn}}
$$
Here, $\widehat{E}$ is the **estimated energy consumption** for the measured inference. The product $E_{\mathrm{spike}}N_{\mathrm{spike}}$ estimates the energy associated with all counted spike operations. The product $E_{\mathrm{syn}}N_{\mathrm{syn}}$ estimates the energy associated with all counted synaptic operations. Adding the two contributions gives the operation-count energy estimate.

Consider an inference that produces $100$ spike operations and $600$ synaptic operations. If the assigned spike cost is $2$ energy units per spike operation and the assigned synaptic-operation cost is $0.5$ energy units per synaptic operation, then
$$
\widehat{E}
=
(2)(100)+(0.5)(600)
=
200+300
=
500
\text{ energy units}
$$
The calculation reveals how the estimate is assembled. Spike activity contributes $200$ units, and synaptic activity contributes $300$ units. Even though the spike count is smaller, each spike carries a larger assigned unit cost in this example. The final value depends jointly on activity counts and operation costs.

**Question.** Why can the synaptic-operation count be larger than the total spike count?

**Answer.** One spike can be processed through multiple outgoing synaptic connections. If a spiking neuron sends its event to ten receiving neurons, that single spike can generate ten counted synaptic operations. Network connectivity therefore connects the two measures: spike count records how many events neurons emit, while synaptic-operation count records how much connection-level processing those events trigger.

The complete mental model is a chain from simulated activity to a cost estimate. Every neuron at every timestep contributes a zero-or-one spike indicator. Summing those indicators produces the total spike count. Spikes propagate through included connections, producing a count of synaptic operations. Each count is then weighted by its assigned per-operation cost, and the weighted contributions are added. In this way, a stream of discrete neural events becomes a reproducible measure of computational activity and an operation-based estimate of energy consumption.
