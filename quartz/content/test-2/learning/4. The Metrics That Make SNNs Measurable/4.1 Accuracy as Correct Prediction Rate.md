### Accuracy as Correct Prediction Rate

Imagine an SNN watching a stream of input examples and making one decision for each one: this image is a digit 7, this gesture means "swipe left," this sensor pattern belongs to class A. Earlier ideas gave us the pieces needed to describe that behavior: a **prediction** is the model's chosen answer, a **correct prediction** is a chosen answer that matches the true label, and the **total predictions** are all the answers the model produces over the evaluation set. Accuracy turns those pieces into a single measurable number, so the model's behavior can be compared instead of only described.

A model that makes predictions one by one can feel difficult to judge from examples alone. If it correctly labels the first image, misses the second, correctly labels the third, and keeps going through hundreds or thousands of cases, the raw sequence of right and wrong answers is too long to hold in mind. Accuracy compresses that sequence into a rate: out of all the attempts, what fraction were correct? That fraction gives a first answer to the question, "How often does this model choose the right class?"

The metric is built from counting. Let the number of correct predictions be the count of cases where the model's output matches the expected label. Let the number of total predictions be the count of all cases the model was asked to classify. Accuracy is the ratio between those two counts:
$$
\text{Accuracy} = \frac{\text{Correct Predictions}}{\text{Total Predictions}}
$$
Here, **accuracy** is the fraction of predictions that are correct. **Correct predictions** means the number of outputs that match the true answers. **Total predictions** means the full number of evaluated examples, including both correct and incorrect outputs. The formula lets you compute a correctness rate from a completed evaluation run.

For example, suppose an SNN classifies 1,000 images and 955 of its predicted labels match the true labels. Its accuracy is
$$
\frac{955}{1000} = 0.955
$$
As a percentage, that is 95.5%. The percentage form is often easier to read: the model got 95.5 out of every 100 predictions right on average. A beginner confusion often comes from treating this number as if it tells the whole story of the model. It tells one precise story: how frequently the model's final answers match the labels over the evaluated set. It does not describe how much energy the model used to produce those answers, how long inference took, or how many spikes were generated along the way.

That distinction becomes especially important for spiking neural networks because the promise of an SNN is tied to event-based computation. A conventional ANN usually carries information through continuous-valued activations across many operations. An SNN carries information through spike events, so a silent neuron can mean that no event needs to be processed at that moment. If two models reach similar accuracy, the one that gets there with fewer spikes or lower energy can be much better suited for edge devices, neuromorphic hardware, or battery-limited sensing. Accuracy makes correctness measurable, but SNN evaluation also needs metrics that describe the cost of achieving that correctness.

The useful pattern in the table is that accuracy and energy do not move as one simple number. An ANN can be strong in raw accuracy while carrying a much higher normalized energy cost. A surrogate-gradient SNN can come close in accuracy while using far less energy. An STDP-based SNN can reduce energy even further while giving up some correctness. The columns have to be read together: **model** tells you which computational approach is being evaluated, **accuracy** tells you the rate of correct predictions, and **normalized energy consumption** tells you the relative energy cost attached to those predictions. If accuracy were read alone, the comparison would favor the highest correctness rate and hide the energy behavior that makes SNNs attractive.

A concrete way to interpret this is to imagine choosing a model for a small camera sensor that must classify events all day on a limited battery. A model with slightly higher accuracy may look best if every mistake has the same cost and energy is unlimited. On a device that must run continuously, a model with slightly lower accuracy but dramatically lower energy may be the stronger engineering choice. The evaluation question changes from "Which model is most often correct?" to "Which model is correct enough for the task while staying within the available energy and timing budget?"

The shape of the comparison shows why similar accuracy can mask different computation costs. The ANN sits at the high-energy end, around 200 mJ per inference, because dense continuous-valued operations keep consuming energy across the network. A converted SNN lowers energy substantially, around 20 mJ, but still generates many spikes because rate-coded activity can require large spike counts to represent information. A surrogate-gradient SNN lowers energy further, around 15 mJ, while keeping accuracy close to the ANN range. An STDP-based SNN reaches the lowest energy level, around 5 mJ per inference, with the sparsest spiking activity, while accepting a lower accuracy value. The graph teaches a practical reading habit: look for clusters where accuracy remains reasonably high while energy drops sharply. Those clusters reveal the SNN advantage more clearly than an accuracy column alone.

**Question.** If two models both classify about 97% of examples correctly, are they equally good?

**Answer.** They are equally good only with respect to accuracy on that evaluation set. If one model uses far less energy, fewer spikes, or lower latency, it may be better for real-time or battery-limited deployment. Accuracy tells how often the final answer is correct; it does not tell how expensive each correct answer was to produce.

Accuracy also needs context because SNNs are often chosen for situations where time and energy are part of the task. A gesture-recognition system, for instance, may need an answer quickly enough for a robot to react. A sensor node may need to conserve energy so it can operate for long periods without charging. A spike-sparse model may be preferred because fewer spike events can mean fewer synaptic operations and lower hardware activity. These concerns do not replace accuracy; they complete the measurement. A model that is accurate but too slow may fail in a real-time setting. A model that is accurate but energy-heavy may fail on an edge device. A model that saves energy by sacrificing too much accuracy may fail the classification goal. The useful evaluation is therefore multi-metric: accuracy says whether the answer is right, while energy, latency, spike count, and convergence behavior describe the path taken to produce that answer.

**Question.** Why start with accuracy if it is incomplete?

**Answer.** Accuracy is the simplest correctness measure and gives the baseline for every other comparison. Before asking whether a model is energy-efficient or fast, we need to know whether it is making the right predictions often enough. Once that baseline is visible, the other metrics explain the cost and practicality of reaching it.

A good mental model is to treat accuracy as the scoreboard for correctness, not the full report on the computation. It counts wins over attempts: correct predictions divided by total predictions. For SNNs, that scoreboard becomes meaningful when it is placed beside the resources used to earn those wins. Two networks can arrive at similar correctness rates through very different activity patterns: one may spend dense continuous computation, another may communicate through sparse spikes, and another may trade some accuracy for major energy savings. Accuracy makes prediction behavior measurable; the surrounding metrics reveal whether that behavior is efficient, timely, and suitable for the setting where the SNN must actually run.
