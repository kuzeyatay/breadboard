### Energy Consumption and Energy Efficiency

After measuring accuracy and spike count, the next question is whether the network spends its computation wisely. Imagine an edge camera watching a hallway for motion. Most of the time, the scene stays still. A conventional artificial neural network still pushes dense numerical activations through layer after layer for every inference, even when very little changes. A spiking neural network has a different accounting style: silence costs very little, and work is concentrated around spike events. Energy measurement turns that intuition into a number, so two models can be compared by asking how much physical computation they spend to produce one useful answer.

A spike is a discrete event, so it gives the network a natural unit of activity to count. When a neuron fires, that spike may travel across connections to later neurons. Each connection that receives and uses the spike performs a small piece of computation called a synaptic operation. The energy used by an SNN therefore comes from two closely related sources: the cost of producing or transmitting spikes, and the cost of processing those spikes at synapses. A network with many spikes usually triggers many synaptic operations, so spike count becomes a practical proxy for how much event-driven computation the network is doing.

The total energy for one inference can be written as:
$$
E_{\text{total}} = N_{\text{spikes}} \cdot E_{\text{spike}} + N_{\text{synops}} \cdot E_{\text{synop}}
$$
Here, $E_{\text{total}}$ is the total energy consumed to process one input and produce one inference. $N_{\text{spikes}}$ is the number of spikes generated during that inference. $E_{\text{spike}}$ is the energy cost of one spike event. $N_{\text{synops}}$ is the number of synaptic operations caused by spike activity. $E_{\text{synop}}$ is the energy cost of one synaptic operation. The formula says that total energy grows when the network fires more often, when each spike is expensive, when spikes activate more synapses, or when each synaptic operation is expensive.

A concrete interpretation makes the metric feel less abstract. Suppose one model produces 20,000 spikes for an inference, while another produces 4,000 spikes. If both models run on similar hardware with similar spike and synaptic-operation costs, the second model has a much smaller energy burden because it creates fewer events for the system to route and accumulate. That is why spike count and energy per inference are read together. A low spike count suggests that the network is using sparse activity: fewer neurons fire, fewer downstream updates are triggered, and less energy is consumed.

The model comparison makes this relationship visible through energy per inference and spike count per inference. The ANN reaches the highest energy consumption, about 200 millijoules per inference, because its computation relies on continuous-valued operations rather than sparse spike events. A converted SNN lowers energy to about 20 millijoules, yet it still produces roughly 20,000 spikes per inference because rate-coded spike propagation can require many events to represent values. A surrogate-gradient SNN reduces the burden further, reaching about 15 millijoules with roughly 12,000 spikes by learning spiking patterns more directly. An STDP-based SNN reaches about 5 millijoules with roughly 4,000 spikes, showing the strongest sparsity among the compared models. The pattern is the measurable version of the event-driven idea: fewer spikes usually mean fewer triggered operations, and fewer triggered operations usually mean lower energy.

A beginner may wonder whether the most energy-efficient model is simply the one with the lowest energy number. The answer depends on whether the model still solves the task well. A network that consumes almost no energy by staying silent would be useless if it gives poor predictions. Energy efficiency must therefore couple energy with correctness. Accuracy measures the fraction of predictions the model gets right, while energy consumption measures the cost of producing those predictions. A model becomes efficient when it preserves useful accuracy while reducing energy per inference.

That coupling is captured by normalized energy efficiency:
$$
\eta_{\text{energy}} = \frac{A}{E_{\text{total}}}
$$
Here, $\eta_{\text{energy}}$ is the normalized energy efficiency. $A$ is the model's accuracy, usually expressed as a fraction or percentage. $E_{\text{total}}$ is the total energy consumption for an inference, measured in a unit such as millijoules. The formula computes how much accuracy the model delivers per unit of energy. A larger value means the model produces more correct performance for each unit of energy it spends.

Consider two models. If Model A has 98% accuracy and uses 200 millijoules, its efficiency using percentage accuracy is $98 / 200 = 0.49$ accuracy-points per millijoule. If Model B has 95% accuracy and uses 20 millijoules, its efficiency is $95 / 20 = 4.75$ accuracy-points per millijoule. Model A is more accurate in raw terms, but Model B delivers far more accuracy for each millijoule. This is the reason normalized energy efficiency is useful: it prevents accuracy from hiding energy cost, and it prevents low energy from hiding poor predictive performance.

**Question.** If an ANN has the best accuracy, why might an SNN still be the better choice for an edge device?

**Answer.** An edge device often has limited battery life, heat tolerance, and processing power. An ANN with the highest raw accuracy may consume much more energy per inference, such as around 200 millijoules in the comparison. An SNN with slightly lower accuracy can consume far less energy, such as 15 or 20 millijoules, while still giving strong performance. For a device that must run continuously, the better choice is often the model that gives enough accuracy at a much lower energy cost.

The accuracy and normalized energy consumption columns in the performance comparison help separate raw performance from practical deployability. Accuracy tells how often the model is correct. Normalized energy consumption tells how expensive that correctness is relative to the compared models. A high-accuracy model with high normalized energy consumption may be attractive when power is abundant. A model with slightly lower accuracy and much lower normalized energy consumption becomes attractive when the device must operate under energy constraints. This is why surrogate-gradient SNNs and STDP-based SNNs occupy different useful positions: surrogate-trained models can stay close to ANN accuracy while reducing energy substantially, while STDP-based models push energy and spike count even lower with some accuracy tradeoff.

Energy per inference also gives the metric a hardware-aware meaning. One inference is one complete pass from input to output: one image classified, one sensor event interpreted, or one decision produced. Measuring millijoules per inference tells us how expensive each answer is. If a device performs thousands or millions of inferences, a small per-inference saving becomes a large total saving. Reducing energy from 200 millijoules to 20 millijoules is a tenfold reduction for each decision. Reducing it to 5 millijoules is a fortyfold reduction compared with 200 millijoules. Those savings come from the same mechanism introduced earlier: event-driven computation concentrates work around spikes instead of continuously updating dense activations.

**Question.** Does a lower spike count always guarantee a better model?

**Answer.** A lower spike count usually helps reduce energy because fewer spike events tend to cause fewer synaptic operations. The model still needs enough spikes to represent the input and support accurate decisions. Spike count becomes meaningful when read with accuracy and energy per inference. A sparse model that stays accurate is efficient; a sparse model that loses too much accuracy has saved energy by giving up too much information.

The mental model is an energy budget for useful decisions. Spikes are the visible events, synaptic operations are the downstream work those events trigger, and total energy is the cost of running that event-driven computation for one inference. Normalized energy efficiency then asks how much correctness the network buys with that cost. An SNN becomes measurable as an efficient system when its sparse spikes reduce synaptic work, its total energy falls, and its accuracy remains high enough that each millijoule still produces a reliable answer.
