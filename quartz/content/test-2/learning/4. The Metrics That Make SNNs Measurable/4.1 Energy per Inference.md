## Energy per Inference

After counting spikes and synaptic operations, the natural question becomes: how much energy does one complete decision cost? Imagine a small vision system on a battery-powered device deciding whether an object is present in a camera frame. The device receives an input, runs the network once, and produces an output such as "object detected" or "no object detected." That single forward pass is one **inference**. If the network must do this many times per second, the cost of one inference becomes the basic unit for estimating battery life, heat, and hardware efficiency.

A spiking network gives a useful way to measure this cost because its activity arrives as discrete events. A neuron stays quiet while its membrane potential remains below threshold. When enough input accumulates, the neuron emits a spike. Each spike can trigger work in connected neurons, because downstream synapses must receive and process that event. Energy is therefore tied to two measurable things: how many spikes were produced, and how many synaptic operations those spikes caused.

A **spike count** records the number of spikes generated during one inference. If one input image causes 4,000 spikes across the network, that inference used 4,000 spike events. A **synaptic operation** is the work done when a spike travels through a connection and affects a downstream neuron. If one spike is sent to 100 connected synapses, that one spike can create 100 synaptic operations. This is why spike count and synaptic operation count are related but not identical. Spike count tells us how many events the neurons emitted; synaptic operation count tells us how much connection-level processing those events triggered.

Energy per inference turns these activity counts into a cost. To do that, we assign an energy cost to each kind of event. A spike has an **energy per spike**, meaning the average energy spent to generate or communicate one spike. A synaptic operation has an **energy per synaptic operation**, meaning the average energy spent when one spike is processed through one synaptic connection. Once those unit costs are known or estimated for a hardware platform, the total energy for one inference can be computed by adding the spike-related energy and the synapse-related energy:
$$
E_{\text{total}} = N_{\text{spikes}}E_{\text{spike}} + N_{\text{synops}}E_{\text{synop}}
$$
Here, $E_{\text{total}}$ is the **total energy per inference**, usually reported in units such as millijoules. $N_{\text{spikes}}$ is the **number of spikes** generated during that inference. $E_{\text{spike}}$ is the **energy per spike**. $N_{\text{synops}}$ is the **number of synaptic operations** caused during the inference. $E_{\text{synop}}$ is the **energy per synaptic operation**. The formula lets us compute the energy cost of one completed decision by multiplying each activity count by its unit energy cost, then adding the results.

A simple worked interpretation makes the formula concrete. Suppose an SNN produces $4{,}000$ spikes during one inference. Suppose those spikes produce $80{,}000$ synaptic operations. If each spike costs $0.0002$ millijoules and each synaptic operation costs $0.00001$ millijoules, then the spike-related energy is
$$
4{,}000 \times 0.0002 = 0.8 \text{ mJ}
$$
And the synaptic-operation energy is
$$
80{,}000 \times 0.00001 = 0.8 \text{ mJ}
$$
Adding them gives
$$
E_{\text{total}} = 0.8 + 0.8 = 1.6 \text{ mJ}
$$
This example shows why the metric is more informative than spike count alone. A network with few spikes can still spend substantial energy if each spike fans out through many synapses. A network with more spikes can be efficient if the spikes are sparse in the right places and create fewer costly operations. Energy per inference makes the behavior measurable because it connects visible network activity to physical cost.

The same idea explains why low-energy claims need an explicit cost model. Saying that a spiking network is efficient because it spikes sparsely is only a starting point. The claim becomes measurable when the counted events are paired with energy costs. A model that produces 20,000 spikes per inference will usually have a larger event-processing burden than one that produces 4,000 spikes, assuming similar hardware and similar synaptic fan-out. Reported comparisons show this pattern: converted SNNs can reduce energy relative to conventional artificial networks, reaching about 20 mJ per inference while still producing around 20,000 spikes. Surrogate-gradient SNNs can reduce both energy and spike count further, reaching about 15 mJ with roughly 12,000 spikes. STDP-based SNNs can be even sparser, around 4,000 spikes, with energy near 5 mJ per inference. These numbers follow the same logic as the formula: fewer events usually mean fewer costly operations, especially when the network and hardware are designed to exploit event-driven computation.

The reason spiking systems can save energy is that silence does useful work. In a dense continuous-valued network, each layer typically processes arrays of values whether the input is changing strongly or weakly. In a spiking network, a neuron that does not reach threshold does not emit an event. No spike means fewer downstream synaptic operations. During inference, computation concentrates around moments and pathways where activity crosses threshold. The network spends energy where events occur, so sparse firing becomes a route to lower cost.

**Question.** If a network produces fewer spikes, does it always have lower energy per inference?

**Answer.** Fewer spikes usually reduce energy because there are fewer events to generate and fewer opportunities to trigger synaptic operations. The full energy cost still depends on $E_{\text{spike}}$, $N_{\text{synops}}$, and $E_{\text{synop}}$. A small number of spikes can be expensive if each spike activates many synapses or if the hardware has a high cost per event. Energy per inference is useful because it includes both spike generation and synaptic processing instead of relying on spike count alone.

Energy per inference also helps compare different SNN training approaches. ANN-to-SNN conversion often preserves useful behavior from a trained artificial network, but it may rely on rate-coded activity, where information is represented through many spikes over a time window. That can lead to a higher spike count and therefore more energy. Surrogate-gradient training can learn spiking patterns more directly, often reducing unnecessary spikes while keeping strong predictive performance. STDP-based learning can produce very sparse activity, which helps lower energy further, though the broader system may trade that energy gain against other performance needs.

**Question.** What does "5 mJ per inference" mean in practical terms?

**Answer.** It means that one complete pass from input to output costs about 5 millijoules of energy. If the network classifies one sensor frame, that single classification costs 5 mJ. If it performs many classifications, the total energy scales with the number of inferences: 100 inferences at 5 mJ each would use about 500 mJ, assuming the cost stays the same for each input.

The mental model is a chain from events to cost. An input causes neurons to accumulate signals over time. Some neurons cross threshold and emit spikes. Those spikes travel through synapses and create synaptic operations. Each spike and each synaptic operation carries an energy cost. Energy per inference adds those costs over one complete decision, turning network activity into a physical efficiency measure. This is why the metric sits at the center of energy analysis for spiking neural networks: it links the sparse timing of spikes to the practical cost of computation.
