Imagine training two spiking neural networks for the same classification task. Both eventually reach a useful accuracy, but one reaches that level after 20 passes through the training data while the other needs 80. If the only number you record is the final accuracy, these two training runs look almost the same. If you care about how long the model takes to become useful, they are very different. A model that learns sooner can save training compute, shorten experimentation cycles, and become practical in settings where repeated retraining or adaptation is part of the job.

A training process unfolds in repeated rounds called **epochs**. One epoch means the model has had one full pass through the training dataset. During each epoch, the model adjusts its internal parameters, then its performance can be checked with an accuracy value. Accuracy is the fraction of examples the model classifies correctly, often written as a percentage. If a model classifies 970 out of 1000 examples correctly, its accuracy is 97%.

Convergence time turns this sequence of epoch-by-epoch accuracy measurements into a single training behavior metric. It asks a simple operational question: when did the model first become good enough? To answer that, we choose a **target accuracy**, which is the accuracy level that counts as acceptable for the task. Then we look for the earliest epoch where the model reaches that target.

The metric can be written as:
$$
T_{\text{conv}} = \min \{ e \mid A(e) \ge A_{\text{target}} \}
$$
Here, $T_{\text{conv}}$ is the **convergence time**, measured in epochs. The symbol $e$ represents a training epoch. The function $A(e)$ means "the model's accuracy after epoch $e$." The value $A_{\text{target}}$ is the chosen target accuracy threshold. The symbol $\min$ means "take the smallest epoch number that satisfies the condition." The condition $A(e) \ge A_{\text{target}}$ says that the accuracy at epoch $e$ is at least as high as the target.

This formula lets you compute the first moment, in training epochs, when the model crosses the useful-performance line. Suppose the target accuracy is 97%. A surrogate-gradient spiking neural network has accuracies of 91%, 94%, 96.5%, 97.1%, and 97.4% at epochs 5, 10, 15, 20, and 25. The first listed epoch at or above 97% is epoch 20, so its convergence time is 20 epochs. The later value of 97.4% at epoch 25 does not change the convergence time, because the metric cares about the first crossing of the threshold.

The word **minimum** does important work in the definition. Training can continue after the target is reached, and accuracy may still improve slightly, fluctuate, or settle into a stable range. Convergence time records the earliest epoch that meets the target, because the metric is designed to measure training speed rather than final polish. A run that reaches 97% at epoch 20 and later reaches 97.8% has a convergence time of 20 epochs for a 97% target. A different run that reaches 97.8% eventually but only crosses 97% at epoch 60 has a convergence time of 60 epochs for the same target.

**Question.** If two models both finish training at 98% accuracy, do they have the same convergence time?

**Answer.** They have the same convergence time only if they first reached the chosen target accuracy at the same epoch. If the target is 97% and Model A first reaches it at epoch 20 while Model B first reaches it at epoch 50, their convergence times are 20 and 50 epochs even though both later finish at 98%. Convergence time measures how quickly training reaches useful performance, not just how high the final score becomes.

This distinction matters for comparing training methods in spiking neural networks because different methods can learn at different speeds even when their final performance is close. Surrogate-gradient training can reach competitive accuracy with relatively fast stabilization, with reported behavior around the 20th epoch in the available comparison. STDP-based training can be more energy-efficient during inference and produce sparse spiking activity, while often taking more epochs to settle and sometimes reaching a lower accuracy plateau. The convergence time metric gives a clean way to express that difference: one method may become practically usable earlier, while another may be attractive for different reasons such as sparse activity or low energy use.

A beginner confusion often comes from hearing the word "converged" and imagining that training has completely stopped changing. In this metric, convergence means the model has reached a chosen accuracy threshold for the first time. The model may continue to change afterward. Its accuracy may improve a little, wobble around, or stabilize. The threshold turns the vague idea of "learning has gone far enough" into a measurable event.

The target accuracy threshold must be chosen before the metric is interpreted. A demanding target gives a larger convergence time than an easier one. If a model reaches 95% accuracy at epoch 12 and 97% accuracy at epoch 20, then its convergence time is 12 epochs for a 95% target and 20 epochs for a 97% target. The model has one training history, but the convergence time depends on the performance line you ask it to cross. This is why comparisons need the same target accuracy threshold across models. Comparing one model at a 95% target with another at a 97% target would mix two different standards.

Convergence time also helps when choosing between models for an application. A research setting that trains once and deploys for a long time may tolerate slower convergence if the deployed model has desirable behavior afterward. A real-time learning system, an adaptive sensor, or an experiment that must be retrained many times benefits from faster convergence because useful performance arrives sooner. Training speed becomes part of model selection because the cost of learning is part of the model's practical behavior.

**Question.** Why use the first epoch that reaches the target instead of the epoch where accuracy looks stable?

**Answer.** The first target-crossing epoch is easier to define consistently. "Looks stable" can depend on visual judgment or on extra rules about how much fluctuation is allowed. The minimum epoch satisfying $A(e) \ge A_{\text{target}}$ gives a precise measurement: find the earliest epoch where the accuracy is high enough. That makes convergence time easier to compare across training runs.

The mental model is a race toward a usefulness line. Each epoch is one step forward in training, and the accuracy after that epoch tells you how close the model is to the line. The target accuracy defines the line, and convergence time is the step number where the model first reaches it. Once you see it this way, convergence time becomes more than a label for a finished training run. It is a compact measurement of learning speed, turning the behavior of training into a number that can be compared alongside other model-selection concerns.
