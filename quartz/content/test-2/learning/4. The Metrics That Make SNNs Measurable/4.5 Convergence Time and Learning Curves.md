### Convergence Time and Learning Curves

Imagine training three spiking neural networks for the same recognition task. After each epoch, each model has seen the training data once, adjusted its internal parameters, and produced two familiar measurements: a loss value that says how costly its mistakes still are, and an accuracy value that says what fraction of examples it classifies correctly. If all three models eventually become useful, the practical question becomes sharper: which one becomes useful soonest? A model that reaches good performance after a few epochs is easier to train, easier to tune, and better suited to situations where learning time is limited.

The simplest way to make "learns quickly" measurable is to choose a performance level before comparing models. That chosen level is called a **target accuracy**. If the target accuracy is 90%, then the training process asks a concrete question: at what epoch does the model first reach at least 90% accuracy? The answer is the model's **convergence time**. In this setting, convergence does not mean that every number has stopped changing forever. It means the training curve has reached the chosen performance target.

A compact way to write this is:
$$
T_{\text{conv}} = \min \{ e : A(e) \geq A_{\text{target}} \}
$$
Here, $T_{\text{conv}}$ is the convergence time, measured in epochs. The symbol $e$ means a particular epoch number. The function $A(e)$ means the model's accuracy after epoch $e$. The value $A_{\text{target}}$ is the target accuracy chosen for the comparison. The expression says: look across the epochs, find the first epoch where the model's accuracy reaches or exceeds the target, and call that epoch the convergence time.

For example, suppose a surrogate-gradient SNN has accuracies of 72%, 81%, 88%, 91%, and 93% over the first five epochs, and the target accuracy is 90%. Its convergence time is epoch 4, because epoch 4 is the earliest point where its accuracy reaches the target. If a converted SNN reaches 90% at epoch 8, then the surrogate-gradient model has faster convergence for that target. The comparison depends on the target: a low target may make several models look similar, while a demanding target separates models by how quickly they approach high performance.

Loss curves give another view of the same learning process. **Training loss** measures how much error remains according to the training objective. A falling loss curve means the model's updates are reducing the cost of its predictions. A steep early drop means the model is finding useful adjustments quickly. A curve that flattens at a high value means the model is still making costly errors even after more epochs. Accuracy tells whether predictions are correct often enough; loss tells how the training process is improving underneath that final correctness score.

The training-loss comparison below shows three SNN training approaches over epochs: converted SNNs, surrogate-gradient SNNs, and STDP-based SNNs. The visual pattern matters more than any single point. The surrogate-gradient curve falls most steeply and settles lower by the end of the 20-epoch window, which shows fast and stable reduction in training error. The converted SNN improves more moderately. The STDP-based curve falls more slowly and levels off at a higher loss, showing slower convergence under this training setup.

The shape of this loss graph teaches how to read convergence as a process rather than a finish-line label. A curve that drops quickly during early epochs is learning useful structure early. A curve that keeps bouncing or declining slowly is still searching for useful changes. A curve that becomes nearly flat has stabilized; if it stabilizes at a low loss, training has found a good fit, while if it stabilizes at a higher loss, the model has settled with more remaining error. In the comparison here, the surrogate-gradient SNN shows the clearest fast-convergence pattern because its loss decreases rapidly and becomes stable by the twentieth epoch.

Reading the table beside the graph strengthens the interpretation. The surrogate-gradient loss values decline in the most consistent way, so the curve's steep descent is backed by the row-by-row numbers. The converted SNN values show a slower reduction, matching the more gradual graph. The STDP-based values improve least aggressively and settle higher, which makes its slower convergence visible numerically. This is why convergence is best treated as a measurable training behavior: the statement "one model learns faster" becomes a claim about when it reaches a target and how its loss changes across epochs.

Accuracy curves complete the picture because a model can reduce loss while the accuracy curve changes at a different pace. Accuracy is threshold-like: a prediction counts as correct or incorrect. Loss is more sensitive: it can improve when the model becomes more confident in the right answer, or less confidently wrong, even before many predictions flip from incorrect to correct. Looking only at final accuracy would hide the path taken to reach that value. Looking only at loss would hide whether lower error has translated into correct classifications often enough.

The STDP-based SNN improves more slowly and reaches a lower accuracy level in this comparison, which fits the pattern of slower loss reduction and earlier plateauing.

Now the metric becomes practical. If the target accuracy is placed somewhere along these curves, convergence time is the first epoch where each curve crosses that level. A lower target may be reached by several models within the 20-epoch window. A higher target may be reached quickly by the surrogate-gradient SNN, later by the converted SNN, and perhaps slowly or incompletely by the STDP-based SNN under the same conditions. The target turns a visual learning curve into a direct measurement.

**Question.** Why do we need both loss curves and accuracy curves if convergence time is defined using target accuracy?

**Answer.** Target accuracy gives a clean time-to-performance measurement, but the loss curve explains how training is getting there. Two models might reach the same accuracy at nearby epochs, while one has a steadily falling loss and the other has an unstable or flattening loss. The first model is showing a more reliable learning process. The accuracy curve answers "when does it become correct often enough?" while the loss curve answers "how smoothly and strongly is the training objective improving?"

A common beginner confusion is to treat convergence as the final accuracy after the last epoch. Convergence time is a time measurement, so it depends on the path through training. If two SNNs both end near the same accuracy at epoch 20, the one that reached the target at epoch 6 converged faster than the one that reached it at epoch 18. The final score still matters, but it answers a different question. Final accuracy says how well the model performs at the end of the training window. Convergence time says how soon the model became good enough by a chosen standard.

**Question.** If an STDP-based SNN has slower convergence, does that make it a poor training method?

**Answer.** Slower convergence means it takes more epochs to reduce loss or reach a target accuracy in this comparison. That measurement belongs alongside other measurements, such as energy use and spike count. An STDP-based SNN can be attractive when sparse spiking and low energy are priorities, while a surrogate-gradient SNN can be attractive when fast convergence and high accuracy are priorities. Convergence time helps make that tradeoff explicit instead of hiding it behind a single performance number.

The mental model is a race against a chosen performance line. Each epoch moves a model forward along its learning curve. The loss curve shows whether the model is steadily reducing training error; the accuracy curve shows when those improvements become correct predictions often enough. Convergence time marks the first crossing of the target accuracy line. Once these pieces are connected, "how quickly does this SNN training method learn?" becomes something visible, countable, and comparable across converted, surrogate-gradient, and STDP-based SNNs.
