### Normalized Energy Efficiency

After measuring accuracy, latency, energy per inference, and spike count separately, a natural question appears: if two models both solve the task fairly well, which one gives more correct behavior for each unit of energy it spends? Imagine a small vision system on a battery-powered device. One model recognizes images with very high accuracy but spends a large amount of energy each time it makes a prediction. Another model is slightly less accurate but uses far less energy. Looking only at accuracy favors the first model. Looking only at energy favors the second. Normalized energy efficiency gives a way to connect those two facts in one measurable quantity.

Accuracy tells us how often the model gives the correct answer. If a classifier has an accuracy of 97%, then out of 100 similar test inputs, it is expected to classify about 97 correctly. Energy consumption tells us how much energy the model spends to make one inference, usually measured in millijoules per inference, abbreviated mJ/inference. A model that uses 200 mJ per inference spends forty times as much energy as a model that uses 5 mJ per inference. These two measurements describe different sides of the same decision: correctness and cost.

Normalized energy efficiency combines them by asking how much accuracy is obtained per unit of energy:
$$
\text{Normalized Energy Efficiency} = \frac{\text{Accuracy}}{\text{Energy Consumption}}
$$
Here, **normalized energy efficiency** means the amount of predictive correctness gained for each unit of energy spent. **Accuracy** is the model's correctness score, usually written as a percentage or decimal fraction. **Energy consumption** is the energy required for one inference, commonly measured in millijoules per inference. The formula lets you compute an "accuracy per energy" score, so models with different energy costs can be compared on a shared efficiency scale.

For example, suppose a conventional neural network reaches 99.2% accuracy while consuming 200 mJ per inference. Using the percentage directly, its normalized energy efficiency is:
$$
\frac{99.2}{200} = 0.496
$$
That means the model provides about 0.496 percentage points of accuracy for each millijoule spent. Now consider a surrogate-gradient spiking neural network with about 97.8% accuracy and 15 mJ per inference:
$$
\frac{97.8}{15} = 6.52
$$
This model gives about 6.52 percentage points of accuracy per millijoule. Its raw accuracy is lower, but its accuracy per energy is much higher. The ratio makes visible what separate measurements can make harder to feel: a small loss in correctness can sometimes buy a large reduction in energy.

The same idea becomes clearer with an ultra-low-energy spiking system. An STDP-based spiking neural network may reach about 95.5% accuracy while using about 5 mJ per inference. Its normalized energy efficiency is:
$$
\frac{95.5}{5} = 19.1
$$
This number says that each millijoule buys much more correct classification behavior than in the higher-energy models. The model is less accurate than the strongest conventional network, but it is extremely efficient when energy is scarce. For a wearable sensor, implantable device, or remote edge system, that kind of tradeoff can be more useful than maximizing accuracy alone.

A helpful way to think about the ratio is like distance per fuel in a vehicle. A car that travels 500 km on 50 liters gives 10 km per liter. Another car that travels 450 km on 15 liters gives 30 km per liter. The first car travels farther in total, but the second uses fuel more efficiently. Normalized energy efficiency works similarly: accuracy is the useful outcome, and energy is the resource consumed to obtain it.

Spike count connects naturally to this metric because spikes are a major part of the energy story in spiking neural networks. A dense conventional network keeps computing with continuous-valued activations throughout its layers. A spiking network communicates through discrete spike events. When fewer spikes are produced during inference, fewer event-driven computations are needed, and energy can fall. Converted spiking networks can reduce energy substantially compared with conventional networks, but rate-coded spike propagation can still produce many spikes, around tens of thousands per inference. Surrogate-gradient spiking networks can learn more efficient spiking patterns, reducing both energy and spike count. STDP-based spiking networks can be even sparser, reaching only a few thousand spikes per inference and very low energy consumption.

This is why normalized energy efficiency is especially useful for comparing spiking models with conventional models. A conventional network may dominate in raw accuracy, while a spiking network may deliver almost the same accuracy with far less energy. The ratio turns that tradeoff into a single number. It answers the practical question: how much correct prediction do we get for the energy we pay?

**Question.** If one model has higher accuracy, does it always have better normalized energy efficiency?
**Answer.** No. Normalized energy efficiency depends on both accuracy and energy consumption. A model with slightly lower accuracy can have much higher efficiency if it uses much less energy. For instance, 97.8% accuracy at 15 mJ gives a much larger accuracy-per-energy score than 99.2% accuracy at 200 mJ.

The ratio is powerful because it compresses a two-part comparison into one value, but the separate measurements still need to remain visible. A single efficiency number can hide whether a model is efficient because it is very accurate, because it uses very little energy, or because of both. It can also hide latency and spike count. A model might have excellent accuracy per energy but still respond too slowly for a real-time task. Another model might have strong efficiency but require a spike pattern that is unsuitable for a particular neuromorphic chip. The ratio guides comparison, while the individual metrics explain the reason behind the comparison.

Consider two spiking models. A surrogate-gradient model may offer about 97.8% accuracy, about 10 ms latency, and moderate energy around 15 mJ per inference. An STDP-based model may offer lower energy around 5 mJ per inference and sparse spiking activity, but with slightly lower accuracy around 95.5% and slower learning behavior. The normalized efficiency score favors the lower-energy model, while the full evaluation may favor the surrogate-gradient model for a time-sensitive application. The ratio helps narrow the choice, and the surrounding metrics help finish the choice.

**Question.** Why use normalized energy efficiency instead of just reporting energy per inference?
**Answer.** Energy per inference shows cost, but it does not show whether the model is still solving the task well. A model could use very little energy because it performs too little useful computation and gives poor predictions. Normalized energy efficiency keeps correctness in the measurement by dividing accuracy by energy consumption.

The mental model is simple: accuracy measures the useful result, energy measures the price paid, and normalized energy efficiency measures the useful result per unit price. Spiking neural networks become attractive when their event-driven computation lowers that price while preserving enough correctness for the application. The ratio makes that behavior measurable, but good model selection still looks back at the full chain: accuracy for correctness, energy per inference for cost, spike count for event activity, and latency for response time.
