Imagine that an SNN already produces spikes in response to an input, and that a loss measures how far its prediction is from the desired answer. Direct training aims to reduce that loss by adjusting the network's parameters while the signals remain spikes throughout the computation. The network therefore learns the timing and pattern of spike activity needed for the task itself. This end-to-end process is called **direct SNN optimization**: an input enters the spiking network, the network produces a prediction, the loss evaluates the prediction, and an optimization procedure updates the parameters responsible for the spike dynamics.

The practical difficulty appears at the moment a neuron decides whether to spike. A typical spiking neuron accumulates incoming activity until its internal state crosses a threshold. Crossing the threshold produces a spike; remaining below it produces silence. That threshold creates a sudden jump in the neuron's output. Ordinary gradient-based optimization relies on smooth changes: it asks how a small parameter adjustment would change the output and, through the output, change the loss. At a hard spike threshold, tiny adjustments usually leave the spike decision unchanged, while an adjustment that crosses the threshold changes the output abruptly. The resulting gradient provides little useful guidance for learning.

A **surrogate gradient** supplies that guidance. During the forward computation, the neuron still uses its hard threshold and emits discrete spikes. During the backward computation, when the optimizer determines how parameters contributed to the loss, it temporarily treats the threshold as though it had a smooth slope near the firing point. "Surrogate" means substitute: the method substitutes a manageable gradient for the problematic gradient of the spike decision. Parameters can then receive graded update signals, especially when a neuron's internal state lies near its threshold.

A simple analogy is a doorway controlled by a motion sensor. The actual door has two observable states: closed or open. Yet a technician adjusting the sensor needs more information than those two states provide. The distance between the detected motion and the activation boundary gives a useful adjustment signal. A detection just below the boundary calls for a small change, while one far below it gives much weaker evidence that a tiny adjustment would help. Surrogate-gradient optimization uses a similar picture around a neuron's firing threshold. The spike remains a discrete event, while proximity to the threshold guides learning.

This creates a complete training loop. The SNN processes an example across time, allowing its neurons to integrate inputs and generate spikes. Those spikes determine the network's prediction. The loss measures the prediction error. The backward pass carries information from that error through the network and through time, using surrogate slopes wherever spike thresholds would otherwise interrupt the gradient. An optimizer then updates quantities such as connection strengths so that future spike patterns are more likely to support the correct prediction. Repeating this loop over training examples gradually shapes both which neurons fire and when they fire.

Consider a short sound whose identity depends on how its frequency pattern develops over time. Early spikes may represent the beginning of the sound, while later spikes reflect its changing structure. If the SNN predicts the wrong class, the surrogate-gradient signal can assign useful learning pressure to connections that influenced these events. A neuron that nearly fired at an informative moment may be encouraged toward firing there on a similar example. A neuron whose spike pushed the prediction in an unhelpful direction may receive an update that reduces that response. Through repeated adjustments, the network learns temporal features directly within its spiking dynamics. End-to-end trained SNNs can therefore capture structure in spatiotemporal and auditory signals without separating feature learning from spike-based computation.

**Question.** If the backward pass uses a smooth approximation, does the trained network produce smooth-valued outputs instead of spikes?

**Answer.** The smooth approximation serves only as a learning signal during gradient calculation. The forward pass still applies the firing threshold and produces discrete spike events. Training adjusts the parameters of the spiking system; it does not replace its event-driven operation. Once training is complete, inference follows the learned spike dynamics without needing the surrogate derivative.

The resulting performance profile combines strong predictive accuracy with rapid loss reduction. Surrogate-gradient SNNs can approach the accuracy and generalization of conventional artificial neural networks because their parameters are optimized directly for the target task. Their training loss tends to fall steeply and consistently across epochs, which indicates **fast convergence**: the optimizer finds increasingly effective parameter settings within a relatively short training progression. A smooth surrogate supplies informative updates to many parameters at once, allowing errors at the output to influence earlier layers and earlier time steps.

Fast convergence describes the shape of the learning process rather than a guarantee that every run finishes quickly. The neuron model, input encoding, surrogate shape, and other training settings all affect how easily useful gradients travel through the network. Directly trained SNNs can require careful hyperparameter adjustment, and some configurations may need additional epochs. A stable, steep decline in loss is the practical sign to look for: the model is using the gradient signal effectively instead of plateauing with little improvement.

Accuracy and convergence describe training, while **inference latency** describes how long the trained network must observe and process an input before producing a useful prediction. Surrogate-gradient training can encourage informative spike patterns within short simulation windows. That gives directly trained SNNs a low-latency profile: they can often reach a decision without waiting for a long stream of rate-coded spikes. Fewer generated spikes also reduce the amount of event-driven computation. The resulting network can combine near-ANN accuracy with relatively sparse activity and quick responses, although its exact balance depends on the task and training choices.

**Question.** Why can direct surrogate-gradient training support low latency?

**Answer.** The loss acts on the behavior required for the task, so learning can strengthen spike patterns that convey useful information early in the processing window. The network develops temporal responses suited to the prediction rather than relying on long periods of repeated spiking to represent a value. When the needed evidence appears in a compact sequence of events, inference can finish sooner and with fewer spike operations.

The most useful mental model is a discrete spiking system guided by a temporary smooth learning signal. The network experiences inputs, accumulates internal state, emits spikes, and forms predictions through time. The loss identifies how the prediction should improve, and the surrogate gradient carries that information across otherwise abrupt thresholds. Repetition turns those corrective signals into task-specific spike timing and connectivity. The practical outcome is an SNN that can learn end to end, converge through a strong decline in loss, approach conventional network accuracy, and deliver predictions with sparse activity and low inference latency.
