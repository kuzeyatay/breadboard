Imagine that a trained spiking neural network must recognize a hand gesture on a battery-powered camera. During inference, the camera presents input for a simulation window, spikes travel through the network, and the model produces a prediction. A correct label is valuable, yet the device must also respond quickly, preserve its battery, and process each gesture without generating excessive activity. Training adds another concern: the network must reach useful behavior within a practical number of epochs. Evaluating this system therefore requires several measurements that describe prediction quality, response time, computational activity, energy demand, and learning progress.

Accuracy measures how often the network produces the correct prediction. If the model classifies 100 gestures and assigns the correct label to 95 of them, its accuracy is 95%. This gives a direct measure of task performance, so it remains essential whenever the application depends on reliable classification. Accuracy alone leaves the operating cost hidden. Two models can correctly classify the same number of gestures while requiring very different simulation windows, spike counts, and amounts of energy.

Inference latency describes how long the trained network takes to produce a prediction for one input. In an SNN, this delay is closely connected to temporal processing. The network receives and integrates spikes across simulation steps, so a longer simulation window gives neurons more time to accumulate evidence. That extra time can support a stable prediction, while it also delays the result. A gesture controller that responds after 10-15 milliseconds may feel immediate, whereas a model that requires a much longer window may be unsuitable for a fast control loop even when both models achieve similar accuracy.

Latency must be interpreted in the context of the whole inference process. A model that begins producing useful spikes early in the window can reach a decision quickly. Another model may depend on many repeated spikes to represent the same input and therefore need more simulation steps before its output becomes reliable. Converted SNNs commonly preserve strong classification performance by representing activation strength through spike rates. A larger activation then appears as more spikes over time, which can require higher spike activity and longer windows. Directly trained spiking models can learn temporal patterns that support competitive predictions with shorter reported latencies, including values below 15 milliseconds in the evaluated setting.

Energy per inference measures the energy consumed while processing one input and producing one output. It is commonly reported in millijoules per inference, abbreviated mJ per inference. This unit makes the measurement concrete: if a device performs many inferences, the energy cost of each one accumulates into battery drain and heat. A model using 5 mJ per inference consumes one quarter of the inference energy of a model using 20 mJ under the same measurement conditions. Over one million inferences, that difference becomes 15,000 joules.

Spike-based computation can reduce energy because activity occurs through discrete events. When a neuron remains silent, downstream computation associated with its spikes can remain idle. The resulting benefit depends on how much spiking activity the model actually generates. A network that emits tens of thousands of spikes per input creates many more events for the system to route and process than one that emits only a few thousand. Reported comparisons illustrate the scale of this relationship: configurations producing roughly 20,000 spikes per inference consumed about 20 mJ, configurations around 12,000 spikes consumed about 15 mJ, and sparse configurations near 4,000 spikes reached about 5 mJ. These values belong to their particular experimental setup, yet their pattern gives a useful interpretation: fewer events often accompany lower energy demand.

Spike count records the total number of spikes generated while the network processes one input. It serves as a measure of neural activity and as a practical indicator of the event traffic created during inference. Each spike may trigger communication, memory access, and state updates. A count of 4,000 therefore represents a much sparser computation than a count of 20,000 for the same type of input and evaluation window.

The count gains meaning only when considered beside accuracy and latency. A very low spike count can conserve energy while carrying too little information for precise classification. A high spike count can support a strong prediction while increasing event-processing cost. The simulation window also affects the count: giving a network more time creates more opportunities for neurons to fire. A fair comparison therefore examines spike count together with the window length and the resulting prediction quality.

**Question.** Does a lower spike count always mean that an SNN is better?

**Answer.** A lower count means the network generates fewer events, which can reduce communication and energy costs. Model quality still depends on whether those events carry enough information to produce accurate and timely predictions. A sparse model with lower accuracy may suit an ultra-low-power sensor, while a moderately active model with higher accuracy and shorter latency may suit a real-time recognition system. The deployment goal determines which balance is useful.

The final measurement concerns training rather than inference. Convergence behavior describes how the model's performance changes as training proceeds. Training is divided into epochs, with each epoch representing one pass through the training data. A loss value measures the size of the model's prediction error during this process. When loss falls rapidly and then stabilizes, the model is converging: additional epochs produce progressively smaller changes.

A convergence curve reveals information that the final accuracy conceals. Suppose two models eventually reach similar accuracy. One stabilizes near the twentieth epoch, while the other continues improving slowly for many more epochs. The faster-stabilizing model requires less training time to reach its useful state and makes experimentation easier because its behavior becomes predictable sooner. A curve that settles at a higher loss indicates that training has stabilized with more residual error. Slower convergence can still be acceptable when the resulting model provides another valuable property, such as sparse activity or very low inference energy.

**Question.** Why include convergence when energy and latency already describe efficiency?

**Answer.** Energy and latency describe the cost of using a trained model for one prediction. Convergence describes the process required to obtain that model. A system may be inexpensive during inference yet demand longer training before its behavior stabilizes. Keeping these stages separate allows a designer to evaluate recurring deployment costs and the one-time or repeated cost of learning.

The five metrics form a connected mental model of an SNN's life cycle. Convergence tracks how the network becomes useful during training. Accuracy checks whether its learned behavior solves the task. Latency measures how soon each decision arrives, spike count reveals how much event activity produces that decision, and energy per inference measures the physical cost of processing it. Model selection then becomes a choice among practical priorities: reliable predictions, rapid responses, sparse communication, low energy consumption, and manageable training behavior. Together, these measurements reveal the complete operating profile that a single accuracy value leaves unseen.
