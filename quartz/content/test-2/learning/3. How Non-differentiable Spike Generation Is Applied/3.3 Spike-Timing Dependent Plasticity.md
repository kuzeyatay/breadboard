## Spike-Timing Dependent Plasticity

Once spikes carry information through their timing, a synapse can learn by observing the order in which nearby spikes occur. Imagine two connected neurons. The upstream neuron sends a spike across the synapse, and shortly afterward the downstream neuron fires. That sequence suggests that the incoming spike helped push the downstream neuron toward its firing threshold. The connection can respond by becoming stronger, making a similar incoming spike more influential in the future. If the downstream neuron fires before the incoming spike arrives, the connection can instead become weaker because that particular input arrived too late to explain the response. **Spike-Timing Dependent Plasticity**, usually shortened to **STDP**, turns this temporal relationship into a learning signal.

The adjustable strength of a connection is called its **synaptic weight**. A larger weight gives an arriving spike a stronger effect on the receiving neuron; a smaller weight gives it a weaker effect. **Synaptic plasticity** means that this weight can change through activity. Under STDP, the change depends on the relative timing of two events: a **presynaptic spike**, produced by the sending neuron, and a **postsynaptic spike**, produced by the receiving neuron. The relevant observation is their separation in time and their order.

Consider a presynaptic neuron that fires at 10 milliseconds and a postsynaptic neuron that fires at 13 milliseconds. Their time difference is small, and the presynaptic event came first. The synapse treats this sequence as evidence that the incoming activity contributed to the later output spike, so the connection is strengthened. Now imagine that the postsynaptic neuron fires at 10 milliseconds while the presynaptic spike arrives at 13 milliseconds. The same three-millisecond separation has the opposite order. The arriving spike follows the response, so the synapse is weakened. In this way, timing provides both the direction of adaptation and an indication of how closely the events were related.

The word "dependent" in STDP points to this relationship: weight changes depend on relative timing rather than on spike counts alone. Two neurons might each produce ten spikes, yet their synapse can adapt differently depending on how those spikes line up. Repeated cases in which the presynaptic neuron fires shortly before the postsynaptic neuron tend to reinforce the connection. Repeated cases in which the order is reversed tend to reduce it. The network gradually becomes sensitive to temporal patterns that recur in its inputs.

This mechanism provides a way to apply learning even though spike generation itself is non-differentiable. A neuron emits a discrete spike when its internal state reaches a threshold. Crossing that threshold causes an abrupt change from silence to an event, so an ordinary smooth derivative is unavailable at the moment of firing. STDP uses a different route: it updates the synaptic weight directly from observable spike events and their timing. The learning rule asks when the connected neurons fired, determines their temporal order, and adjusts only the connection between them. It therefore turns the discontinuous events themselves into the information used for adaptation.

STDP is described as a **local learning rule** because each update can be based on information available at one synapse. That synapse needs the timing of the presynaptic spike arriving through it and the timing of the postsynaptic neuron's response. It does not need a network-wide calculation of how every connection contributed to a final error. A large network can therefore contain many synapses adapting through their own nearby event histories.

An analogy is a set of doors equipped with simple timing sensors. Each door records when someone presses its entrance button and when the door opens. If opening repeatedly follows soon after a particular button press, the controller increases that button's influence. If the press repeatedly arrives after the door has already opened, the controller reduces its influence. Each controller uses its own pair of timestamps rather than consulting a central record of every door in the building. In an SNN, the "button press" is the presynaptic spike, the "door opening" is the postsynaptic spike, and the adjustable influence is the synaptic weight.

**Question.** How can a synapse tell whether a presynaptic spike actually caused the postsynaptic neuron to fire?

**Answer.** STDP uses temporal proximity as a local indication of contribution. A presynaptic spike arriving shortly before a postsynaptic spike is treated as likely to have helped produce that response. The rule does not reconstruct the full causal history of the neuron, which may have received many inputs. Instead, repeated timing relationships accumulate over many events. Connections whose spikes consistently precede the response become stronger, while connections with less useful timing tend to weaken.

Because learning arises from the activity pattern itself, STDP can support **unsupervised learning**. The network receives spikes but does not require a supplied label telling it the correct output for each input. Regularities in the incoming event stream create repeated timing relationships. Synapses associated with those relationships are selectively adapted, allowing neurons to become responsive to recurring temporal structure. For example, if a particular group of input neurons repeatedly fires in a similar sequence before an output neuron spikes, the corresponding connections can strengthen. Later presentations of that sequence then have a greater chance of driving the same neuron to threshold.

The event-driven character of STDP also fits **low-activity learning**. Synaptic updates are tied to spikes, so periods of silence naturally contain little or no update activity. In the reported comparison among network models, the STDP-based SNN produced about 4,000 spikes per inference and used about 5 millijoules, the lowest spike count and energy consumption among the compared approaches. These values illustrate the connection between sparse event activity and reduced computation: fewer spikes mean fewer synaptic events to process. The same comparison associates STDP with slower convergence and slightly reduced accuracy, so sparse local adaptation comes with practical tradeoffs rather than guaranteeing the strongest result on every measure.

**Question.** Does a neuron learn merely because two connected neurons both fire frequently?

**Answer.** Their firing frequency alone leaves out the ordering that drives STDP. Learning depends on whether presynaptic spikes repeatedly arrive before or after postsynaptic spikes and on how closely those events occur. Frequent spikes with inconsistent timing may produce a different adaptation pattern from fewer spikes that repeatedly occur in a reliable sequence.

A useful mental model is a synapse acting as a small temporal association detector. It watches an incoming spike, watches the receiving neuron's response, and changes its influence according to the relationship between those events. Across repeated activity, many such local adjustments reshape the network's connections. Spike timing supplies the evidence, synaptic plasticity stores the result, and unsupervised learning emerges as recurring temporal patterns gain stronger pathways through the network.
