## Accuracy, Latency, Energy, and Spike Count Tradeoffs

You already have the basic pieces: an SNN computes with spikes, latency measures how long an inference takes, energy measures how much work the system spends, spike count measures how many events move through the network, and convergence describes how training settles over time. Now imagine choosing a model for a small battery-powered camera that must recognize gestures as they happen. A model with excellent accuracy can still drain the battery too quickly. A model with extremely low energy use can still react too slowly. A model with very few spikes can still give up a few percentage points of recognition accuracy. The results become useful when they are read as a set of tradeoffs rather than as a race with one permanent winner.

A multi-metric evaluation means judging a model by several measurements at once. Accuracy asks, "How often is the answer correct?" Latency asks, "How quickly does the answer arrive?" Energy asks, "How much power is spent per inference?" Spike count asks, "How many spike events were needed to produce the answer?" Convergence asks, "How quickly and reliably does training approach a stable level of performance?" Each metric describes a different cost of computation. When they are placed together, they show the shape of a method's behavior: where it is strong, where it spends resources, and where it asks the designer to compromise.

A simple example makes the conflict concrete. An ANN may reach the strongest raw accuracy, but it uses continuous-valued operations throughout the network. In the reported comparison, this corresponds to about 200 mJ per inference. A converted SNN lowers that energy to about 20 mJ, but still uses roughly 20,000 spikes per inference because rate-coded activity often needs many spikes over a simulation window to represent values. A surrogate-gradient SNN brings energy to about 15 mJ and spike count to about 12,000 while keeping accuracy close to ANN levels, around 1-2% lower in the reported results. An STDP-based SNN reaches the sparsest activity, about 4,000 spikes, and the lowest energy, about 5 mJ per inference, while giving up some accuracy, around 95.5%, and converging more slowly. These numbers show a pattern: each method occupies a different region of the design space.

A performance tradeoff appears when improving one metric tends to push another metric in a less desirable direction. The STDP-based result is the cleanest energy example. Fewer spikes mean fewer event transmissions and fewer operations, so energy drops sharply. That makes sense from the mechanism: if a network stays silent unless a useful timing relationship appears, it spends less work. The cost is that local timing-based learning does not chase a supervised accuracy objective as directly as gradient-based learning does, so the final accuracy and convergence behavior can be less favorable. The surrogate-gradient result sits closer to the accuracy-latency corner. It uses gradient-based optimization to shape spiking behavior, so it can reach high accuracy, converge by about the 20th epoch, and produce low latency around 10 ms, while still using far less energy than a dense ANN.

**Question.** If STDP-based SNNs use the least energy, why would anyone choose a surrogate-gradient SNN instead?

**Answer.** A low-energy model is best when the deployment constraint is strict power use, such as a tiny sensor that must run for a long time. A surrogate-gradient SNN becomes attractive when the system also needs high accuracy and fast response. Its energy use is higher than STDP-based SNNs, about 15 mJ rather than 5 mJ per inference, but it can preserve accuracy close to ANN performance and reach latency near 10 ms. The choice depends on which cost the application can afford.

A deployment constraint is a real-world limit that decides which tradeoff matters most. A mobile or edge device may have a tight energy budget. A robot reacting to motion may have a tight latency budget. A safety-related classifier may have a tight accuracy budget. A neuromorphic system may care strongly about spike count because spikes correspond to communication events on the hardware. Once a constraint is named, the results become easier to read. The model with the highest accuracy can be the wrong choice for a battery sensor. The model with the fewest spikes can be the wrong choice for a task that needs the highest supervised accuracy. The model with the fastest convergence can be useful when training time and adaptation matter.

Metric conflict is the center of SNN evaluation because the metrics measure connected but different consequences of spike-based computation. Spike count and energy often move together because fewer spikes usually mean fewer operations and less communication. Latency and spike count can also interact because some coding schemes need longer simulation windows to collect enough spikes for a stable decision. Accuracy can conflict with both because extra computation, longer windows, or denser activity may help the model classify more reliably. Converted SNNs show this tension clearly: they can preserve competitive performance after conversion from an ANN, but they often require higher spike counts and longer simulation windows. The method inherits useful accuracy behavior, then pays for it through time and activity.

Scaling the task makes this reading even more necessary. On a smaller dataset such as MNIST, several methods can look close enough that a single accuracy number feels persuasive. On a harder setting such as CIFAR-10, accuracy gaps widen: ANNs can reach about 92%, while direct SNNs can drop near 85%. At the same time, the energy advantage of SNNs becomes more visible, with surrogate-trained SNNs operating at less than 10% of ANN energy costs. A larger task therefore stretches the tradeoff curve. Accuracy separates methods more sharply, while energy savings become harder to ignore.

Latency gives another practical interpretation. For event-based gesture recognition on DVS128, surrogate SNNs can produce inference latencies around 5-10 ms, compared with 20-50 ms for ANN counterparts. A delay of a few tens of milliseconds can decide whether a robot reacts smoothly or feels sluggish. In this setting, a method that combines low latency with reasonable accuracy earns its value through timing. The learner should read the latency number as a physical delay in a feedback loop, not just as a column in a result table.

**Question.** Does a lower spike count always mean a better SNN?

**Answer.** A lower spike count usually helps energy efficiency because fewer spikes reduce event communication and computation. The full evaluation still has to include accuracy, latency, and convergence. A network can be very sparse and energy-efficient while learning more slowly or classifying less accurately. Spike count becomes decisive when energy or neuromorphic communication is the main constraint, and it becomes one part of a wider judgment when correctness and reaction time also matter.

No single SNN method wins every metric. Surrogate-gradient SNNs are strong when the goal is high accuracy with low latency and large energy savings relative to ANNs. Converted SNNs are useful when ANN-like performance is desired, while accepting higher spike counts and longer simulation windows. STDP-based SNNs are strongest when sparse activity and minimal energy dominate the design goal, while accepting reduced accuracy and slower convergence. The reported values support model selection by showing how each method spends computation: dense continuous activity buys raw ANN accuracy, optimized spiking buys a balanced accuracy-latency-energy profile, and sparse timing-based learning buys extreme efficiency. A good mental model is to picture each method as a point on a map whose axes are correctness, time, energy, activity, and training behavior. Choosing an SNN means choosing the point whose costs fit the device, task, and environment.
