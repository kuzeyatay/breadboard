### Accuracy and Energy Performance Results

Imagine choosing a vision model for a small battery-powered device, such as a camera that recognizes handwritten digits on a sensor or identifies simple objects at the edge of a network. If the model is judged only by accuracy, the choice seems straightforward: pick the model that gives the largest percentage of correct answers. But the device also has to spend energy every time it makes a prediction. A model that is slightly more accurate may drain far more power, while a model that gives up a small amount of accuracy may run for much longer on the same battery. The performance results become useful when accuracy and energy are read together.

Accuracy measures how often a model gives the correct answer. If a model sees 10,000 MNIST digit images and classifies 9,800 correctly, its MNIST accuracy is 98%. MNIST is a relatively simple image-recognition benchmark built from handwritten digits, so high performance on MNIST shows that a model can learn clean, small visual patterns. CIFAR-10 is harder because its images contain natural objects such as airplanes, cars, birds, cats, and trucks. The objects vary more in color, texture, and background, so CIFAR-10 accuracy usually separates models more strongly than MNIST accuracy does.

Energy consumption measures how much computational energy the model uses to produce one inference, meaning one completed prediction. In these results, energy is also compared in normalized form. Normalized energy consumption treats one model, usually the conventional ANN baseline, as a reference level and expresses the others relative to it. If the ANN is taken as 1.0, then a model using one tenth as much energy has normalized energy 0.1. This lets the eye compare models even when exact millijoule values and accuracy percentages live on different scales.

The table places MNIST accuracy, CIFAR-10 accuracy, and normalized energy consumption side by side so that model comparison becomes a three-axis reading rather than a single ranking.

Read the table across each row rather than down only one column. The ANN/CNN row represents the familiar dense-computation model: it tends to hold the strongest raw accuracy, especially as the task becomes harder, but it also sits at the high-energy end of the comparison. On MNIST, SNN approaches can come close to ANN-level accuracy, with surrogate-gradient-trained SNNs reported within roughly 1-2 percentage points of ANN accuracy. That small accuracy gap has a very different meaning once energy is included. If a surrogate-gradient SNN reaches about 97.8% accuracy while using around 15 mJ per inference instead of about 200 mJ for the ANN, the model is sacrificing only a little correctness while cutting energy by more than an order of magnitude.

A useful way to interpret normalized energy is to translate it into repeated predictions. Suppose the ANN spends 200 mJ for one prediction and a surrogate-gradient SNN spends 15 mJ. With the same 200 mJ budget, the ANN makes about one prediction, while the surrogate-gradient SNN makes about 13 predictions because $200 \div 15 \approx 13.3$. The normalized-energy comparison captures that practical difference: the lower-energy model can be asked to classify more often, run longer, or fit into a tighter power budget.

CIFAR-10 makes the tradeoff sharper because the task is more complex. When moving from MNIST-scale recognition to CIFAR-10-scale recognition, accuracy differences widen. ANNs reach around 92% on CIFAR-10, while direct SNN approaches can drop closer to 85%. That wider gap means the energy-saving choice carries a larger accuracy cost on the harder dataset. At the same time, energy savings become more valuable as model size and computation grow. Surrogate-trained SNNs operating at less than 10% of ANN energy cost show why a lower-accuracy model may still be the stronger engineering choice for edge devices, robotics, or neuromorphic hardware.

![Performance analysis and energy of SNNs versus ANN across model types](/test-2/assets/source-visuals/2510-27379v1-page-7-graph-g1-performance-analysis-and-energy-of-snns-versus-a.png)

*Performance analysis and energy of SNNs versus ANN across model types* *(p. 7)*

The shape of the comparison shows why the best model depends on the target use. The ANN/CNN sits high in raw accuracy but also high in energy, around 200 mJ per inference. A converted SNN lowers energy to roughly 20 mJ, although its rate-coded spike propagation can still produce many spikes, around 20,000 per inference. A surrogate-gradient SNN improves the balance further, reaching about 15 mJ with around 12,000 spikes while keeping accuracy close to the ANN. An STDP-based SNN reaches the lowest-energy region, around 5 mJ per inference with about 4,000 spikes, while accepting lower accuracy and slower convergence.

This pattern gives a concrete meaning to the accuracy-energy tradeoff. A tradeoff appears when improving one desirable quantity tends to worsen another. Here, accuracy and energy pull against each other across model types. The ANN is attractive when the highest possible classification score matters most and power is available. The surrogate-gradient SNN is attractive when the system needs near-ANN accuracy with much lower energy. The STDP-based SNN is attractive when energy is extremely scarce and a modest loss in accuracy is acceptable. The converted SNN occupies a middle region: it inherits useful behavior from an ANN but can require more spike activity and longer simulation windows than more directly optimized spiking approaches.

**Question.** If the ANN has the highest accuracy, why would anyone choose an SNN?

**Answer.** An SNN can give nearly the same accuracy while using far less energy. For example, a surrogate-gradient SNN can approach ANN accuracy within about 1-2 percentage points while using around 15 mJ per inference instead of about 200 mJ. On a battery-powered or always-on device, that energy reduction can matter more than the small accuracy difference.

A common beginner confusion is to treat MNIST accuracy as the final verdict because the percentages are easy to compare. MNIST accuracy only becomes meaningful beside cost. A model with slightly lower MNIST accuracy and dramatically lower normalized energy may be preferable for an embedded device, while a high-energy model with slightly higher accuracy may be preferable for a server-side application where power is less constrained. The same reasoning becomes even more careful on CIFAR-10, where accuracy differences grow and the task better exposes the limits of each model type.

**Question.** Does lower normalized energy always mean the model is better?

**Answer.** Lower normalized energy means the model spends less energy per prediction relative to the reference model, but the decision also depends on accuracy and task difficulty. An STDP-based SNN can use the least energy, around 5 mJ per inference, but a surrogate-gradient SNN may be chosen when the application needs higher accuracy with still-large energy savings.

The mental model is a map rather than a leaderboard. MNIST accuracy tells how well the model handles a clean, simpler vision task. CIFAR-10 accuracy tells how well the model survives a harder visual setting. Normalized energy tells how expensive each prediction is relative to the baseline. Once these axes are read together, the results show a family of choices: dense ANNs buy maximum accuracy with high energy, surrogate-gradient SNNs buy a strong balance, converted SNNs reduce energy while carrying spike-count costs, and STDP-based SNNs push deepest into low-power operation. The right model is the one whose position on this map matches the job it must perform.
