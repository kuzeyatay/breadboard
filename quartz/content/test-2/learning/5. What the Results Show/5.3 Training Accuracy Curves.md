### Training Accuracy Curves

Imagine training three spiking neural networks for the same recognition task and checking their accuracy after each pass through the training data. After one pass, one model may already recognize many examples correctly. Another may improve steadily but need more passes before it becomes reliable. A third may improve at first and then stop gaining much accuracy. If you only look at the final accuracy after training ends, these three stories can collapse into a single number. A learning curve keeps the story visible by showing how accuracy changes across epochs, so the result includes both where a model finishes and how it gets there.

An **epoch** is one complete pass through the training set. If a model trains for 20 epochs, it has had 20 opportunities to adjust its behavior using the available training examples. **Training accuracy** is the fraction of training examples the model classifies correctly at a given point in training. If a model sees 1,000 training examples and classifies 920 correctly after epoch 8, its training accuracy at epoch 8 is 92%. A **learning curve** plots that accuracy against epoch number. The horizontal direction shows time in training; the vertical direction shows how well the model is doing on the training task. The shape of the curve becomes an **accuracy trajectory**, meaning the path the model follows as it learns.

This path is especially useful for comparing converted SNNs, surrogate-gradient SNNs, and STDP-based SNNs because they do not learn in the same way. A converted SNN begins from a trained artificial neural network and translates its continuous activations into spike-based behavior. Its accuracy can be strong, but the spiking version often needs enough simulation time and enough spike activity to approximate the original ANN's activation rates well. A surrogate-gradient SNN is trained directly as a spiking network using a workaround that makes spike-based learning compatible with gradient descent. Since an ideal spike is a sudden event, its exact firing step is hard to optimize with ordinary gradients; the surrogate gradient supplies a smooth training signal during learning while preserving spiking behavior during operation. An STDP-based SNN learns through **spike-timing dependent plasticity**, where connections change according to the relative timing of spikes. When one neuron tends to fire shortly before another, the connection can strengthen; when the timing is less useful, it can weaken. This local timing rule supports unsupervised adaptation, but it usually produces a more gradual accuracy trajectory.

The line for each model shows the mean accuracy, while the shaded band around it shows variation across five independent runs, with the band representing about one standard deviation above and below the mean. A narrow band means repeated runs behaved similarly; a wider band means the training outcome varied more from run to run.

The surrogate-gradient curve rises most sharply. That steep early climb means the model gains accuracy quickly during the first part of training. By around the 20th epoch, it stabilizes near 98%, which means additional epochs are producing smaller improvements because the model has already reached a high-performing region. In learning-curve language, the model has **converged**: its accuracy has stopped changing much from one epoch to the next. The important detail is the route to convergence. A model that reaches high accuracy quickly can be useful when training time is limited or when fast optimization behavior is part of the design goal.

The converted SNN curve improves more slowly. Its trajectory suggests that the converted model can become competitive, but the path is less direct. This makes sense for a model whose spiking behavior must approximate computations that were originally continuous-valued. The conversion gives the model a strong starting structure, but the spike-based representation may need more epochs or longer simulation windows to express the original network's decisions accurately. If the final converted-SNN accuracy appears close to another model's final accuracy, the curve still tells you that the converted system may have taken a slower route to get there.

The STDP-based curve rises more gradually and plateaus earlier. A plateau means the curve flattens because training is no longer producing large accuracy gains. For STDP, this fits the character of timing-based local learning. Each connection updates from nearby spike relationships rather than from a global error signal that directly says how wrong the whole network's classification was. The model can adapt useful spike patterns, but its accuracy gains tend to accumulate slowly and may settle below the directly trained surrogate-gradient model. The plateau should be read as a property of the learning rule in this comparison: the STDP system is finding structure through spike timing, but its training accuracy does not keep climbing at the same rate through all 20 epochs.

**Question.** If the surrogate-gradient SNN and converted SNN both end with high accuracy, why should I care that one curve rises faster?

**Answer.** The faster-rising curve shows that the model reaches useful performance earlier in training. Final accuracy answers "How good was it at the end?" The trajectory answers "How quickly did it become good, and how stable was the path?" Those are different pieces of evidence. A model that reaches high accuracy by epoch 10 and then stabilizes has different training behavior from one that is still climbing at epoch 20, even if their final numbers are close.

A beginner confusion often comes from treating the highest endpoint as the whole result. The endpoint is only one vertical slice of the curve. The slope, the plateau, and the shaded variation also carry meaning. A steep slope means rapid improvement. A flat region means convergence or stalled improvement. A shaded band shows how repeatable the training behavior is across runs. If one model has a high mean accuracy but a broad shaded band, its result may be less predictable than a model with a slightly lower mean and tighter variation. The curve keeps accuracy tied to its metric context: accuracy is being measured at each epoch, under repeated runs, for a specific training approach.

**Question.** Does a flatter curve always mean the model has failed to learn?

**Answer.** A flatter curve means the measured training accuracy is changing slowly. If the curve becomes flat at a high value, the model has likely converged successfully. If it becomes flat at a lower value, the model has reached a limit under that training setup. For the STDP-based SNN, the earlier plateau means its timing-based adaptation improves performance but settles sooner than the surrogate-gradient approach in this accuracy comparison.

The full mental model is to read a learning curve like a motion path. The model starts at its initial accuracy, moves upward as training changes its parameters, and eventually either keeps improving or settles into a plateau. Surrogate-gradient training follows a steep path toward high accuracy and stabilizes near the top by the end of the 20-epoch window. Conversion follows a slower path, reflecting the work of making spike activity reproduce a previously continuous computation. STDP follows a gentler path shaped by local spike-timing adaptation and reaches its plateau earlier. The final score tells you where each model lands; the accuracy trajectory tells you how each learning rule travels there.
