## Accuracy-Energy-Latency Tradeoffs

The earlier accuracy-energy profiles, latency comparisons, spike measurements, and convergence curves each reveal one part of SNN behavior. A deployment decision brings those parts together. Imagine choosing an SNN for a small robot that must recognize an event quickly while operating from a limited battery. High accuracy helps the robot make the right decision, low latency helps it react in time, and low energy consumption helps it remain active longer. The training paradigm influences all three outcomes, along with spike count and convergence behavior, so the best choice depends on which constraints shape the robot's job.

Consider surrogate-gradient training. A spike is a discrete event, which creates difficulty for ordinary gradient-based optimization because the spike-generation step does not provide a smooth derivative. A surrogate gradient supplies a smooth approximation during training, allowing the network to learn through end-to-end optimization. In the evaluated results, this approach reaches about 97.8% accuracy, produces latency as low as 10 milliseconds, and consumes about 15 millijoules per inference. It also converges relatively quickly, reaching strong performance by the twentieth training epoch. These measurements form a balanced profile: accuracy remains close to conventional ANN performance, responses arrive quickly, and energy use stays moderate.

That profile suits a latency-sensitive recognition task. If a camera detects an obstacle ahead of a moving robot, a 10-millisecond response gives the control system information quickly enough to begin reacting. The energy cost of 15 millijoules per inference may fit the robot's budget when fast, accurate decisions receive the highest priority. The same choice can become less attractive in a sensor that must operate unattended for long periods, because repeated inferences accumulate energy costs over time.

STDP creates a different profile. Spike-Timing-Dependent Plasticity changes connections according to the relative timing of spikes, allowing the network to adapt through local temporal relationships. The evaluated STDP-based SNN reaches about 95.5% accuracy and uses about 5 millijoules per inference, the lowest reported energy level among the compared approaches. It also produces the lowest spike counts, while its convergence proceeds more slowly.

The relationship between spike count and energy helps explain this result. Each spike triggers communication and processing activity. A network that sends fewer spikes generally performs fewer event-driven operations, so its inference can require less energy. Think of spikes as short messages passed among neurons. A system sending only essential messages creates less communication traffic than one sending many messages across a longer interval. The STDP model's sparse activity therefore aligns with its low energy use.

Its 5-millijoule inference cost is one-third of the surrogate model's roughly 15-millijoule cost. Choosing STDP would exchange about 2.3 percentage points of accuracy-97.8% versus 95.5%-for a substantial reduction in energy, together with sparse spiking activity. An ultra-low-power unsupervised setting may favor that exchange. A remote event sensor, for example, may value long operating life and local adaptation more heavily than the final few percentage points of classification accuracy. Slower convergence also becomes acceptable when learning can unfold over time and immediate optimization is outside the deployment's main requirements.

ANN-to-SNN conversion occupies another region of the tradeoff. Conversion begins with a trained conventional neural network and translates its activations into spike-based computation. This strategy preserves competitive accuracy, which makes it appealing when an existing ANN already performs well. Converted networks commonly require longer simulation windows and higher spike counts, however. A longer window delays the moment when enough spikes have accumulated to support a decision, while a higher spike rate increases event-driven activity. The resulting profile can favor accuracy retention while placing greater demands on latency and energy.

A useful model-selection rule therefore needs several objectives. An **objective** is a measurable outcome that the deployment seeks to improve, such as accuracy, latency, energy per inference, spike count, or convergence speed. **Multi-objective model selection** evaluates these outcomes together. Rather than compressing every result into a universal ranking, it asks which profile fits a particular set of priorities.

One model would dominate another if it performed at least as well on every objective and better on at least one. The evaluated paradigms instead produce **non-dominating choices**: each offers an advantage that comes with movement along another performance axis. Surrogate-gradient training offers high accuracy, low latency, and faster convergence with moderate energy use. STDP offers the lowest energy and spike count with lower accuracy and slower convergence. Conversion offers competitive accuracy while requiring more spikes and longer simulation windows. Since each profile has a region where it is useful, the deployment requirements determine the preferred model.

**Question.** Why can't we simply choose the model with the highest accuracy?

**Answer.** Accuracy measures how often predictions are correct, while a deployed system must also produce those predictions within its time and energy limits. A model with slightly higher accuracy may consume more energy, generate more spikes, or wait through a longer simulation window before responding. For a fast robot, latency may set the binding constraint. For a battery-powered sensor, energy per inference may set it. Accuracy remains part of the decision, and its weight depends on the consequences of errors relative to the costs of delay and energy use.

Deployment priorities can be imagined as weights placed on the five measured objectives. A real-time application places greater weight on latency and accuracy, steering the decision toward the surrogate-gradient profile. An energy-constrained application places greater weight on energy and spike count, steering it toward STDP. A project that already has a strong ANN and values retained accuracy may accept the converted model's longer simulation window and heavier spiking activity. Training conditions add another consideration: faster convergence reduces the time required to reach strong performance, whereas slower convergence may fit applications where gradual unsupervised adaptation is valuable.

**Question.** Does low spike count always identify the best SNN?

**Answer.** Low spike count supports energy-efficient event-driven computation, but model quality includes the usefulness and timing of those spikes. A sparse network that misses too many relevant patterns may fall short of the required accuracy. A network that waits too long to accumulate evidence may violate a response deadline. Spike count becomes decisive when communication activity and energy dominate the deployment budget; it becomes one factor among several when accuracy or reaction speed carries greater weight.

The results form a mental map rather than a finishing order. Surrogate gradients occupy the region of high accuracy, quick responses, and faster convergence. STDP occupies the region of sparse activity and minimal energy. Conversion occupies a route from established ANN accuracy into spike-based operation, with longer windows and more spike traffic. Moving from one deployment to another changes which region is most valuable. Model selection therefore begins with the application's limits-how accurate, how fast, how energy-efficient, how sparse, and how quickly trained the system must be-and then chooses the training paradigm whose combined profile fits those limits.
