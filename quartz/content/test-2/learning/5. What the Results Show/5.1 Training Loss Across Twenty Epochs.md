## Training Loss Across Twenty Epochs

Once training loss, epochs, and convergence are familiar, the natural question becomes how to read an entire training run. Imagine watching three learners practice the same kind of task for twenty sessions. A final score tells you where each learner finished, but the sequence of scores reveals much more: who improved quickly, who advanced steadily, and who reached a point where further practice produced little change. Training curves provide that sequence for a neural network. Each epoch represents one complete pass through the training data, and the loss recorded after that epoch measures how far the model's outputs remain from the training objective.

The ordered series of these loss values forms a **loss trajectory**. Its direction shows whether optimization is making progress, its steepness shows how quickly progress occurs, and changes in its shape show whether progress continues consistently. A downward trajectory means the model is reducing its training error. A steep downward segment indicates that the learning process is finding useful adjustments rapidly, while a shallow segment indicates slower improvement. When a curve becomes nearly flat, it has reached a **plateau**: additional epochs are producing only small reductions in loss.

This makes convergence a pattern observed across time. A model with fast convergence removes a large portion of its loss during relatively few epochs. A model with slow convergence needs more epochs to achieve comparable improvement, or it may settle at a plateau before reaching the same region. The final epoch still contributes evidence, but the route to that endpoint reveals the behavior of the optimization process.

The three trajectories can be read together in the loss graph:

*Training loss across 20 epochs for converted, surrogate-gradient, and STDP-based SNN models* *(p. 10)*

Begin by following each curve from left to right. All three span the same twenty-epoch training interval, so their slopes can be compared over matching stages. The surrogate-gradient SNN has the steepest and most consistent decline. Its loss falls rapidly and continues moving downward, which indicates that its training procedure repeatedly finds adjustments that reduce the objective. The shape therefore supports two connected interpretations: convergence is relatively fast, and progress remains sustained across the run.

The converted SNN follows a more moderate trajectory. Its loss also declines, showing successful optimization over the measured epochs, but the descent is less rapid than the surrogate-gradient curve. Think of two hikers moving down the same hillside: both are traveling in the desired direction, yet the steeper path loses elevation more quickly with each step. In the same way, a greater downward slope means that each additional epoch is associated with a larger reduction in loss.

The STDP-based SNN descends more slowly and levels off earlier. The early flattening indicates that later epochs bring relatively little additional improvement. Its optimization behavior therefore differs in both rate and persistence: loss decreases gradually, and the useful change becomes small sooner. Within this twenty-epoch window, the model appears to approach a plateau while the other strategies continue to show stronger convergence.

The vertical separation between curves adds another layer of interpretation. When one trajectory lies below another at the same epoch, it has reached a lower training loss by that stage. Matching epochs is essential because loss at epoch five and loss at epoch twenty represent different amounts of training opportunity. Comparing curves along a shared vertical slice-examining all three at one selected epoch-answers the fair question: after the same number of passes through the data, how far has each strategy progressed?

A beginner may see the lowest endpoint and immediately declare a winner. Curve reading calls for a broader picture. The early slope reveals how quickly useful learning begins. The middle portion reveals whether improvement continues. The later portion reveals whether the process is still descending or has begun to plateau. A curve that drops sharply and then becomes flat has rapid early convergence followed by limited later change. A curve that descends steadily has a more consistent rate of progress. These shapes describe the dynamics of optimization even before the final losses are compared.

**Question.** How can the surrogate-gradient SNN be described as both fast-converging and stable?

**Answer.** Fast convergence comes from the steep decline in its loss trajectory: substantial loss reduction occurs across relatively few epochs. Optimization stability comes from the consistency of that decline across the training run. The curve continues in the useful downward direction rather than showing an erratic sequence of large reversals. Here, stability means that epoch-to-epoch optimization produces a coherent trend, while speed describes how rapidly the loss decreases.

The table also clarifies **optimization stability**. Stability can be examined by asking whether successive epochs usually carry the loss in a consistent direction and whether the size of the improvement changes smoothly. A steady sequence suggests that the strategy is making reliable progress under the reported training setup. A plateau appears as several later rows with only small changes. Thus, stability concerns the behavior of the sequence, while convergence rate concerns how quickly that sequence moves downward.

**Question.** Does an early plateau mean that training has fully converged?

**Answer.** An early plateau shows that the measured loss is changing very little during the remaining observed epochs. It supports the conclusion that optimization has stabilized within this run and under this training setup. Claims about behavior beyond twenty epochs would require additional observations. The careful interpretation is therefore tied to the displayed interval: the STDP-based trajectory slows and plateaus early across these twenty epochs.

Taken together, the graph and table create a practical mental model for reading training results. Treat each epoch as one moment in a learning process, connect those moments into a loss trajectory, and read that trajectory through direction, slope, consistency, and separation. The surrogate-gradient SNN moves downward fastest and most consistently, the converted SNN improves at a moderate rate, and the STDP-based SNN progresses slowly before flattening earlier. Convergence then becomes visible as a journey through twenty epochs rather than a single value at the finish.
