## Energy and Spike Activity Across SNN Strategies

Once total spike count and inference energy are available for the same model, they can be read together to reveal how much activity the model creates and how costly that activity becomes during one prediction. Imagine watching several networks solve the same kind of task. One network generates thousands of events as signals move through its layers, while another reaches its result with only a small fraction of those events. Counting the events tells us how active each network is. Measuring the energy used for the complete prediction tells us the resulting computational cost.

A **spike count** records the number of spikes generated during one inference. An **inference** is one complete pass from receiving an input to producing a prediction. Each spike can trigger communication and synaptic operations, so a network that generates many spikes generally creates more work. **Energy per inference**, measured here in millijoules, captures the energy consumed by that entire prediction. Reading these measurements side by side creates an energy-activity comparison: spike count describes the amount of event-driven activity, while energy describes the total cost associated with completing the inference.

The reported results show this relationship across an ANN CNN and three SNN strategies: ANN-to-SNN conversion, surrogate-gradient training, and STDP-based learning. The paired graph makes the overall pattern visible before the individual values are examined.

![Comparison of energy consumption and spike count per inference across ANN and SNN models](/test-2/assets/source-visuals/2510-27379v1-page-9-graph-g1-comparison-of-energy-consumption-and-spike-count.png)

*Comparison of energy consumption and spike count per inference across ANN and SNN models* *(p. 9)*

The energy values fall sharply as the comparison moves from the ANN CNN to the SNN strategies. The ANN CNN consumes approximately 200 mJ for one inference. Its computation uses continuous-valued operations rather than spike-based activity, so its cost is represented by the energy measurement rather than by an SNN-style spike count. The three SNNs consume approximately 20 mJ, 15 mJ, and 5 mJ per inference. Within the SNN group, the spike counts also decrease in the same order: about 20,000 spikes for the converted SNN, 12,000 for the surrogate-gradient SNN, and 4,000 for the STDP-based SNN.

That matching downward movement is the central activity-energy pattern. The converted SNN generates the most spikes among the SNN strategies and also uses the most energy among them. The surrogate-gradient SNN generates fewer spikes and uses less energy. The STDP-based SNN produces the sparsest activity and has the lowest energy per inference. Low-spike operation therefore aligns with low reported energy across these three strategies.

The values become easier to interpret when organized model by model.

![SNN energy efficiency summary by model, energy per inference, and spike count](/test-2/assets/source-visuals/2510-27379v1-page-9-table-t1-snn-energy-efficiency-summary-by-model-energy-p.png)

*SNN energy efficiency summary by model, energy per inference, and spike count* *(p. 9)*

The table supports a paired reading across each row. For the converted SNN, approximately 20,000 spikes accompany an energy cost of 20 mJ per inference. Conversion begins with an ANN and represents its activations through spike trains. Rate-coded propagation can require many spikes to express activation strength, which helps explain why this model retains relatively high activity. Its 20 mJ energy figure is far below the ANN CNN's approximately 200 mJ, yet it remains the most active and energy-consuming of the reported SNN strategies.

The surrogate-gradient SNN reduces activity to approximately 12,000 spikes and energy to 15 mJ per inference. Direct training allows the network to learn useful spiking patterns rather than relying entirely on a converted rate representation. In concrete terms, it uses about 8,000 fewer spikes than the converted SNN while consuming about 5 mJ less energy for each prediction. This places it between conversion and STDP: its event activity is sparser than the converted model's, and its reported energy cost is correspondingly lower.

The STDP-based SNN reaches approximately 4,000 spikes and 5 mJ per inference. Compared with the converted SNN, it generates about one-fifth as many spikes and consumes about one-quarter as much energy. Compared with the surrogate-gradient SNN, it generates about one-third as many spikes and consumes about one-third as much energy. These comparisons show what **activity-driven computational cost** looks like in practice: reducing the number of events that propagate through the network reduces the opportunities for event-triggered synaptic work, and the measured energy falls with that activity.

A useful analogy is a building in which every delivered message causes a worker to perform a task. A converted SNN sends roughly 20,000 messages during one inference, the surrogate-gradient SNN sends roughly 12,000, and the STDP-based SNN sends roughly 4,000. Fewer messages create fewer triggered tasks. Spike count plays the role of message volume, while energy per inference plays the role of the total electricity bill for completing the job. Message volume helps explain the bill, while the bill still measures the complete system cost.

**Question.** If the STDP-based SNN produces the fewest spikes, can spike count alone determine which strategy is best?

**Answer.** Spike count identifies the sparsest network activity, and the reported results connect that sparse activity with the lowest energy per inference. Model selection also depends on the required predictive performance. The STDP-based SNN achieves the strongest reported energy efficiency but has reduced accuracy relative to the other models, while the surrogate-gradient SNN offers a balance between accuracy and energy efficiency. The best strategy therefore depends on whether the main priority is minimum activity, minimum energy, or stronger predictive performance.

Spike count and energy per inference remain distinct measurements even when they move together. Two models could, in principle, generate the same number of spikes while incurring different total costs because a full inference includes the consequences of those spikes and other operations involved in completing the prediction. The present results establish an empirical alignment for the compared SNNs: 20,000 spikes pair with 20 mJ, 12,000 pair with 15 mJ, and 4,000 pair with 5 mJ. They do not establish a universal conversion rate from one spike to a fixed number of millijoules.

**Question.** Does the 5 mJ result mean an STDP-based SNN will consume exactly 5 mJ wherever it runs?

**Answer.** The 5 mJ value belongs to the reported comparison and its evaluation conditions. It supports a relative conclusion within that comparison: the STDP-based SNN has the lowest measured energy and the lowest spike count. Predicting energy under other conditions would require measurements for those conditions rather than extending this value directly.

The combined mental model is a chain from learning strategy to spike pattern, from spike pattern to network activity, and from network activity to inference cost. Conversion produces relatively dense rate-coded activity, surrogate-gradient training learns a sparser pattern, and STDP produces the sparsest activity in the reported results. As spike activity falls across those SNN strategies, energy per inference falls alongside it. Spike count reveals how busy the event-driven network becomes; energy per inference reveals the total cost of turning that activity into one completed prediction.
