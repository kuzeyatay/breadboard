## Training Accuracy Across Epochs

Once training accuracy, epochs, and convergence time are in place, the next step is to read a training run as a story of progress rather than as a single final score. Imagine training three spiking systems for the same number of passes through their training data. After each pass, you pause and ask a simple question: "What fraction of the training examples does this system now classify correctly?" That fraction is the training accuracy for that epoch. If you record it after every epoch and draw the values as a curve, the curve becomes a learning history: it shows whether a model improves quickly, improves slowly, stalls early, or keeps gaining accuracy throughout training.

A single final accuracy can hide the path taken to reach it. Two models might both end near a useful accuracy, but one may reach that level after only a few epochs while another may need many more. For a spiking neural network, that path is especially meaningful because training is already balancing several pressures: the model should learn accurate decisions, but it should also preserve the event-based advantages of spikes, such as sparse activity and low-latency responses. A learning curve lets you see whether a training method gives the network useful behavior quickly or whether the network spends many epochs making only small gains.

The horizontal axis of the curve is the epoch number. An epoch means one complete pass through the training set. The vertical axis is training accuracy, usually expressed as a percentage. A point at epoch 5 tells you how well the model performs after five passes through the data. A rising curve means the network is learning patterns that help it classify the training examples. A curve that becomes nearly flat means additional training is producing smaller changes. That flattening is how convergence appears visually: the model has not necessarily become perfect, but its training behavior has begun to stabilize.

For this comparison, the curve is especially useful because the three SNN training approaches learn in different ways. A converted SNN begins from an artificial neural network that has already learned continuous-valued activations, then translates that behavior into spikes. Its training story is shaped by how well the converted spike activity can approximate the original activation patterns. A surrogate-gradient SNN is trained directly as a spiking network while using a smooth substitute during optimization so that gradient-based learning can still adjust the model despite the hard threshold that produces spikes. An STDP-based SNN changes its connections using spike timing relationships, so learning is driven by when pre-synaptic and post-synaptic spikes occur relative to each other.

Those differences show up as different accuracy trajectories. The converted SNN can achieve competitive performance, but the translation from continuous activations into spike-based behavior may require longer simulation windows and more spike activity to approximate the original network's responses. The STDP-based SNN tends to learn more gradually because its timing-based updates are local and do not directly optimize a supervised classification objective in the same way. The surrogate-gradient SNN is positioned between these pressures: it keeps the spiking structure while using a training signal that can push the network toward accurate decisions efficiently.

Read the graph from left to right as three learning histories unfolding over the same training budget. The surrogate-gradient curve rises quickly and approaches its stable high-accuracy region by about the 20th epoch. That shape supports the claim of fast convergence: the model is not merely ending with strong accuracy, it is moving toward that accuracy within the 20-epoch window. The converted SNN curve represents a more moderate trajectory, showing that conversion can produce competitive behavior but does not display the same direct, rapid climb associated with surrogate-gradient training. The STDP-based curve rises more slowly and settles earlier at a lower level, matching the idea that timing-based unsupervised adaptation can be efficient in spike use and energy but less aggressive in supervised accuracy improvement.

The phrase "by about the 20th epoch" should be read as a convergence marker, not as a magical boundary. A marker is a point in training where the curve has revealed enough of its shape to judge the method's learning speed. If a curve is still climbing steeply at epoch 20, the model may need more training before its stable behavior is clear. If the curve is already flattening near a strong accuracy level, epoch 20 is enough to show that the method has reached usable performance quickly. In the surrogate-gradient case, the curve's rise and stabilization within this window clarify why it is described as converging quickly relative to the converted and STDP-based alternatives.

A helpful way to think about target accuracy crossing is to imagine drawing a horizontal line at the accuracy level you would consider usable for a task. The epoch where a model's curve first crosses that line tells you how long training had to run before the model became practically useful under that target. If the surrogate-gradient curve crosses that line earlier than the other curves, then it reaches usable performance sooner. If it also stays near that level afterward, then the crossing is not a brief accident; it reflects a stable training trajectory.

**Question.** If two SNNs end with similar training accuracy, why should we care which curve rises faster?

**Answer.** The faster-rising curve shows that the model reached useful performance in fewer epochs. That affects training cost, experimentation speed, and model selection. A model that approaches strong accuracy by around 20 epochs gives clearer evidence of efficient convergence than a model that needs many more passes through the data to reach the same region.

A common beginner confusion is to treat the final point of the curve as the entire result. The final point tells you the accuracy at the last measured epoch, but the curve tells you how that result was reached. A steep early rise means the training method quickly found useful internal settings. A slow rise means the model needed more repeated exposure before its predictions improved. A plateau at a lower level means the learning rule may have stabilized before reaching the same accuracy region as the stronger method. The curve is therefore a speed-and-stability picture, not only an end-score picture.

The surrogate-gradient method gains this advantage because it gives a spiking network a practical way to learn through threshold-based firing. A spike is a discrete event: a neuron either fires at a moment or it does not. That all-or-nothing behavior is useful for sparse event-based computation, but it creates a training difficulty because tiny parameter changes may not create tiny output changes at the spike threshold. Surrogate-gradient training handles this by using a smooth approximation during learning, allowing weight updates to point in useful directions while the network still operates with spikes. In the learning curve, that mechanism appears as a faster accuracy trajectory: the model can adjust toward better classifications without abandoning the spiking form.

**Question.** Does convergence by about 20 epochs mean the surrogate-gradient SNN has reached the highest possible accuracy?

**Answer.** It means the measured training trajectory has largely approached its stable region within the 20-epoch comparison. It does not prove that no further improvement is possible under any setting. It supports the narrower result that, in this comparison window, surrogate-gradient training reaches strong performance quickly relative to converted and STDP-based SNNs.

The mental model is to see each curve as a trace of how a learning rule turns repeated exposure into correct decisions. The converted SNN carries over learned behavior and adapts it into spikes, so its curve reflects the limits and benefits of that translation. The STDP-based SNN follows timing-driven adaptation, so its curve reflects gradual local learning and earlier stabilization. The surrogate-gradient SNN learns directly in the spiking setting while receiving an optimization signal strong enough to improve accuracy quickly. By the time the curves reach the 20th epoch, their shapes have already told the main result: training accuracy over time shows not just which method performs well, but which method gets there quickly enough to make convergence a practical advantage.
