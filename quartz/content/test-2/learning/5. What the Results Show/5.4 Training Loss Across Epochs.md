## Training Loss Across Epochs

After seeing how spiking neural networks can be compared by accuracy, latency, energy use, spike count, and convergence time, the training process itself deserves a slower look. Imagine watching three learners practice the same task for 20 rounds. If you only check their score at the end, you miss the shape of their learning: one learner may improve quickly and settle, another may improve steadily but more slowly, and another may make early progress and then stop improving much. A training loss curve gives that missing motion back. It shows how much error remains after each epoch, where an **epoch** means one full pass through the training data. Lower training loss means the model's predictions are becoming better aligned with the desired outputs during training.

A spiking neural network learns through changes in how spikes lead to decisions. During training, the network receives inputs, produces spike-based activity, and adjusts its internal parameters or connection strengths depending on the learning rule being used. The training loss is a single number computed after an epoch to describe how far the model's current behavior is from the target behavior. When that number falls from epoch to epoch, the model is reducing its error. When the number changes only slightly, the model is approaching a stable region. This stable behavior is called **convergence behavior**: the visible pattern of how the model moves toward a settled loss level over training time.

A useful way to read convergence is to follow the curve from left to right rather than jumping to the final point. The horizontal direction is training time measured in epochs, and the vertical direction is training loss. A steep downward segment means the model is learning quickly during those epochs. A flatter segment means the model is still training, but each extra epoch produces a smaller improvement. If the curve becomes nearly horizontal, the model has largely stabilized under that training setup. This makes convergence visible as a trajectory: the path matters because two models can end near similar losses while taking different amounts of training time to get there.

*Convergence behavior of SNN models training loss across epochs* *(p. 10)*

The graph compares three SNN variants across 20 epochs: a converted SNN, a surrogate gradient-trained SNN, and an STDP-based SNN. The surrogate gradient curve drops most sharply and most consistently, which means its training rule reduces loss faster from one epoch to the next. This fits the role of surrogate-gradient training: it gives a practical way to train spike-based models with gradient-like updates even though spikes are discrete events. The model can therefore make directed adjustments that quickly reduce the mismatch between its output and the training target. By around the 20th epoch, the curve has stabilized, so the model has reached a region where additional epochs give smaller changes in loss.

The converted SNN follows a more moderate path. A converted SNN begins from an artificial neural network-style representation and translates it into a spiking form. Its loss curve shows improvement across epochs, but the descent is less steep than the surrogate-gradient model. That shape suggests the converted model can learn or settle into useful behavior, while its training dynamics are less direct than the surrogate-gradient case. A beginner might picture this as a learner who starts with a helpful set of habits from a different format, then needs time to make those habits work in spike-based timing.

The STDP-based SNN has the slowest convergence behavior in the comparison. **STDP**, or spike-timing dependent plasticity, adjusts connections according to the timing relationship between spikes. If one neuron tends to spike before another in a relevant pattern, the connection can be strengthened or weakened depending on the timing rule. This learning style is closely tied to local spike timing, so it supports unsupervised adaptation and low spike activity, but its loss curve descends more slowly and plateaus at a higher value. The curve's shape tells a specific story: the model makes some progress, then reaches a level where the same training process produces limited additional loss reduction.

A common beginner confusion is to treat "converged" as meaning "best." In these curves, convergence means the loss has become stable under the current training rule and setup. A model can converge quickly to a low loss, or it can converge slowly to a higher loss. The distinction matters when choosing an SNN for a task. A real-time learning task benefits from a model that lowers loss rapidly and stabilizes within fewer epochs. A setting that values low energy and unsupervised adaptation may accept slower convergence if the training behavior matches the application's priorities.

**Question.** If two SNNs both become stable by the 20th epoch, how can the training loss curves still show that one learned more effectively?

**Answer.** The curves reveal both the speed and the level of learning. One model may reach a lower loss earlier and stay there, showing faster and stronger loss reduction. Another may flatten at a higher loss, showing that it stabilized while still making more training errors. The final epoch alone hides the route each model took.

The table supports the same interpretation in a more precise format. A graph helps the eye see the shape of convergence, while a table lets you inspect the epoch-by-epoch values behind that shape. Each row or entry connects a model, an epoch, and a training loss value. The **model** tells which SNN variant is being measured. The **epoch** tells when the measurement was taken during training. The **training loss** tells how much error remained at that point. Reading these three pieces together keeps the result tied to its metric context: the claim is about training loss during training, across epochs, for these SNN variants.

The tabulated values make the visual pattern auditable. For the surrogate gradient-trained SNN, the losses decrease in the steepest and most consistent way, so the table backs up the impression of fast convergence from the curve. For the converted SNN, the values decrease more moderately, matching the middle trajectory. For the STDP-based SNN, the values decline more slowly and then level off earlier at a higher loss, matching the plateau seen in the graph. The table prevents an overly vague reading of the curves because each visual slope corresponds to actual loss values at specific epochs.

Loss reduction means the difference between an earlier loss and a later loss. If a model has a high loss at the beginning and a much lower loss after several epochs, it has achieved substantial loss reduction. If the loss barely changes between later epochs, the model is gaining little from continued training. This is why epoch-by-epoch comparison is stronger than checking only the starting and ending loss. A model that reduces loss sharply during the first part of training may be valuable when training time is limited. A model that keeps improving gradually may still be useful when longer training is acceptable. A model that plateaus early at a higher loss is signaling a limit in how far that training method is pushing performance on this task.

**Question.** Why does the STDP-based curve plateau at a higher loss matter if STDP can be energy-efficient?

**Answer.** Energy efficiency and convergence behavior measure different parts of the model's usefulness. The plateau shows that the STDP-based model stops reducing training loss as much as the other models within the 20-epoch comparison. That can be acceptable for applications that prioritize low-energy unsupervised adaptation, while tasks demanding lower training error may favor the surrogate gradient-trained SNN.

The mental model is a race toward lower error, but the race has more than one meaningful feature. The downward direction shows loss reduction. The left-to-right direction shows training time in epochs. The steepness shows how quickly learning happens. The flattening shows stabilization. In this comparison, surrogate-gradient training produces the fastest and most consistent descent, converted SNNs show moderate convergence, and STDP-based SNNs settle more slowly at a higher loss. A training loss curve therefore turns convergence from a single final label into a visible learning history.
