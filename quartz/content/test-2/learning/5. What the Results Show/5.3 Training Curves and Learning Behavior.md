### Training Curves and Learning Behavior

After comparing SNN methods by their final performance, the natural next question is how each method gets there. Imagine training three students for the same recognition task. If all three reach a passing score by the end, their final scores tell only part of the story. One student may improve quickly and then settle, another may improve slowly but steadily, and another may need many practice rounds before the pattern becomes clear. Training curves give us that time-based view of learning. For spiking neural networks, this view is especially useful because different training methods shape spike behavior in different ways, so two models with similar final accuracy can still have very different learning paths.

An epoch is one complete pass through the training data. At the end of each epoch, the model has had another chance to adjust its internal parameters. A training loss curve plots the model's loss after each epoch, so the horizontal direction represents training time and the vertical direction represents how much error remains according to the training objective. Loss is a numerical penalty: lower loss means the model's current behavior better matches the target behavior used during training. A fast-falling loss curve usually means the training method is finding useful parameter updates quickly. A curve that flattens while still high means the method has reached a plateau where more epochs bring only limited improvement.

![Convergence behavior of SNN models training loss across epochs](/test-2/assets/source-visuals/2510-27379v1-page-10-graph-g1-convergence-behavior-of-snn-models-training-loss.png)

*Convergence behavior of SNN models training loss across epochs* *(p. 10)*

The loss curves show learning as a motion downward. The method whose curve drops most sharply is reducing its training penalty fastest, which signals quicker convergence. Convergence means the model is approaching a stable training state where additional epochs change the loss only slightly. In these results, surrogate-gradient training is the method that stabilizes most quickly, reaching its low-loss region by about the twentieth epoch. That behavior fits the role of surrogate gradients: they give a spiking model a usable training signal even though spikes themselves are discrete events. The model can therefore adjust its parameters through gradient-based learning while still using spike-based computation.

Converted SNNs follow a different learning story. A converted SNN begins from a trained artificial neural network and translates continuous activations into spike-based behavior. Because the method inherits much of its structure from the original network, it can reach competitive performance, but the loss behavior reflects the challenge of making spike rates approximate the original continuous activations. Its curve can improve, yet the path depends on how well the conversion preserves the original model's decision behavior across simulation time. A beginner might expect conversion to remove the need for learning behavior analysis because the model starts from a trained network. The curve shows why that expectation is incomplete: even when a model is converted from a strong starting point, its spike-based version still has to express decisions through events over time, and that expression can affect convergence.

STDP-based SNNs show a slower kind of learning. Spike-Timing Dependent Plasticity, or STDP, changes connections based on the timing relationship between spikes. When one neuron tends to fire shortly before another, the connection can be strengthened; when the timing relation is less useful, the connection can weaken. This rule is local because it depends on spike timing near a connection rather than on a global error signal sent through the whole network. Local timing-based adaptation can be efficient and biologically inspired, but in these results it converges more slowly and plateaus at a higher loss than surrogate-gradient training. The curve therefore tells us that STDP is learning, but its training objective settles less aggressively toward low loss over the same epoch range.

The table version of the same result is useful because it turns the curve into exact checkpoints. A graph helps the eye see shape: steep drop, flattening, and relative ordering. A table helps compare specific epochs: which model has lower loss at a given training stage, and how much loss remains after the same number of passes through the data.

Read the table by choosing an epoch row and comparing the loss entries across SNN models. The epoch column gives the training checkpoint, the training loss columns give the remaining penalty, and the model labels identify which training strategy produced each value. If one method has a lower loss at epoch 10 and stays lower at epoch 20, it is learning faster under that loss measure. If another method improves between the same epochs but remains higher, it is still moving in the right direction while requiring more training time or settling into a less optimized state. This is why a training loss curve only becomes meaningful beside its cost: a lower loss is more attractive when it is reached in fewer epochs, with less training effort, or with a method whose other constraints fit the task. A curve is a learning trace, and the practical question is how much progress the method buys for each additional epoch.

Loss curves and accuracy curves answer related but different questions. Loss asks, "How well is the model optimizing the training objective?" Accuracy asks, "How often does the model predict the correct class?" Accuracy over epochs is called a learning curve when it tracks task performance during training. A model can reduce loss before accuracy rises strongly, because the model may be becoming more confident on examples it already gets right or less wrong on examples it still misclassifies. Accuracy changes only when enough decision boundaries shift to turn incorrect predictions into correct ones. For that reason, the accuracy curve is the best place to see when learning becomes visible as improved task performance.

![Learning curves showing training accuracy versus epochs for converted, surrogate-gradient, and STDP-based SNNs](/test-2/assets/source-visuals/2510-27379v1-page-11-graph-g1-learning-curves-showing-training-accuracy-versus.png)

*Learning curves showing training accuracy versus epochs for converted, surrogate-gradient, and STDP-based SNNs* *(p. 11)*

The accuracy curves invite a different reading from the loss graph. Instead of looking for the fastest downward movement, look for the fastest upward movement and the earliest stable high region. Surrogate-gradient SNNs climb quickly, showing that their optimization progress turns into useful classification performance within relatively few epochs. Converted SNNs can also reach strong accuracy, but their curve reflects the dependence on spike-rate approximation: performance improves as the spike-based representation becomes reliable enough across the simulation window. STDP-based SNNs rise more gradually, which matches the slower convergence seen in the loss results. Their accuracy curve shows adaptation over time, but the improvement is less direct than the gradient-trained route.

**Question.** If surrogate-gradient training already has the fastest loss decrease, why do we still need the accuracy curve?

**Answer.** The loss curve shows how the training objective is being optimized, but the accuracy curve shows whether that optimization is producing correct classifications. A method can make the loss smaller by improving confidence or reducing penalty without immediately changing many wrong answers into right answers. The accuracy curve confirms when the training progress becomes task performance.

A common confusion is to treat the final point on the curve as the whole result. The final point answers, "Where did the method end up after this many epochs?" The curve answers, "How did the method behave along the way?" That path matters because convergence time is part of model selection. If two methods end near similar accuracy, the one that reaches useful accuracy earlier may be preferable when training time is limited. If a method improves slowly but uses a learning rule suited to local spike timing, its curve reveals that its value lies in a different learning behavior rather than in racing to the lowest loss.

**Question.** What does it mean when a curve flattens before reaching the best-looking value?

**Answer.** A flattening curve means additional epochs are producing smaller changes. For a loss curve, the model is no longer reducing its penalty quickly. For an accuracy curve, the model is gaining fewer new correct predictions per epoch. The method may have reached a stable region for its training rule, even if another method reaches a lower loss or higher accuracy.

The mental model is to read training as motion through time rather than as a single score. Loss curves show the optimization path: how rapidly each method reduces its training penalty and where that reduction settles. Accuracy curves show the performance path: how rapidly each method turns training into correct predictions. Surrogate-gradient SNNs show fast, direct improvement because the training signal guides parameters toward useful spike behavior. Converted SNNs show competitive learning shaped by how well continuous activations become spike-based responses. STDP-based SNNs show gradual timing-driven adaptation, with slower convergence and a higher loss plateau over the observed epochs. Together, the curves turn "which method is best?" into a more precise question: which learning behavior fits the amount of training time, the desired accuracy growth, and the cost of reaching convergence?
