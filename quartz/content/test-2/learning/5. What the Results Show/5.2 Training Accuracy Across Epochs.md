## Training Accuracy Across Epochs

The earlier loss trajectories showed how each training strategy reduced its errors over repeated passes through the training data. Accuracy adds a more concrete view of the same learning process: it tracks the share of training examples classified correctly after each epoch. An **epoch** is one complete pass through the training set. By following accuracy from one epoch to the next, we can see when a model begins making useful predictions, how quickly those predictions improve, and whether improvement continues or settles into a plateau.

Imagine training three spiking neural networks for the same classification task. After every epoch, each network is asked to classify the training examples. A model that gets more examples right produces a higher training accuracy. Plotting those measurements creates an **accuracy trajectory**: a curve showing how correct classification develops during training. A sharply rising curve indicates rapid learning, while a gently rising curve indicates gradual learning. When a curve becomes nearly flat, additional epochs are producing little change in the number of correct predictions.

The three trajectories reveal distinct learning behavior for surrogate-gradient, converted, and STDP-based spiking neural networks.

*Learning curves of training accuracy versus epoch for surrogate-gradient, converted, and STDP-based spiking neural networks* *(p. 11)*

The surrogate-gradient trajectory rises most quickly and maintains the strongest improvement across training. Its steep early climb means that relatively few passes through the data are needed before the model classifies many training examples correctly. As the curve approaches its later plateau, the model continues to refine its predictions, but each additional epoch contributes a smaller accuracy gain. This shape agrees with the earlier loss evidence: surrogate-gradient training produced the steepest and most consistent reduction in loss. Falling loss and rising accuracy therefore tell a coherent story of rapid convergence.

Surrogate-gradient learning achieves this behavior by providing a usable training signal for a spiking network. A spike is a discrete event, while ordinary gradient-based optimization works most naturally with smoothly changing quantities. The surrogate approach supplies an approximation that allows error information to guide learning despite the discrete spike mechanism. In practical terms, the network receives frequent guidance about how its parameters should change. The rapidly rising accuracy curve shows the result of that guidance: mistakes are corrected efficiently enough to change the predicted class for many examples early in training.

The converted SNN follows a more moderate trajectory. Its accuracy improves across epochs, showing that its behavior becomes better aligned with the classification task, yet the rise is slower than the surrogate-gradient curve. This pattern complements the converted model's loss trajectory, which showed moderate convergence. Together, the curves indicate steady progress at a lower learning speed. The converted model can reach competitive performance, but its route there requires more gradual adjustment and may rely on longer simulation windows to represent continuous activations through spike rates.

Consider two models whose accuracy eventually settles near a similar level. If one reaches that region early while the other approaches it slowly, their final performance may look comparable even though their training behavior differs substantially. The early-rising model has greater **comparative learning speed** because it extracts useful decision patterns in fewer epochs. The slower model may still become effective, but it requires more training passes before reaching a similar stage. Reading the entire trajectory therefore reveals information that a single final accuracy measurement would hide.

The STDP-based trajectory improves most slowly and levels off earlier. STDP, or spike-timing-dependent plasticity, changes connections according to the relative timing of spikes. Its gradual adaptation can discover useful temporal relationships, but the training signal is less directly tied to correcting classification errors than the signal used in surrogate-gradient training. The accuracy curve consequently rises at a gentler rate and reaches a plateau sooner. The earlier loss results reinforce this interpretation: STDP-based training reduced loss slowly and plateaued early. When both measurements flatten, the model has entered a region where further epochs produce limited improvement under the current learning strategy.

A plateau needs to be read in context. In an accuracy graph, a flat region means that the number of correctly classified examples is staying roughly stable. In a loss graph, continued movement can still occur because loss responds to the strength of predictions as well as whether the predicted class is correct. Suppose a model already classifies an example correctly but becomes more confident in that correct answer during the next epoch. Its accuracy remains unchanged because the example was correct both times, while its loss can decrease because the prediction has improved in quality. This gives loss a finer sensitivity to changes that have not yet crossed the boundary between an incorrect and a correct classification.

**Question.** Why can training loss decrease while training accuracy stays almost flat?

**Answer.** Accuracy records whether each predicted class is correct, so it changes only when an example crosses from one side of the decision boundary to the other. Loss also responds to confidence. A model can assign increasing confidence to already-correct predictions or reduce the severity of incorrect predictions without changing which class wins. Those adjustments lower loss while leaving the count of correct classifications nearly unchanged.

The reverse relationship also deserves attention. A visible rise in accuracy confirms that optimization is changing actual classification outcomes rather than merely adjusting confidence values. When surrogate-gradient training combines a steep loss decline with a rapid accuracy increase, both indicators support the conclusion that it learns quickly. When converted SNN training shows moderate movement in both curves, the evidence supports steadier convergence. When STDP-based training shows slower accuracy growth alongside an early loss plateau, the pair points toward limited progress within the training period.

**Question.** Which curve should determine whether one strategy converges faster?

**Answer.** Use the loss and accuracy trajectories together. Loss shows the continuous reduction of prediction error, while accuracy shows when those reductions become correct classifications. A strategy demonstrates faster convergence when its loss falls consistently and its accuracy rises rapidly before both begin to stabilize. Final accuracy answers where training ended; the shapes of both curves explain how efficiently the model arrived there.

The combined mental model is a learner walking downhill while passing classification boundaries. Loss describes the learner's changing height on the slope, capturing every movement toward a better solution. Accuracy counts how many boundaries have been crossed into correct decisions. Surrogate-gradient training descends quickly and crosses many boundaries early, converted training follows a steadier route, and STDP-based training advances gradually before settling. Reading both trajectories turns convergence from a final score into a process: error shrinks, decisions improve, and the speed and shape of those changes reveal how each spiking network learns.
