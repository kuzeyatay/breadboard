## Training Loss and Convergence Behavior

Training loss, epochs, and convergence now come together as a way to watch learning unfold. Imagine pausing each of three spiking neural networks after every training epoch and asking the same question: "How much error remains on the training examples?" Each answer gives one loss value. Placing those values in chronological order creates a **loss trajectory**, a record of how the model's training error changes as learning proceeds. A trajectory carries more information than a single final value because its direction, steepness, and smoothness reveal how the model reached its current state.

A useful way to read a loss trajectory is to imagine walking downhill. The vertical position represents training loss, while movement from left to right represents completed epochs. A sharply descending path means that error is being removed quickly. A shallow descent means improvement is occurring more gradually. A nearly horizontal stretch signals a plateau, where additional epochs produce little change. Small rises or irregular movements indicate variation in the learning process, while a steady descent indicates more stable progress.

The comparison spans 20 epochs for three strategies: a converted SNN, a surrogate-gradient SNN, and an STDP-based SNN. The graph makes their overall shapes visible at once.

![Training loss across 20 epochs for converted, surrogate-gradient, and STDP-based SNN models](/test-2/assets/source-visuals/2510-27379v1-page-10-graph-g1-training-loss-across-20-epochs-for-converted-su.png)

*Training loss across 20 epochs for converted, surrogate-gradient, and STDP-based SNN models* *(p. 10)*

Begin at the left side of the graph and follow each curve toward the right. All three trajectories move toward lower training loss, so each strategy learns something from repeated training. Their paths differ in slope and consistency. The surrogate-gradient curve has the steepest and most sustained decline. It covers more vertical distance over the same horizontal span, which means it removes training error more quickly per epoch. Its downward movement also continues consistently across the training period, producing the clearest evidence of rapid convergence by the twentieth epoch.

Here, **convergence speed** describes how quickly the training process approaches a low and increasingly stable loss. It depends on the whole path rather than the endpoint alone. Suppose two models finish epoch 20 at similar losses. One may have reached that region by epoch 8 and then changed only slightly, while the other may have continued descending until epoch 20. Their final values look similar, yet the earlier arrival indicates faster convergence. For this reason, the early, middle, and late portions of a curve should be read separately before forming an overall judgment.

The surrogate-gradient trajectory shows strong early improvement and continued progress afterward. The steep early slope means that its learning procedure finds effective changes quickly. As the curve later becomes less steep, the model enters a stabilization phase: each additional epoch still helps, but the remaining reductions are smaller. That shape is natural during training. Large, easily corrected errors tend to disappear early, leaving finer adjustments for later epochs.

The converted SNN follows a more moderate downward path. Its loss decreases across training, but the descent is less rapid than the surrogate-gradient trajectory. Thinking again in terms of a hill, the converted model walks down a gentler slope. It makes progress, yet it needs more epochs to cover a comparable vertical distance. Its trajectory therefore indicates moderate convergence over the 20-epoch window.

A converted SNN begins from a model transferred into a spiking form, so the observed trajectory describes how its training loss behaves after conversion within this comparison. The curve's position relative to the surrogate-gradient curve helps answer a practical question: at the same epoch, which strategy has advanced farther toward lower training error? Across the plotted span, the surrogate-gradient strategy generally occupies the stronger position because its loss falls faster and remains on a more favorable trajectory.

The STDP-based SNN descends more slowly and reaches a flatter region earlier. Its shallow slope means that each new epoch removes a smaller amount of training error. The early plateau indicates that continued training brings limited additional reduction within the observed window. This is the slowest convergence pattern among the three strategies. Its trajectory reflects the gradual behavior of STDP-based learning in this experiment, where learning is driven by relationships in spike timing rather than the direct gradient-based optimization used by the surrogate-gradient approach.

The table sharpens distinctions that may look compressed in the graph. Consecutive values in the surrogate-gradient column show the strongest and most consistent reductions. The converted SNN column shows moderate decreases, while the STDP-based column changes more slowly and settles into smaller improvements earlier. These numerical sequences confirm that the graph's slopes represent accumulated epoch-by-epoch changes rather than a visual impression created by the final points.

Training stability can also be read from these sequences. A stable trajectory progresses in a reasonably consistent direction instead of swinging unpredictably between much higher and much lower loss. The surrogate-gradient SNN combines speed with consistency: its loss declines steeply while maintaining a smooth overall direction. The converted SNN also progresses downward, though at a more moderate rate. The STDP-based SNN shows gradual change followed by an early plateau, so its defining feature is limited continued improvement rather than rapid descent.

**Question.** Why does a flatter curve near the end suggest convergence?

**Answer.** A flatter curve means that consecutive epochs produce increasingly small changes in training loss. The learning process is approaching a region where additional training makes only minor adjustments. The plateau's vertical position still matters: flattening at a relatively low loss indicates stabilization after substantial improvement, while flattening earlier and higher indicates that progress slowed before as much training error was removed.

**Question.** Can the lowest training-loss curve automatically be called the best SNN?

**Answer.** Training loss answers a focused question: how closely the model fits its training examples under the chosen loss measure. A lower trajectory supports a claim of faster or stronger optimization during training. Broader judgments require separate evidence about other goals, so this comparison supports conclusions about convergence behavior rather than every aspect of model quality.

The full 20-epoch picture therefore forms a simple mental model: follow each curve from left to right, use its slope to judge the rate of improvement, use its smoothness to judge stability, and use flattening to locate stabilization. The surrogate-gradient SNN descends fastest and most consistently, the converted SNN improves at a moderate pace, and the STDP-based SNN descends slowly before plateauing early. Together, the graph and table show learning as a process through time, where the route taken across all 20 epochs reveals how quickly and smoothly each strategy converges.
