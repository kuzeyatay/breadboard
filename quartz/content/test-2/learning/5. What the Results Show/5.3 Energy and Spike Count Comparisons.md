### Energy and Spike Count Comparisons

After comparing accuracy, latency, and convergence, the remaining question is what each inference *costs* while it is happening. Imagine a small camera on a battery-powered device watching a hallway. Most frames are almost the same: the wall stays still, the floor stays still, and only an occasional person moves through the scene. A dense artificial neural network still performs continuous-valued calculations across its layers for each inference. A spiking neural network can behave more like an event system: quiet inputs can stay quiet, and computation concentrates where spikes occur. That makes energy and spike count a practical way to see costs that an accuracy number can hide.

A spike count is the total number of spike events generated during one inference. Each spike is a discrete event passed through the network. When a neuron's accumulated signal reaches its firing threshold, it emits a spike; when it stays below threshold, it remains silent. Silence therefore reduces activity. Energy per inference measures how much energy the model uses to produce one output for one input, reported here in millijoules, or mJ. These two measurements belong together because spike-based computation spends energy mainly when activity happens. More spikes usually mean more event processing, more communication between neurons, and more accumulated cost. Fewer spikes mean a sparser computation, where fewer parts of the model are active for that inference.

The relationship is easiest to see by comparing model families side by side. The ANN/CNN baseline uses about 200 mJ per inference, which is the highest energy cost in the comparison. A converted SNN lowers energy to about 20 mJ, showing that spike-based execution can sharply reduce energy use. Yet the converted SNN still produces about 20,000 spikes per inference, because its activity is shaped by rate-coded spike propagation: information is represented through many spikes over a simulation window. Surrogate gradient SNNs reduce both energy and spike activity further, using about 15 mJ and about 12,000 spikes per inference. STDP-based SNNs sit at the lowest end of both scales, using about 5 mJ per inference and about 4,000 spikes.

The graph makes the activity-cost pattern visible. The ANN/CNN bar for energy towers over the spiking models, so the largest energy saving comes from moving away from continuous-valued dense computation. Among the spiking models, the converted SNN has a much lower energy bar than the ANN/CNN, but its spike-count bar remains high. That shape tells you that conversion reduces energy while preserving a busy spiking representation. The surrogate-gradient SNN pulls both bars downward, suggesting a more efficient learned spiking pattern. The STDP-based SNN has the shortest energy and spike-count bars together, which means its computation is the sparsest and cheapest among the compared models.

This is why accuracy alone gives an incomplete view of performance. Suppose two models classify the same input correctly. If one model needs 20,000 spikes and the other needs 4,000 spikes, they produce the same visible answer but take very different internal paths. The high-spike model spends more events to arrive there. The low-spike model reaches an answer with less activity. On a plugged-in workstation, that difference may feel small for one inference. On a sensor node, wearable device, or edge system running many inferences repeatedly, the difference accumulates into battery life, heat, and deployment feasibility.

Each row should be read as a tradeoff: the model column names the computational style, the energy efficiency column describes how favorable that style is for low-power use, and the energy consumption column gives the approximate cost per inference. The ANN/CNN entry anchors the high-energy baseline. The converted SNN entry shows a major energy drop but retains higher activity than more directly optimized spiking approaches. The surrogate-gradient SNN entry shows a balanced middle ground. The STDP-based SNN entry marks the ultra-low-energy end of the comparison.

The STDP result is especially useful for thinking about low-energy inference. Spike-Timing Dependent Plasticity, or STDP, adjusts connections based on the relative timing of spikes. In a beginner-friendly picture, neurons that participate in useful timed spike patterns strengthen their influence, while less useful activity contributes less. The resulting network can become sparse because useful computation is concentrated into fewer spike events. In the reported comparison, that sparsity appears as roughly 4,000 spikes and about 5 mJ per inference. The cost is that STDP-based SNNs are reported with slightly lower accuracy and slower convergence than the stronger accuracy-oriented methods. For an energy-constrained application, that tradeoff can be acceptable when the goal is to run reliably with very little power.

A converted SNN teaches the opposite side of the same idea. Conversion begins from an ANN-style model and translates its behavior into spikes. That can preserve competitive performance, but the resulting spiking activity may require many events because the network uses spike rates to approximate continuous activations. A rate-coded signal often needs repeated spikes to represent a value clearly across time. If a larger activation is represented by more spikes, then many neurons can remain active across the simulation window. The model saves energy compared with the ANN/CNN baseline, yet its spike count reveals internal work that the energy number alone might make too easy to overlook.

**Question.** If the converted SNN uses much less energy than the ANN/CNN, why worry about its high spike count?

**Answer.** The high spike count shows that the converted SNN is still doing a large amount of event-based work inside the inference. Its energy is much lower than the ANN/CNN baseline, but about 20,000 spikes per inference means many neuron events still need to be processed and communicated. Spike count helps reveal this hidden activity cost, especially when many inferences run on a power-limited device.

The surrogate-gradient SNN sits between these two cases. It learns spiking patterns more directly than a converted SNN, so its activity can be more efficient. Its reported energy of about 15 mJ and spike count of about 12,000 place it below the converted SNN on both measures. At the same time, surrogate-gradient training is associated with strong accuracy, close to ANN accuracy in the broader results. That makes it a useful choice when the system needs both good predictive performance and a substantial energy reduction.

Energy and spike count therefore act like two views of the same internal process. Energy per inference tells how much power the computation consumes to produce an answer. Spike count tells how much discrete activity the network used while producing it. When both are low, as in the STDP-based SNN comparison, the model is attractive for ultra-low-power settings. When energy is lower but spike count remains high, as in the converted SNN comparison, the model has gained efficiency while still carrying a heavier activity burden. A good mental model is to imagine each spike as a small paid message. Accuracy tells whether the final message is correct; spike count tells how many paid messages were sent; energy tells the total cost of sending them. Low-energy inference comes from getting the right answer with fewer useful events.
