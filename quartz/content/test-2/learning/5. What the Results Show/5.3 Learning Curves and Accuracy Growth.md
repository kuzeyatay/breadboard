## Learning Curves and Accuracy Growth

The loss curves you have just learned to read show how strongly a model's predictions disagree with its training targets. A training-accuracy curve adds a more concrete view: after each training epoch, it records the fraction of training examples the model classifies correctly. An epoch is one complete pass through the training data. If a model correctly classifies more examples as the epochs proceed, its accuracy curve rises. Reading the loss and accuracy curves together reveals whether decreasing error is producing the practical result that classification training is meant to achieve.

Imagine two models after an early training epoch. Each model classifies 80 out of 100 training examples correctly, so both have 80% training accuracy. One model makes confident mistakes on the remaining 20 examples, while the other makes uncertain mistakes that are closer to the correct class. Their accuracy is equal because accuracy counts correct decisions, yet their loss can differ because loss also responds to the strength of the model's predictions. This is why accuracy growth complements loss reduction: loss exposes changes in prediction quality that may occur before those changes alter the final class decisions, while accuracy shows when improved predictions become correct classifications.

A training-accuracy curve places the epoch number on the horizontal axis and training accuracy on the vertical axis. Its height shows the model's current classification performance on the training data. Its slope shows the rate of accuracy growth. A steep upward segment means that each additional epoch is producing many newly correct classifications. A shallow segment means that improvement is proceeding gradually. When the curve begins to flatten, additional epochs produce smaller gains, suggesting that training is approaching a stable level.

The surrogate-gradient curve rises most rapidly and approaches its stable accuracy sooner. Its early slope indicates strong accuracy growth: relatively few epochs are needed before a large share of the training examples are classified correctly. By around the twentieth epoch, its behavior has stabilized, matching the loss comparison in which surrogate-gradient training produces the steepest and most consistent loss decline. The two views therefore tell a coherent story. Prediction error falls quickly, and the number of correct classifications rises quickly.

The converted SNN follows a more moderate path. Its accuracy improves across training, but the curve approaches its later level less rapidly than the surrogate-gradient curve. This agrees with the moderate convergence seen in its loss behavior. The model is learning useful classifications, although each group of added epochs produces improvement at a slower rate. The curve's shape provides more information than its final height alone: two models can eventually approach similar training accuracy while requiring different amounts of training to get there.

The STDP-based SNN grows more slowly and levels off earlier at a lower accuracy. Its flatter trajectory means that additional epochs convert fewer remaining mistakes into correct classifications. That pattern aligns with a loss curve that declines slowly and plateaus at a higher value. Together, the two measurements indicate slower convergence and a more limited final classification fit under this training strategy. Its behavior also fits the broader tradeoff in which STDP-based SNNs achieve sparse spiking activity and low energy consumption while giving up some classification accuracy.

A useful way to compare these curves is to choose one target accuracy and ask when each model reaches it. The moment a curve reaches or passes that horizontal target is called a **threshold crossing**. Suppose the target represents the training accuracy required for a particular comparison. A model whose curve crosses it at an earlier epoch reaches that training criterion sooner. The corresponding epoch can serve as a convergence epoch, provided the same target is applied to every model.

Consistency is essential here. If one model is judged against a lower target and another against a higher target, their crossing epochs no longer measure the same achievement. A fair comparison draws one horizontal threshold across all curves and reads the crossing point for each strategy. In the displayed pattern, the fast-rising surrogate-gradient curve reaches a shared attainable target earliest, the converted SNN reaches it later, and the slowly rising STDP curve may reach it later still or remain below a sufficiently high target during the observed training period.

Threshold crossing gives convergence a practical interpretation. Loss may continue to decrease through small refinements even after accuracy has crossed the chosen target, because the model can improve the confidence or quality of predictions whose class decisions are already correct. Conversely, a loss decline accompanied by little accuracy growth means that prediction scores are changing without converting many incorrect classifications into correct ones yet. The cross-check prevents either measurement from carrying the entire judgment.

**Question.** Why can training loss decrease while the training-accuracy curve stays flat for several epochs?

**Answer.** Accuracy changes only when an example's predicted class becomes correct or incorrect. Loss can change whenever prediction scores move, even if the winning class remains the same. During a flat accuracy interval, the model may be becoming less confident in wrong answers or more confident in correct answers. Those changes reduce loss and can prepare several examples to cross their decision boundaries during later epochs, at which point the accuracy curve rises.

Training accuracy also has a specific scope. It measures classifications on the examples used during training. Test accuracy measures performance on separate examples, while inference latency measures the time required to process a sample after training. An epoch value on the graph describes training progress; it does not represent milliseconds of inference. The statement that a model crosses an accuracy threshold by an earlier epoch therefore means that it reaches the chosen training criterion in fewer passes through the data.

**Question.** If two models finish with similar training accuracy, how can their curves still show different convergence behavior?

**Answer.** Their paths can differ even when their final heights are close. A model with rapid early accuracy growth reaches a shared threshold in fewer epochs and then stabilizes. Another model may rise gradually and reach the same neighborhood only near the end of training. Comparing threshold-crossing epochs captures this difference, while the loss curves reveal whether each model's prediction error also declines smoothly and settles.

A complete convergence judgment therefore follows a connected chain. Loss tracks how prediction error changes, training accuracy tracks how many training decisions become correct, the slope of the accuracy curve reveals the pace of that improvement, and threshold crossing turns the curve into a comparable convergence epoch. When rapid loss reduction accompanies rapid accuracy growth and an early crossing of a shared target, the evidence supports fast convergence. When loss plateaus high while accuracy grows slowly and stabilizes lower, the evidence supports slower, more limited convergence. Together, the curves provide a fuller mental model of learning: internal prediction quality improves, those improvements turn into correct classifications, and the epoch axis shows how much training is required for that change to occur.
