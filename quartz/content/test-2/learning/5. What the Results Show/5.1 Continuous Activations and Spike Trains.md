## Continuous Activations and Spike Trains

The motivation is already in place, so this page develops continuous activations and spike trains from the previous concepts. Focus on the specific mechanism, metric, result, or limitation this lesson adds to the learning path.

A continuous activation is a signal whose meaning is carried by the size of a number. If a neuron has activation `0.82`, that value is the message at that moment: it may mean a feature is strongly present, or that a class is strongly supported, depending on where the neuron sits in the network. The computation is dense because many neurons communicate values at the same time, and each downstream connection may need to use those values. In the hallway example, a dense system keeps updating a large pattern of numbers even when only a small edge, shadow, or moving person has changed.

A spike train changes the signal by spreading meaning across time. A spike train is a sequence of spike events emitted by a neuron over several time steps. At each time step, the neuron either spikes or stays silent, so the local signal is binary: event or no event. The richer message comes from the pattern across time. A neuron that spikes many times in a short window may be signaling strong evidence. A neuron that spikes once very early may be signaling that it detected something quickly. A neuron that stays silent may be signaling that its feature never became strong enough to report.

Consider a sensor reading that grows brighter over five time steps. A continuous activation system might send values such as `0.10`, `0.25`, `0.50`, `0.72`, and `0.91`. The magnitude at each step carries the information. A spiking system might instead send silence, silence, spike, silence, spike. The exact values have been replaced by events. The receiving neuron reads the event pattern: when the spikes occurred, how many arrived, and which input neurons produced them. The signal has moved from "what number is being sent right now?" toward "what events happened over this interval?"

This shift is useful because many inputs do not need constant communication. Sparse signaling means only a relatively small portion of neurons fire during a given time window. A quiet neuron does not repeatedly send a numerical value just to say that nothing has crossed its reporting threshold. In the hallway, neurons tuned to empty floor texture may remain silent while a few neurons tuned to motion edges emit spikes. Computation then concentrates around the changing or strongly supported parts of the input.

The mechanism behind this can be built from a simple accumulator. A spiking neuron receives input spikes from earlier neurons. Each incoming spike pushes its internal state upward, much like drops of water filling a cup. This internal state is often called membrane potential: a running level of accumulated evidence inside the neuron. The neuron also leaks over time, so old evidence gradually fades if it is not reinforced. When the membrane potential reaches a threshold, the neuron fires a spike and resets. The threshold gives the neuron a rule for when evidence is strong enough to communicate.

A beginner confusion often comes from thinking that a spike is too simple to carry useful information because each event is only "on." The useful information does not live inside one spike as a large number. It lives in the timing, count, and source of spikes across a window. A single raindrop tells little about the weather, but a pattern of drops over seconds tells whether the rain is light, heavy, beginning, or stopping. In the same way, a spike train can represent intensity through spike count, urgency through early timing, and structure through which neurons spike together.

**Question.** If each spike is only a binary event, how can a spike train represent a value that seems continuous, such as brightness or confidence?

**Answer.** The value can be represented by the pattern of events over time. A brighter input can cause more spikes in a fixed window, earlier spikes after the input begins, or more consistent spiking across related neurons. The individual event is binary, but the train has structure: number of spikes, spacing between spikes, timing of the first spike, and coordination with other spike trains. Those temporal patterns allow a downstream neuron to recover useful graded information without requiring every neuron to send a continuous number at every step.

Spike trains also support comparison across alternatives. Imagine an output layer with one neuron representing "person," another representing "empty hallway," and another representing "open door." In a continuous activation system, the comparison might happen by looking at three numbers at one moment, such as `0.76`, `0.18`, and `0.41`. The largest value gives the strongest alternative. In a spiking system, the comparison can unfold over time. The "person" neuron may spike earlier or more often, while the others spike rarely or remain quiet. The decision can come from which output spike train shows the strongest temporal evidence.

This time-based comparison helps explain why a spike train is more than a compressed activation. A continuous activation gives a snapshot-like value. A spike train gives an event history. If two alternatives are close, their spike trains may compete across several time steps before one becomes clearer. If one alternative is obvious, its neuron may spike quickly and repeatedly, allowing the system to reach a decision with fewer steps. The signal carries both strength and temporal development.

Sparse spike trains can reduce unnecessary computation because communication is tied to events. When fewer neurons spike, fewer synaptic operations need to happen. A synaptic operation is the work of passing a signal through a connection from one neuron to another. Dense computation keeps many such operations active because continuous values are always available to be multiplied, added, and forwarded. Spike-based computation can skip many of those operations during silence. The quiet hallway produces less activity because the network has less to report.

**Question.** Does silence mean the spiking network has lost information?

**Answer.** Silence is part of the code when the neuron has a clear threshold rule. If a neuron stays silent during a time window, it indicates that its accumulated input did not reach the level needed to fire. That can mean a feature is absent, weak, or unsupported by recent input. The meaning of silence depends on the neuron's role and the time window being read, but it is still informative because the network expects communication to happen only when evidence becomes strong enough.

The mental model is a shift from continuously broadcasting measurements to reporting meaningful events. A continuous activation says, "Here is my current value." A spike train says, "Here are the moments when my evidence became strong enough to announce." Dense computation treats every step as something to fill with numbers. Sparse signaling lets time carry part of the message, so quiet periods, early spikes, repeated spikes, and competing spike patterns all become useful. A spiking neural signal is therefore best understood as a small timeline of evidence: inputs accumulate, thresholds decide when to speak, and downstream neurons interpret the rhythm of events as information.
