### Latency Comparisons Across Models

After accuracy, energy use, spike count, and convergence, latency asks a more urgent question: once an input arrives, how long does the model take to make a decision? Imagine a small robot using an event-based camera to recognize a hand gesture while moving through a room. If the robot waits too long, the correct answer arrives after the moment when it was useful. A model that is accurate but slow can miss the practical deadline, while a model that answers quickly enough can support real-time control, edge sensing, and interactive systems.

Latency is the decision time of a model, usually measured here in milliseconds per sample. One millisecond is one-thousandth of a second, so a latency of 10 ms means the model needs about one-hundredth of a second to produce its inference after receiving the input. In ordinary neural networks such as CNN-based ANNs, information is processed through continuous numerical activations. In a spiking neural network, information moves through spike events over time. That time dimension is powerful because the timing of spikes carries information, but it also creates a practical question: how much simulated time does the network need before its answer is reliable?

A converted SNN makes this question especially visible. It begins from an ANN and translates the ANN's continuous activations into spike-based behavior. To approximate a continuous activation with spikes, the converted model often relies on spike rates: stronger activation corresponds to more spikes over a time window. The model may need to observe enough spikes before it can estimate the intended activation pattern. That waiting period is the simulation window. A longer simulation window can improve the match to the original ANN-like behavior, but it also delays the final decision. In a fast-response setting, that delay can outweigh the benefit of competitive accuracy.

A simple analogy helps. Suppose one person answers by saying a number immediately, while another answers by tapping on a table and asking you to infer the number from how many taps occur in a fixed time. If you count taps for only a moment, your estimate may be noisy. If you count for longer, your estimate improves, but the answer arrives later. Converted SNNs can face a similar tradeoff when they use longer spike windows to approximate the activation rates of the ANN they came from.

The reported latency comparison places four model families side by side: ANN CNN, Converted SNN, Surrogate Gradient SNN, and STDP-based SNN. The visual comparison makes the deployment tradeoff easier to see because the bars encode decision time directly in milliseconds.

The ANN CNN sits in the higher-latency range, around 20-50 ms depending on model complexity and hardware configuration. That range can still be fast in ordinary computing terms, but in edge robotics or event-driven sensing, doubling or quadrupling the decision time can change whether a response feels immediate. The Converted SNN appears around 20 ms, which reflects the delay added by the need for a longer simulation window. The Surrogate Gradient SNN appears around 10 ms, placing it among the fastest options while retaining competitive accuracy. The STDP-based SNN can stabilize below about 15 ms under optimized conditions, though its early inference behavior can be slightly slower because its spike adaptation develops more gradually.

Surrogate-gradient training is favored when fast decisions matter most because it trains the spiking model directly for useful spike-based computation. Instead of first learning a continuous ANN and then approximating it with spike rates, the model learns through a training method that can adjust spiking behavior while still using gradient-based optimization. The word "surrogate" appears because the exact spike event is difficult to differentiate mathematically during training, so a smooth substitute is used for the learning signal. The result is a spiking network that can learn when to spike in a way that supports quick inference, rather than needing a long counting window after conversion.

Read the table as a deployment question rather than as a ranking detached from use. If an application can tolerate tens of milliseconds and values a familiar continuous architecture, an ANN CNN may remain acceptable. If an application wants a spiking representation but inherits behavior from an ANN, a converted SNN can be competitive, though the simulation-window delay must be budgeted into the response time. If an application needs low latency together with near-ANN accuracy, the surrogate-gradient SNN is the strongest fit in this comparison because it reaches roughly 10 ms while staying close to ANN accuracy. If an application prioritizes sparse activity and very low energy, STDP-based SNNs become attractive, with latency that can settle below 15 ms under optimized conditions, though their learning and early inference behavior are more gradual.

**Question.** If converted SNNs can preserve competitive accuracy, why are they less attractive for the fastest deployments?

**Answer.** A converted SNN often uses spike rates over a simulation window to approximate the continuous activations of the ANN it came from. A longer window gives the model more spike evidence, but the decision cannot be finalized until enough of that window has passed. Fast deployment rewards models that need fewer milliseconds before the output is trustworthy, so the extra waiting time can make a converted SNN less suitable even when its accuracy remains competitive.

The beginner confusion to avoid is treating "spiking" as automatically faster in every form. Speed depends on how the spikes are used. A spiking model that needs many time steps to accumulate rate information can be slower than a spiking model trained to produce useful decisions quickly. The surrogate-gradient SNN gains its advantage from direct training of spike behavior, allowing the timing and pattern of spikes to support early decisions. The converted SNN inherits a need to approximate continuous values, which can stretch inference across a longer simulation interval. The STDP-based SNN learns through spike-timing relationships and can be very efficient in spike count and energy, but its adaptation can make its early response less immediate than the directly trained surrogate-gradient approach.

**Question.** Does the lowest latency always mean the best model?

**Answer.** Lowest latency gives the fastest decision, but model choice also depends on accuracy, energy use, spike count, and how the system will be deployed. For low-latency accuracy targets, the surrogate-gradient SNN is favored because it combines roughly 10 ms inference with competitive accuracy. For ultra-low-power settings, an STDP-based SNN may be preferred even if its response is slightly slower. For applications where conversion from an existing ANN is convenient, a converted SNN can still be useful, as long as its longer simulation window fits the timing budget.

The mental model is a race between evidence and deadline. An ANN CNN computes through continuous activations and often lands in the 20-50 ms range. A converted SNN translates those activations into spike behavior and may need about 20 ms because it waits for enough spike activity across a simulation window. A surrogate-gradient SNN learns spike-based decisions directly and can reach about 10 ms, making it the preferred choice when quick response and strong accuracy must be balanced. An STDP-based SNN uses spike timing to learn sparse, efficient activity and can reach sub-15 ms latency under optimized conditions, especially when energy efficiency is the stronger priority. When the deadline is tight and accuracy still matters, the latency comparison points most clearly toward surrogate-gradient SNNs.
