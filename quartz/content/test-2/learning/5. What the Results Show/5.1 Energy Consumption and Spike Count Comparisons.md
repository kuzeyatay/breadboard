## Energy Consumption and Spike Count Comparisons

Imagine a small wearable sensor that spends all day watching for a hand gesture, a heartbeat pattern, or a change in motion. Most of the time, the signal is quiet or repetitive. A conventional artificial neural network still tends to push numerical values through many layers for every inference, even when very little has changed. That can be acceptable on a plugged-in machine, but it becomes expensive when the model lives on a tiny battery. In that setting, model quality cannot mean accuracy alone. A useful model must also ask for little energy each time it makes a decision.

Spiking Neural Networks change the accounting because their computation is event-based. A spiking neuron communicates by sending a spike, a brief discrete event, when its internal state reaches a threshold. Silence carries meaning too: if a neuron does not spike, downstream neurons do not need to process that event. Energy consumption is the amount of energy used to complete one inference, reported here in millijoules, or mJ. Spike count per inference is the number of spikes generated while the model processes one input. These two quantities belong together because spikes are the work units of a spiking system. Fewer spikes usually mean fewer event transmissions and fewer neuron updates, so a model with sparse activity can make a decision while doing less work.

The comparison across model families shows how this changes the interpretation of results. An ANN CNN uses continuous-valued operations and has the highest reported energy consumption, about 200 mJ per inference. A converted SNN begins with a trained ANN and translates its activations into spikes, often through rate coding, where a stronger activation is represented by more spikes over a simulation window. That conversion reduces energy to about 20 mJ per inference, a large drop, but the spike count remains high at roughly 20,000 spikes. The model has become spiking, yet it still needs many events to approximate the original continuous values.

The graph makes the tradeoff visible by placing energy consumption and spike count side by side for the four model types. The ANN CNN sits at the high-energy end because every inference relies on dense numerical activity. The converted SNN drops sharply in energy, but its spike bar stays comparatively tall, which fits the idea that rate-coded conversion can require many spikes to represent values accurately. The surrogate gradient SNN improves both measurements, using about 15 mJ and around 12,000 spikes, because its spiking patterns are trained more directly. The STDP-based SNN reaches the lowest point on both axes, about 5 mJ and roughly 4,000 spikes per inference. That pairing is the central result for energy-constrained use: sparse spiking and low energy appear together.

Surrogate gradient training helps explain the middle of the graph. A true spike is difficult to train with ordinary gradient descent because the firing event is abrupt: a neuron either spikes or it does not. Surrogate gradient methods use a smooth approximation during learning so the network can adjust its weights toward useful spike patterns. The result is a model that keeps many of the benefits of spiking computation while maintaining strong performance. In the energy comparison, this shows up as a clear improvement over converted SNNs: fewer spikes and lower energy per inference. A good way to read this result is that surrogate training learns when spikes are useful instead of merely translating dense ANN activity into many events.

STDP, or Spike-Timing Dependent Plasticity, approaches learning from a different direction. A connection is strengthened or weakened depending on the timing relationship between spikes: if one neuron tends to fire just before another, that timing can reinforce the connection. This local timing-based rule encourages sparse, event-driven activity. In the reported results, that sparsity appears as the smallest spike count, about 4,000 spikes per inference, and the lowest energy consumption, about 5 mJ per inference. STDP may converge more gradually and may give up some accuracy compared with the highest-performing alternatives, but its energy profile makes it attractive when the device must run for a long time on very limited power.

A beginner might wonder whether the model with the fewest spikes is automatically the best model. The answer depends on the job. Spike count is a cost signal, not a complete quality score. If two models solve the task equally well, the one with fewer spikes is usually more attractive for energy-limited hardware. If one model uses fewer spikes but misses too many important patterns, the savings may not justify the lost performance. That is why energy-efficiency results should be read in metric context: energy consumption and spike count explain the cost of inference, while accuracy and convergence explain how reliably the model learns and performs.

**Question.** If a converted SNN already lowers energy from about 200 mJ to about 20 mJ per inference, why does its high spike count still matter?

**Answer.** The energy drop shows that moving from dense continuous computation to spiking computation can be very effective. The high spike count shows that the converted model is still using many events to represent information, often because rate coding needs repeated spikes over time to approximate ANN activations. That makes the converted SNN more efficient than the ANN CNN, but less sparse than surrogate-trained or STDP-based SNNs. For a battery-powered system, the difference between about 20,000 spikes and about 4,000 spikes can strongly affect how much event traffic the hardware must handle during each inference.

The energy efficiency table condenses the same idea into a model-selection view. Instead of only asking which model is most accurate, it asks which model gives a useful balance between energy consumption and energy efficiency. That framing is especially helpful for edge AI and wearable systems, where the model may need to operate continuously without drawing much power.

The table separates the model name from the reported energy behavior, which keeps the evidence tied to the metric being interpreted. ANN CNNs occupy the high-consumption category, around 200 mJ per inference, so they represent strong conventional computation with heavy energy demand. Converted SNNs reduce that demand substantially, around 20 mJ, making them a practical bridge from ANN models into spiking systems. Surrogate gradient SNNs improve the balance further, around 15 mJ, because training directly shapes spiking behavior. STDP-based SNNs occupy the ultra-low-power end, around 5 mJ or less per inference, because their activity is highly sparse and their learning rule is local.

The phrase "energy-efficiency summary" should be read as a compact interpretation of what the measurements support. It does not declare one universal winner for every task. It shows that STDP-based SNNs are strongest when ultra-low-power operation is the priority, while surrogate gradient SNNs are compelling when the goal is to keep energy low while preserving stronger task performance. Converted SNNs are useful when an existing ANN can be reused, but their spike count reveals a remaining cost. ANN CNNs provide the baseline that makes the energy reductions visible.

**Question.** Why can STDP be attractive even if another method reaches high performance more quickly?

**Answer.** STDP is attractive when the deployment setting rewards low energy and sparse activity more than fast training behavior. Its reported spike count is the lowest, around 4,000 spikes per inference, and its energy consumption is about 5 mJ per inference. Those numbers support ultra-low-power use cases such as wearable technology and edge devices. A model that learns more slowly can still be the better choice when it spends far less energy every time it runs.

The mental model is to treat each inference as a small budget. Accuracy describes what the model gets right, but energy consumption describes how much power it spends, and spike count describes how much event activity it uses to spend that power. Converted SNNs show that spiking form alone can reduce energy, surrogate gradient SNNs show that learned spike patterns can reduce it further, and STDP-based SNNs show how sparse local timing rules can push energy and spike count to the lowest reported range. Model quality becomes a balanced judgment: the best choice is the one whose accuracy, learning behavior, energy use, and spike activity match the constraints of the device that must run it.
