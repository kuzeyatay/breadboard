## Accuracy and Energy Tradeoffs Across Models

Imagine choosing a neural model for a small device that must recognize patterns while running on a limited battery. Accuracy tells you how often the model gives the correct answer, so it is tempting to rank the models by accuracy alone and pick the highest one. Energy consumption tells a different part of the story: each inference costs some amount of electrical work, and a model that is only slightly less accurate may use far less energy each time it runs. A useful model comparison keeps both measurements visible at the same time, because the preferred model depends on what the deployment needs most.

The comparison here places four model families beside one another: a conventional ANN/CNN reference, a converted SNN, a surrogate-gradient SNN, and an STDP-based SNN. The ANN/CNN uses continuous-valued operations, meaning activity is represented by numerical activations that are processed throughout the network. The SNN models use spikes, so computation is carried by discrete events. A spike-based model can save energy because silence carries information too: when no spike occurs, there is less activity to process. The energy advantage grows when the model produces fewer spikes per inference, because fewer events usually mean less computation.

A converted SNN begins with an ANN-style model and translates its activity into spike-based behavior. This often preserves much of the accuracy of the ANN reference, but the conversion can require many spikes because information may be represented through spike rates across a simulation window. A surrogate-gradient SNN is trained directly as a spiking model while using a training method that can handle the difficulty of optimizing spike thresholds. It learns spike patterns that can stay accurate while using fewer events than a converted SNN. An STDP-based SNN uses spike-timing dependent plasticity, where learning is tied to the relative timing of spikes. In this comparison, that produces the sparsest activity and the lowest energy use, while giving up some accuracy compared with the strongest accuracy-oriented models.

The table is the safest place to begin because it asks you to read model quality and normalized energy side by side. "Normalized energy consumption" means the energy values are placed on a shared comparison scale, so the learner can compare relative energy cost across models instead of treating each number as isolated.

Read each row as a pair: "How accurate is this model?" and "How much normalized energy does it spend to get that accuracy?" The ANN/CNN row acts as a reference point for high raw accuracy and high energy use. The converted SNN row shows the effect of moving an ANN-like computation into spikes: energy falls strongly, while accuracy remains competitive. The surrogate-gradient SNN row is especially important for balanced deployment because its accuracy stays close to the ANN reference while its energy is far lower. The STDP-based SNN row shows the most aggressive energy reduction, with the tradeoff that its accuracy is lower than the surrogate-gradient and ANN-style choices.

A beginner confusion often appears here: if one model has the lowest energy, should it automatically be called best? The answer depends on the task. A battery-powered sensor that only needs reasonable recognition may prefer the STDP-based SNN because saving energy is the dominant goal. A system that must keep accuracy very close to the ANN reference may prefer the surrogate-gradient SNN because it preserves more accuracy while still cutting energy substantially. Model comparison is therefore tied to the metric being asked about. "Best for energy" and "best for accuracy" can point to different models, and a responsible comparison keeps that distinction visible.

The graph makes the same tradeoff easier to feel visually because the energy values separate sharply across the model types.

The ANN/CNN sits at the high-energy end, around 200 mJ per inference, which reflects the cost of continuous-valued activity. The converted SNN drops to about 20 mJ per inference, a large reduction, but it still produces roughly 20,000 spikes because rate-coded spike propagation can remain activity-heavy. The surrogate-gradient SNN reduces energy further to about 15 mJ and lowers spiking activity to roughly 12,000 spikes, showing that direct spiking training can improve the energy-spike pattern. The STDP-based SNN reaches about 5 mJ per inference and about 4,000 spikes, giving the clearest example of sparse event-driven computation.

Those numbers show why model quality and energy consumption do not collapse into a single ranking. If the ranking is "highest accuracy," the ANN/CNN and surrogate-gradient SNN look strongest, with surrogate-gradient SNNs reported close to ANN accuracy. If the ranking is "lowest energy," the STDP-based SNN becomes strongest. If the ranking is "balanced accuracy and energy," the surrogate-gradient SNN becomes attractive because it combines high accuracy with much lower energy than the ANN/CNN reference. The converted SNN occupies a middle position: it gains much of the energy benefit of spiking computation, but its spike count and energy remain higher than the more directly optimized spiking approaches.

The phrase "accuracy-energy tradeoff" names this exact situation. A tradeoff appears when improving or preserving one desirable property tends to cost something in another property. Here, accuracy measures prediction quality, while energy measures cost per inference. A model with slightly reduced accuracy may be valuable if its energy reduction is large enough for the application. A model with higher energy may still be valuable when accuracy requirements are strict. The tradeoff is practical rather than abstract: it decides whether a model belongs in a high-accuracy setting, a low-power setting, or a balanced edge-computing setting.

**Question.** If the STDP-based SNN uses the least energy, why would anyone choose the surrogate-gradient SNN?

**Answer.** The surrogate-gradient SNN gives a stronger balance when accuracy remains a major requirement. It uses far less energy than the ANN/CNN reference while staying close to ANN-level accuracy, whereas the STDP-based SNN pushes energy lower with a larger accuracy cost.

**Question.** What does normalized energy help me compare?

**Answer.** Normalized energy lets you compare the relative energy burden of each model on a common scale. Instead of reading each energy value alone, you can ask how much energy each model spends for its accuracy level and whether that spending fits the deployment goal.

The mental model is a two-axis choice. One axis is accuracy: how reliably the model answers correctly. The other axis is energy: how much computation the model spends per inference. The ANN/CNN reference shows high accuracy with high energy. The converted SNN shows that spike-based operation can lower energy while keeping competitive accuracy. The surrogate-gradient SNN shows a balanced spiking design, with high accuracy and much lower energy. The STDP-based SNN shows the strongest energy savings through sparse spike activity, with a lower accuracy point. Reading the comparison well means keeping both axes in view and choosing the model whose measured strengths match the deployment priority.
