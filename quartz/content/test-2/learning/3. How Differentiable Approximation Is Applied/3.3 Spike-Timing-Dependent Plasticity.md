## Spike-Timing-Dependent Plasticity

Surrogate-gradient learning gave us one route around the abruptness of a spike: during training, a smooth approximation carries an error signal backward through the network. Now imagine focusing on a single synaptic connection instead of coordinating an entire backward pass. A presynaptic neuron sends a spike across that connection, and the postsynaptic neuron produces its own spike shortly afterward. The close timing suggests that the incoming event participated in the postsynaptic response. If the order changes, or if the two events occur far apart, the relationship becomes different. Spike-Timing-Dependent Plasticity, usually shortened to **STDP**, turns that temporal relationship into a learning signal.

The word *plasticity* means that a synaptic connection can adapt. Each connection has a **weight**, a value that determines how strongly a presynaptic spike influences the receiving neuron. STDP modifies this weight using the relative timing of presynaptic and postsynaptic spikes. Let the presynaptic spike occur at time $t_{\text{pre}}$ and the postsynaptic spike occur at time $t_{\text{post}}$. Their relative timing can be written as
$$
\Delta t = t_{\text{post}} - t_{\text{pre}}
$$
Here, $\Delta t$ is the time interval between the two events, $t_{\text{pre}}$ is the presynaptic spike time, and $t_{\text{post}}$ is the postsynaptic spike time. This expression lets us determine both temporal order and separation. A positive $\Delta t$ means the presynaptic event arrived before the postsynaptic event. A negative $\Delta t$ means the postsynaptic event occurred earlier. A value near zero means the two spikes were closely paired in time.

Suppose a presynaptic neuron spikes at 12 milliseconds and the postsynaptic neuron spikes at 15 milliseconds. Then $\Delta t = 15 - 12 = 3$ milliseconds. The connection's timing rule receives a small positive interval. If the presynaptic spike instead occurs at 18 milliseconds while the postsynaptic spike remains at 15 milliseconds, then $\Delta t = 15 - 18 = -3$ milliseconds. The same two neurons have now produced the opposite order. STDP can assign these two cases different directions or strengths of adaptation because their temporal relationships differ.

The interval also carries information beyond order. Spikes separated by a short interval form a close temporal pairing, while spikes separated by a long interval provide a weaker indication that the events belong together. An STDP rule therefore uses the sign and size of $\Delta t$ to decide how a connection changes. The exact update rule can vary, but the organizing principle remains stable: nearby events produce a stronger timing-based association, and changing their order can change the direction of adaptation. Learning emerges from repeated local adjustments as many spike pairs occur.

Think of the process as a door that adjusts itself by observing arrivals on its two sides. The presynaptic spike marks activity arriving at the connection, and the postsynaptic spike marks activity in the receiving neuron. The door needs only the times of those nearby events to adjust its influence. It does not need a complete record of every neuron's activity before making that local decision. Across a network, thousands of connections can apply similar timing-sensitive rules to their own event histories.

This is **local temporal learning**. It is *local* because the information needed for an update is available around the synaptic connection: the presynaptic event, the postsynaptic event, their relative timing, and the current connection. It is *temporal* because the learning signal depends on when events occur, including their order and separation. A spike count alone would say how many events occurred during a window. Relative timing adds structure by showing which event preceded another and how closely they were paired.

Consider two observation windows that each contain one presynaptic spike and one postsynaptic spike. Both windows have identical spike counts. In the first window, the presynaptic spike occurs just before the postsynaptic spike. In the second, the postsynaptic spike occurs first and the presynaptic spike follows much later. A count-based description treats the windows alike. STDP sees distinct temporal patterns and can adapt the connection differently. Timing is therefore part of the represented information and part of the learning mechanism.

**Question.** Does STDP require a teacher to provide the correct output for every input?

**Answer.** STDP can update a connection from the relative timing of spikes available at that connection. The presynaptic and postsynaptic events themselves supply the local temporal signal. This allows learning without a backpropagated error passing through the whole network. The resulting adaptation follows temporal relationships in the observed activity, so its learning objective and behavior differ from supervised gradient-based training.

That difference clarifies how STDP follows a separate optimization route from surrogate gradients. A surrogate-gradient method smooths the spike function during the backward computation so that gradient-based optimization can train the network end to end. STDP works through direct, timing-governed changes at synaptic connections. One method organizes updates using a network-level error propagated backward; the other organizes updates using local relationships between spike events. Both train spiking systems, yet they obtain their learning signals in different ways.

Locality can support sparse activity. In the reported comparison among an ANN/CNN, a converted SNN, a surrogate-gradient SNN, and an STDP-based SNN, the STDP-based model produced about 4,000 spikes per inference and used about 5 millijoules. The surrogate-gradient SNN produced roughly 12,000 spikes and used about 15 millijoules, while the converted SNN produced about 20,000 spikes and used about 20 millijoules. These values show a progression toward fewer events and lower energy in the STDP-based result. They describe the evaluated models rather than a universal guarantee for every STDP system.

The same comparison reveals the associated tradeoffs. STDP-based SNNs converged more slowly and had slightly reduced accuracy, even while achieving the lowest measured spike count and energy consumption. Their early inference phases were also somewhat slower because spike adaptation developed gradually, although optimized conditions stabilized below 15 milliseconds. Surrogate-gradient SNNs reached competitive accuracy and responses around 10 milliseconds by using gradient-based learning of spiking patterns. Choosing a training strategy therefore depends on which combination of accuracy, convergence behavior, latency, spike count, and energy best fits the task.

**Question.** If STDP uses only nearby spike times, how can a whole network learn anything useful?

**Answer.** Each spike changes the temporal context of the connections it passes through, and each connection repeatedly adapts from its own pre- and postsynaptic events. Those small changes accumulate across many connections and many observations. The network's behavior develops from the combined effect of local temporal updates, much as a path emerges when many individual footsteps repeatedly follow related routes.

The resulting mental model begins with two discrete events and the interval between them. Their order and separation provide a local learning signal; the synaptic connection converts that signal into an adjustment of its influence; repeated adjustments shape activity across the network. STDP therefore treats time as both part of the message and part of the teacher, allowing a spiking network to learn through temporal relationships while balancing sparse operation against accuracy and convergence speed.
