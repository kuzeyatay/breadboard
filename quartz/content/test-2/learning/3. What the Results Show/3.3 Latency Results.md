### Latency Results

After comparing training methods and simulation windows, the practical question becomes immediate: when a model sees an input, how long does it take before it can safely make a decision? Imagine a small gesture-recognition device watching a hand begin to move. The useful answer arrives while the gesture is happening, because a delayed answer can miss the moment when a robot, wearable, or edge sensor needs to respond. Latency measures that waiting time. It turns model performance into a time-to-decision question: once the stimulus begins, how many milliseconds pass before the network produces the decision?

A clean way to measure this is to mark two moments on a timeline. The first moment is the **stimulus onset time**, the instant when the input begins. For an event-based vision sensor, this could be the first meaningful burst of camera events caused by movement. For an audio-like spike dataset, it could be the moment the sample starts producing temporal input. The second moment is the **decision time**, the instant when the model has accumulated enough internal evidence to choose an output class. The **latency** is the difference between those two moments:
$$
\text{latency} = t_{\text{decision}} - t_{\text{stimulus onset}}
$$
Here, $t_{\text{stimulus onset}}$ is the time when the input begins, $t_{\text{decision}}$ is the time when the model reaches its decision, and latency is the elapsed time between them, usually reported in milliseconds. If a gesture begins at $0$ ms and the model makes its decision at $10$ ms, then the latency is $10 - 0 = 10$ ms. If another model waits until $25$ ms, its latency is $25$ ms. The smaller value means the decision arrives earlier.

That simple subtraction becomes especially meaningful for spiking neural networks because an SNN unfolds over time. A spiking model does its computation through events, so the model may need only a short burst of spikes to identify the input, or it may need a longer simulation window before the spike pattern becomes clear. A **simulation window** is the amount of modeled time the network is allowed to run before reading out the answer. Longer windows give more time for spike counts or spike patterns to settle, but they also delay the decision. In low-latency deployment, the best model is the one that reaches a reliable answer with the shortest useful window.

The latency comparison puts four approaches side by side: a conventional ANN/CNN baseline, a Converted SNN, a Surrogate Gradient SNN, and an STDP-based SNN. The ANN/CNN uses ordinary dense numerical activations and typically falls in the 20-50 ms range depending on model complexity and hardware. The Converted SNN begins as an ANN and is translated into spiking form, so it often relies on spike rates over a simulation window to approximate the original ANN activations. That rate-based approximation can take time because the model needs enough spikes for the output pattern to become stable. In the comparison, Converted SNN latency is around 20 ms.

The visual pattern gives the main answer: when the decision must happen quickly, the Surrogate Gradient SNN is the low-latency choice in this comparison. Its bar sits around 10 ms, matching the reported low-latency response. The Converted SNN bar is higher, around 20 ms, because conversion often needs a longer simulation window to represent ANN-like activation magnitudes through spikes. The STDP-based SNN sits below the Converted SNN under optimized conditions, with latency below 15 ms, but its learning rule produces gradual spike adaptation, which makes its early inference phase slower than the directly trained surrogate-gradient approach. The ANN/CNN baseline remains a useful comparison because it shows that a conventional dense model can require a longer time window, commonly 20-50 ms, depending on the setup.

A beginner confusion often comes from treating "spiking" as automatically equivalent to "instant." A spike is fast as an event, but a network decision depends on how many events the model needs before the output is clear. If a model needs to count spikes across a long window, the individual spikes may be brief while the decision still arrives later. The Surrogate Gradient SNN performs well here because training shapes the spiking activity directly toward useful early decisions. Instead of waiting for a long rate estimate to emerge, the model can learn patterns that become informative within a short time span.

A concrete way to picture the difference is to imagine two students recognizing a song from a few notes. One student needs to hear a long phrase and count repeated patterns before answering. Another has practiced recognizing the song from the opening rhythm and can answer after a short burst. Converted SNNs resemble the first student when they need enough spikes to approximate the original ANN's activation levels. Surrogate Gradient SNNs resemble the second student because direct training can make early spike timing carry decision-relevant information.

**Question.** If a model sees an input at $0$ ms and decides at $10$ ms, what does the 10 ms latency mean?

**Answer.** It means the model needed 10 milliseconds after stimulus onset to reach its decision. The latency is computed as $t_{\text{decision}} - t_{\text{stimulus onset}}$, so $10\text{ ms} - 0\text{ ms} = 10\text{ ms}$. In a low-latency setting, that value is attractive because the answer arrives quickly enough for time-sensitive use.

**Question.** Why can a Converted SNN be slower than a Surrogate Gradient SNN even though both use spikes?

**Answer.** A Converted SNN often uses spike rates across a simulation window to approximate the continuous activations of the ANN it came from. That approximation can require more time before the output is stable. A Surrogate Gradient SNN is trained directly as a spiking model, so it can learn spike patterns that support earlier decisions, reaching about 10 ms latency in the comparison.

The mental model is a race between evidence and time. Latency asks how soon the network has enough evidence to decide after the input begins. ANNs process dense values and can require tens of milliseconds. Converted SNNs gain a spiking representation but may spend extra time building a reliable rate-based signal. STDP-based SNNs can become quick, especially under optimized conditions, while their gradual adaptation gives them a slightly slower early response. Surrogate Gradient SNNs are the strongest low-latency option here because their training makes short spiking windows useful for decision-making, producing responses as low as 10 ms while still preserving competitive performance.
