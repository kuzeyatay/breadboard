## Energy and Spike Count Results

Imagine a small vision model running on a battery-powered device at the edge of a field. Most of the time, the scene changes only a little: the sky stays still, the soil stays still, and only a leaf or insect moves. A conventional artificial neural network still pushes numerical activations through many layers for every input, even when most of the useful signal is quiet. A spiking neural network gives us a different way to count work. It asks: how many neurons actually fired, how many synaptic operations did those firings cause, and how much energy did one full decision require?

That last phrase, **energy per inference**, means the energy used to process one input and produce one output. If the input is one image, then energy per inference is the energy cost of classifying that image once. Measuring energy this way is useful because it turns a vague claim like "this model is efficient" into a direct cost: one decision costs about this many millijoules. A **millijoule**, abbreviated **mJ**, is one-thousandth of a joule, so a model using 5 mJ per inference uses much less energy per decision than one using 200 mJ per inference.

Spiking models also need a second cost measure: **spike count per inference**. A spike is a discrete firing event produced by a neuron at a particular moment. A **neuron** is one computational unit in the network, and a **timestep** is one small moment in the simulated time window during which the network can update and possibly fire. If we want to know how active the whole network was during one inference, we count spikes across all neurons and all timesteps:
$$
S_{\text{total}}=\sum_i\sum_t s_i(t)
$$
Here, $S_{\text{total}}$ is the total spike count for one inference, $i$ indexes the neurons, $t$ indexes the timesteps, and $s_i(t)$ is the spike indicator for neuron $i$ at timestep $t$. The value of $s_i(t)$ is 1 when that neuron fires at that timestep and 0 when it stays silent. The formula simply adds up every firing event. If 1,000 neurons are observed over 20 timesteps, there are 20,000 possible neuron-timestep slots; if only 4,000 of those slots contain spikes, then the network was active in a sparse, event-driven way.

That spike total becomes meaningful because spikes trigger work. When a neuron fires, its spike travels through connections to other neurons. Each connection that receives and processes the event is a **synaptic operation**. The total energy of a spiking network can therefore be interpreted through two linked costs: the cost of producing or handling spikes, and the cost of the synaptic operations caused by those spikes. A compact way to express that idea is:
$$
E_{\text{SNN}} = E_{\text{spike}} \cdot S_{\text{total}} + E_{\text{synop}} \cdot N_{\text{synop}}
$$
Here, $E_{\text{SNN}}$ is the total SNN energy for one inference, $E_{\text{spike}}$ is the energy cost associated with one spike event, $S_{\text{total}}$ is the total number of spikes, $E_{\text{synop}}$ is the energy cost of one synaptic operation, and $N_{\text{synop}}$ is the number of synaptic operations performed. The formula lets you compute the energy cost of a decision by combining how often neurons fire with how much downstream connection-work those firings create. A quiet network saves energy twice: it produces fewer events, and it creates fewer event-driven operations.

A beginner confusion often comes from treating spike count as only a biological detail. In these results, spike count is a practical engineering measurement. Fewer spikes mean fewer moments where the hardware or simulator has to do work. A model with 20,000 spikes per inference is sending five times as many events as a model with 4,000 spikes per inference, so the lower-spike model has a clearer path to lower energy when the synaptic work per event is comparable.

The comparison becomes concrete when the models are placed side by side.

The table compares four cases: an ANN, a Converted SNN, a Surrogate Gradient SNN, and an STDP-based SNN. The ANN sits at about **200 mJ per inference**, which is the largest energy cost in the comparison. Its computation relies on continuous-valued operations, so the cost is tied to dense numerical processing rather than sparse spike events. The Converted SNN drops to about **20 mJ per inference**, a large reduction, but it still produces roughly **20,000 spikes** because conversion often represents values through firing rates over time. The Surrogate Gradient SNN lowers the energy further to about **15 mJ per inference** with about **12,000 spikes**, showing that direct training of spiking patterns can reduce activity. The STDP-based SNN reaches the lowest reported cost, about **5 mJ per inference**, with about **4,000 spikes**, making it the sparsest and lowest-energy model in this comparison.

The pattern is easier to see visually because energy and spike count move together across the models.

*Comparison of energy consumption and spike count per inference across ANN and SNN models* *(p. 9)*

The graph shows a steep drop from the ANN to the spiking models, then a further decline as the SNN training method produces sparser activity. The ANN appears at the high-energy end, around 200 mJ per inference. The Converted SNN is much lower in energy, around 20 mJ, but its spike count remains relatively high. The Surrogate Gradient SNN moves both measures downward, reaching around 15 mJ and roughly 12,000 spikes. The STDP-based SNN sits at the lowest point, around 5 mJ and roughly 4,000 spikes. The shape of this comparison teaches the activity-cost link: as the number of spikes per inference falls, the energy per inference falls too.

**Question.** If the Converted SNN already uses much less energy than the ANN, why does its spike count still matter?

**Answer.** Its lower energy shows the benefit of switching from dense continuous computation to spike-based processing, but its roughly 20,000 spikes still create many event-driven operations. Spike count matters because each spike can trigger synaptic work. A Converted SNN can be much cheaper than an ANN while still leaving room for additional savings if its activity becomes sparser.

The training method shapes the activity pattern. ANN-to-SNN conversion starts from a trained artificial network and translates its behavior into spikes, often using rate-coded spike propagation. Rate coding represents stronger values through more frequent firing, so it can require many spikes across a simulation window. Surrogate gradient training adjusts a spiking network more directly, allowing it to learn useful spike patterns with fewer events. STDP, or spike-timing dependent plasticity, changes connections based on the timing relationship between spikes. In this comparison, the STDP-based model produces the fewest spikes and reaches the lowest energy, although this lower activity is associated with a tradeoff in accuracy compared with the strongest ANN-style accuracy results.

Energy per inference and spike count per inference therefore answer different parts of the same cost question. Energy per inference gives the final bill for one decision. Spike count explains a major reason that bill rises or falls. A model that fires often behaves like a device that keeps turning on many tiny switches; a model that fires sparsely leaves most switches untouched most of the time. The reported **5 mJ per inference** for the STDP-based SNN is meaningful because it is paired with the smallest spike count, about **4,000 spikes**, so the low energy is connected to a visible reduction in event activity rather than appearing as an isolated number.

**Question.** Does the lowest spike count automatically mean the best model?

**Answer.** The lowest spike count means the model is very efficient in activity and energy, but model choice also depends on the task goal. If the device has an extremely tight energy budget, the STDP-based SNN is attractive because it reaches about 5 mJ per inference with sparse firing. If the priority is accuracy close to an ANN while still saving energy, a surrogate-trained SNN may be preferable because it balances lower energy with stronger performance.

The mental model is a chain: one inference unfolds over timesteps; neurons either stay silent or emit spikes; spikes create synaptic operations; those operations consume energy; the total energy per inference is the cost of the decision. The results show that SNN efficiency becomes visible when we measure both the final energy bill and the activity that produced it. A low-energy spiking network is therefore a network that makes useful decisions while keeping most neurons silent for most of the inference window.
