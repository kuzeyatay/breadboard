## Accuracy and Energy Results

After learning how SNNs can be trained by conversion, surrogate gradients, or STDP, the results need to be read as a choice between two pressures at once. Imagine a small vision device classifying images from a battery-powered sensor. If it guesses correctly almost every time but spends a large amount of energy on each image, it may drain the battery too quickly for the job. If it uses very little energy but makes too many mistakes, it may fail at the task. Accuracy and energy therefore form two axes of judgment: one asks, "How often is the model right?" and the other asks, "How much does each decision cost?"

Accuracy begins with counting outcomes. A classifier receives an input, such as an MNIST digit or a CIFAR-10 image, and produces a label. When the label matches the true class, that result is a correct prediction. When many examples are tested, accuracy measures the fraction of predictions that were correct:
$$
\text{Accuracy} = \frac{\text{number of correct predictions}}{\text{total number of predictions}} \times 100\%
$$
Here, "correct predictions" means the test examples whose predicted labels match their true labels, "total predictions" means all test examples evaluated, and "accuracy" is the resulting percentage. If a model classifies 970 out of 1000 digit images correctly, its accuracy is $970/1000 \times 100\% = 97\%$. That number is easy to understand because it directly describes reliability on the test set.

MNIST and CIFAR-10 make accuracy mean slightly different levels of difficulty. MNIST contains handwritten digits, so the visual patterns are relatively simple: strokes, curves, and digit shapes. CIFAR-10 contains small natural images from ten object classes, so the model must handle color, background variation, texture, and more complex shapes. A model that performs very well on MNIST may lose more accuracy on CIFAR-10 because the second task asks for richer visual discrimination. When the results move from MNIST-scale classification to CIFAR-10-scale classification, the accuracy gap grows: ANNs reach about 92% on CIFAR-10, while direct SNN approaches can fall around 85%. That gap tells us that the harder dataset exposes limits in how some spiking models represent and learn complex image features.

The table should be read row by row and column by column at the same time. The MNIST accuracy column shows how each model handles the simpler digit task, while the CIFAR-10 accuracy column shows how well that same approach scales to a harder image task. The normalized energy consumption column adds the cost of producing those predictions. The ANN row represents the conventional dense model: it tends to dominate raw accuracy, especially on CIFAR-10, because it uses continuous-valued operations throughout the network. The converted SNN row shows what happens when an ANN is translated into a spiking form: accuracy can remain competitive, but energy use stays higher than more directly optimized spiking approaches because rate-coded spike propagation can require many spikes. The surrogate gradient SNN row sits in the balanced region: it closely approaches ANN accuracy, often within about 1-2%, while using far less energy. The STDP-based SNN row represents the strongest energy-saving behavior, with the lowest spike counts and lowest energy consumption, while giving up some accuracy compared with ANN and surrogate-trained models.

Energy results become meaningful when tied to the cost of one inference. An inference is one completed prediction: the model receives one input and produces one label. In dense ANNs, each inference involves many continuous numerical operations. In SNNs, computation is event-driven: spikes occur only when activity crosses a threshold, so fewer spikes usually mean fewer operations and less energy. The reported comparison gives a concrete scale. The ANN consumes about 200 mJ per inference. The converted SNN lowers this to about 20 mJ, the surrogate gradient SNN to about 15 mJ, and the STDP-based SNN to about 5 mJ. These numbers show why energy cannot be treated as a decorative metric beside accuracy. A model that gives up a small amount of accuracy may reduce energy by an order of magnitude.

A compact way to connect the two axes is normalized energy efficiency. The idea is to ask how much useful classification performance is obtained for the energy spent. One simple comparison uses
$$
\text{Normalized energy efficiency} = \frac{\text{accuracy}}{\text{energy consumption}}
$$
In this expression, "accuracy" is the percentage of correct predictions, "energy consumption" is the energy used per inference, and "normalized energy efficiency" compares performance gained per unit of energy. The formula lets you compare models whose accuracy and energy move in different directions. If two models have similar accuracy but one uses much less energy, the lower-energy model has the better efficiency. If one model is much more accurate but also consumes much more energy, the best choice depends on the deployment goal.

For example, suppose one model reaches 98% accuracy while using 200 mJ per inference, and another reaches 96% accuracy while using 15 mJ per inference. The first model wins if the only question is raw correctness. The second model becomes attractive when the device must run continuously, respond under a power budget, or operate at the edge without large cooling and power resources. The difference between 98% and 96% may be acceptable in a low-power sensor, while the difference between 200 mJ and 15 mJ may determine whether the system can be deployed at all.

The graph makes the two-axis reading visible. The ANN occupies the high-accuracy, high-energy region: it is strong when the task rewards maximum classification performance and energy is plentiful. The converted SNN sharply reduces energy compared with the ANN, but its spike count remains relatively high, around 20,000 spikes per inference, because rate-coded activity still produces many events. The surrogate gradient SNN improves the balance further, reaching about 15 mJ per inference and roughly 12,000 spikes while staying close to ANN accuracy. The STDP-based SNN reaches the lowest energy region, around 5 mJ per inference with about 4,000 spikes, which makes it well suited to energy-constrained settings where the lowest power draw matters more than squeezing out the final points of accuracy.

**Question.** If the ANN has the highest accuracy, why would anyone choose a surrogate gradient SNN or an STDP-based SNN?

**Answer.** A deployment target may value energy savings as much as raw correctness. A surrogate gradient SNN can stay close to ANN accuracy while using far less energy, making it useful when both reliability and efficiency matter. An STDP-based SNN uses even less energy and produces fewer spikes, making it attractive for ultra-low-power settings where a modest accuracy reduction is acceptable.

A common beginner confusion is to treat the lowest energy number as automatically best. The better mental picture is a map with accuracy on one axis and energy consumption on the other. Moving toward higher accuracy improves prediction quality; moving toward lower energy improves deployability on constrained hardware. Different training methods land in different regions of that map. ANN models cluster near high accuracy and high energy. Converted SNNs preserve much of the ANN behavior while reducing energy. Surrogate gradient SNNs form a practical middle region, retaining strong accuracy with large energy savings. STDP-based SNNs occupy the ultra-low-energy region, where sparse spikes reduce cost most aggressively.

**Question.** How should MNIST and CIFAR-10 accuracy be compared when judging these models?

**Answer.** MNIST accuracy shows performance on a simpler digit-recognition task, while CIFAR-10 accuracy tests the model on a harder image-recognition task. A model that stays strong on CIFAR-10 demonstrates better scalability to complex visual patterns. The energy column must be read beside both accuracy columns, because a model can look excellent on accuracy alone while being costly per inference.

The accuracy-energy result is therefore a decision rule rather than a single winner. Accuracy tells how often the model is right, energy consumption tells how expensive each prediction is, and normalized energy efficiency links the two into a performance-per-cost view. Once those quantities are read together, the models fall into roles: ANN for maximum raw accuracy, surrogate gradient SNN for strong accuracy with major energy savings, converted SNN for ANN-like behavior with reduced but still substantial spiking activity, and STDP-based SNN for the sparsest, lowest-energy operation. A useful interpretation keeps both axes in mind at every moment: a prediction has value because it is correct, and it has a cost because the hardware must spend energy to produce it.
