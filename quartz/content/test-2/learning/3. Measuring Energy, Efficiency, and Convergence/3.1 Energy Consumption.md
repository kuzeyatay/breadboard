### Energy Consumption

Once spike count and synaptic operations are measurable, energy becomes a quantity you can build from counts instead of a vague claim about a model being "efficient." Imagine a small vision sensor watching a hallway. Most of the time, the scene barely changes: the wall stays still, the floor stays still, and only an occasional person moves through the frame. A dense artificial network still pushes continuous numerical activations through many layers for each inference, which means large blocks of computation happen even when the useful signal is sparse. A spiking network treats silence as part of the representation. When a neuron has enough incoming evidence, it emits a spike; when it has no reason to signal, it stays quiet. Energy measurement begins with that simple observation: fewer events usually mean fewer operations, and fewer operations usually mean less energy per inference.

An **inference** is one complete use of the model to produce an output, such as classifying one input image or recognizing one sensor pattern. During that inference, energy is spent in two closely related ways. The first cost comes from spikes themselves. A **spike** is a discrete event, so spike energy is the energy associated with generating and communicating those events. The second cost comes from the downstream work triggered by spikes. A **synaptic operation** is the update or accumulation that happens when a spike travels across a connection and affects another neuron. If one spike fans out to many connected neurons, that one event can cause many synaptic operations. Energy therefore depends on both how many spikes occur and how much synaptic work those spikes cause.

The measurement can be expressed as a direct accounting rule:
$$
E_{\text{total}} = E_{\text{spike}} + E_{\text{synop}}
$$
Here, $E_{\text{total}}$ is the total energy consumed by one inference. $E_{\text{spike}}$ is the energy spent on spike events during that inference. $E_{\text{synop}}$ is the energy spent on synaptic operations during that inference. The formula lets you compute total energy by adding the event cost and the connection-processing cost. A more operational way to read the same idea is: count the spikes, attach an energy cost to each spike; count the synaptic operations, attach an energy cost to each synaptic operation; then add the two contributions.

For example, suppose an inference produces relatively few spikes, and each spike activates only a modest number of synaptic connections. Both terms in the formula stay small, so total energy stays small. If the spike count rises while the energy per spike and synaptic cost stay the same, $E_{\text{spike}}$ rises immediately. If the number of synaptic operations rises because each spike triggers more downstream activity, $E_{\text{synop}}$ rises even if the spike count itself stays fixed. This is why total energy moves only when its inputs move: spike count, energy per spike, synaptic operation count, or energy per synaptic operation. The formula gives energy a concrete structure.

A beginner often imagines that energy is determined only by the number of neurons in the model. A better picture is to think about a building with many rooms and motion-sensitive lights. The building may be large, but the electricity used at a particular moment depends on which lights turn on and how long they stay active. In a spiking network, the "lights" are event-triggered computations. A quiet network with many inactive neurons can use little energy during an inference because little activity is being generated and propagated. A smaller network with constant dense activity can still spend more energy if many operations fire every time.

The comparison across model types shows how this accounting appears in measured results. The artificial neural network case has high energy consumption because continuous-valued operations remain active throughout inference. Converted SNNs lower energy substantially by using spikes, although their spike count can remain high because rate-coded propagation may require many spikes to represent values over time. Surrogate Gradient SNNs reduce both energy and spike count further by learning spiking patterns directly. STDP-based SNNs show the lowest energy and sparsest spike activity in the comparison, which fits the formula: fewer spikes and fewer triggered operations reduce the total.

**Question.** If a spiking model uses fewer spikes, does its total energy always decrease?

**Answer.** Fewer spikes push energy downward when the energy per spike and synaptic operation behavior stay similar. Total energy includes both spike energy and synaptic operation energy, so a model that emits fewer spikes but causes each spike to trigger many costly synaptic operations may save less energy than spike count alone suggests. The safest interpretation is that spike count is a major driver of energy, while total energy is the sum of event cost and synaptic-operation cost.

Energy is measured in **millijoules per inference** so that different models can be compared on one completed task. A millijoule is a small unit of energy, and "per inference" ties that energy to one model decision. This prevents a misleading comparison between a model that spends energy briefly and a model that spends energy over a longer inference window. In this unit, saying that a model uses 5 mJ per inference means one full input-to-output pass costs about 5 millijoules. Saying another model uses 200 mJ per inference means the same kind of completed decision costs far more energy.

The formula also explains why energy, spike count, and synaptic operations belong together in evaluation. Spike count tells you how many discrete events the network generated. Synaptic operation count tells you how much connection-level work those events caused. Energy per event translates those counts into a physical cost. Total energy gathers the costs into one value that can be compared across model families. When the activity becomes sparse, the event term falls. When fewer spikes propagate through connections, the synaptic-operation term can fall too. When both fall together, millijoules per inference can drop sharply.

**Question.** Why compare energy per inference instead of total energy over a whole experiment?

**Answer.** Energy per inference compares the cost of one completed decision. A whole experiment may contain different numbers of inputs, training steps, or evaluation runs, so its total energy can mix model efficiency with workload size. Millijoules per inference keeps the unit focused: how much energy does this model spend to produce one output?

A useful mental model is to treat a spiking inference as a chain of counted events. An input causes some neurons to reach threshold. Those neurons emit spikes. Each spike may trigger synaptic operations in connected neurons. Each spike and each operation has an energy cost. Add the spike cost and the synaptic-operation cost, and you get total energy for the inference. The model becomes energy-efficient when useful information travels through a small number of well-timed events instead of through continuous activity everywhere.
