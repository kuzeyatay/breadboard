After measuring accuracy as "how often the model is right" and energy consumption as "how much work the model spends to make one inference," the natural question is how to compare them at the same time. Imagine two models running on a small edge device that must classify sensor readings all day from a limited battery. One model is slightly more accurate but uses much more energy each time it makes a prediction. Another model misses a few more cases but runs for far longer before the battery drains. Looking at accuracy alone favors the first model. Looking at energy alone favors the second. A practical choice needs a single way to ask: how much correctness do we get for each unit of energy we spend?

That question leads to **normalized energy efficiency**, a ratio that connects accuracy to energy cost. A ratio is useful whenever one quantity should be judged relative to another. If a car travels 300 miles using 10 gallons of fuel, miles per gallon tells us how much distance we get per gallon. In the same spirit, normalized energy efficiency tells us how much predictive success we get per unit of energy. The metric is built from two measurable counts: the model's accuracy and the energy consumed per inference.

The formula is:
$$
\text{Normalized Energy Efficiency} = \frac{\text{Accuracy}}{\text{Energy Consumption}}
$$
Here, **accuracy** means the fraction or percentage of predictions the model gets correct. **Energy consumption** means the energy used to make one inference, commonly reported in millijoules per inference when comparing these models. **Normalized energy efficiency** means the amount of accuracy obtained for each unit of energy consumed. The formula lets us compute an accuracy-per-energy score, so a larger value means the model is getting more useful prediction performance from the energy it spends.

A small worked interpretation makes the ratio concrete. Suppose one model reaches 98% accuracy while consuming 200 mJ per inference. Its efficiency score is $98 / 200 = 0.49$ accuracy-percent per mJ if accuracy is written as a percentage. Suppose another model reaches 95% accuracy while consuming 15 mJ per inference. Its score is $95 / 15 \approx 6.33$ accuracy-percent per mJ. The first model is more accurate in raw terms, but the second gives far more accuracy for each millijoule. The ratio changes the comparison from "Which model is most correct?" to "Which model buys correctness most efficiently?"

This is especially useful for spiking neural networks because their energy behavior comes from how they communicate. A conventional artificial neural network passes continuous numerical activations through layers, so computation tends to happen broadly across the network during inference. A spiking neural network uses discrete spike events, so silence can mean no work needs to be done at that moment. When fewer spikes are produced, fewer event-driven computations are needed. Energy consumption then becomes tied to activity: dense activity costs more, sparse activity costs less. Normalized energy efficiency gives that energy advantage a place in the same comparison as accuracy.

Reading the table through the formula prevents a common beginner confusion. A learner may see the highest accuracy number and assume that row is automatically the best model. That is only true when energy cost is irrelevant. If the model runs in a battery-powered sensor, an embedded robot, or another energy-constrained setting, the best choice may be the model that gives nearly as much accuracy with far less energy. Normalized energy efficiency turns that practical constraint into a measurable score.

**Question.** If one model has lower accuracy than another, can it still have higher normalized energy efficiency?

**Answer.** Yes. Since normalized energy efficiency is accuracy divided by energy consumption, the score can rise when energy drops more sharply than accuracy. For example, a model with 95% accuracy at 15 mJ per inference can be more efficient than a model with 98% accuracy at 200 mJ per inference, because the second model spends much more energy for each unit of accuracy it gains.

The ratio also explains when normalized energy efficiency can change. It moves only when one of its inputs moves: accuracy or energy consumption. If accuracy improves while energy stays fixed, the score increases because the model gets more correct predictions for the same cost. If energy decreases while accuracy stays fixed, the score also increases because the same correctness is achieved more cheaply. If accuracy improves but energy rises too, the result depends on which change is stronger. A small accuracy gain paired with a large energy increase can lower the score, while a modest accuracy loss paired with a large energy reduction can raise it.

This makes normalized energy efficiency a compact way to reason about the accuracy-per-energy tradeoff. It does not replace accuracy, because some applications require a minimum correctness level. It also does not replace energy consumption, because some devices have strict power limits. It combines them after each has been measured, giving a single score that asks whether the model's correctness is worth its cost.

**Question.** Should normalized energy efficiency be used by itself to choose the final model?

**Answer.** It is best used as a comparison score after checking the application's minimum requirements. A very efficient model may still be unsuitable if its accuracy falls below what the task needs. A very accurate model may be unsuitable if its energy demand exceeds the device's budget. The ratio helps compare acceptable candidates by showing which one gives the strongest accuracy return for the energy spent.

A good mental model is to treat each inference as a purchase. Energy is the price paid. Accuracy is the useful value received. Spiking models can lower the price because they compute through sparse events rather than constant dense activity. Normalized energy efficiency asks how much useful predictive value each model buys with its energy budget. Once accuracy and energy have been measured, the ratio turns separate numbers into a practical judgment about balance.
