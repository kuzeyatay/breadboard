### The Leaky Integrate-and-Fire Neuron

Imagine a stream of spike events arriving at a neuron one time step after another. Some moments are quiet, some moments contain a few weak spikes, and some moments contain a burst of activity from many connected neurons. The neuron needs a way to decide when this incoming activity has become strong enough to pass a message forward. A threshold gives the decision boundary, but the neuron still needs an internal quantity that can rise toward that boundary over time. That internal quantity is the **membrane potential**: a running level of stored electrical pressure that changes as input arrives.

A leaky integrate-and-fire neuron, often shortened to **LIF neuron**, is a simple model of this process. It turns incoming spike activity into outgoing spike events by repeating four actions: it accumulates input, loses some stored potential over time, fires when the stored potential crosses a threshold, and resets after firing. Each part has a clear job. **Integration** means that incoming activity adds to the membrane potential. **Leak** means that the membrane potential gradually fades when input is weak or absent. The **firing threshold** is the level the membrane potential must reach before the neuron emits a spike. **Reset** means that after a spike, the membrane potential is brought back down so the neuron can begin accumulating evidence again.

Start with integration. Suppose a neuron receives a spike from a connected neuron at a particular time step. That incoming spike contributes some amount of input current, which nudges the membrane potential upward. If several connected neurons send spikes close together, their effects can combine. The neuron behaves like a small cup being filled by drops of water: one drop may barely change the level, but many drops arriving close together can raise the level substantially. In an SNN, this gives time a practical role. Spikes that arrive close together can cooperate because their contributions overlap inside the membrane potential.

Leak prevents the neuron from treating every old input as equally fresh. If the neuron receives a small input and then nothing else happens for a while, the membrane potential drifts back downward. The cup analogy becomes a cup with a tiny hole in the bottom: drops still raise the water level, but the level also drains between drops. This leak makes the neuron sensitive to recent activity. A scattered pattern of weak spikes may never build enough potential to fire, while a concentrated burst can reach the threshold quickly.

The firing threshold turns the gradual internal buildup into a discrete event. While the membrane potential remains below threshold, the neuron keeps updating its internal state. When the membrane potential reaches the threshold, the neuron emits a spike. That output spike is the message sent to later neurons. The important change is that the output is event-based: the neuron does not continuously report every small value of its membrane potential. It waits until the accumulated input is strong enough, then sends a spike at a specific time.

The pattern is easiest to read as a graph of membrane potential over time.

*LIF neuron membrane potential model* *(p. 4)*

The curve rises when input current pushes the membrane potential upward. Between pushes, the curve slopes downward because leak is reducing the stored potential. When the curve reaches the threshold line, the neuron fires a spike. Immediately afterward, the membrane potential drops back to a reset level. That sharp drop is part of the mechanism: the spike is the event, and the reset prepares the neuron to measure the next buildup of incoming activity. Reading the graph from left to right, the neuron is repeatedly asking, "Has recent input accumulated enough to justify sending an event now?"

This reset also explains why a LIF neuron can produce a sequence of spikes instead of one permanent high output. After firing, the membrane potential returns to a lower level, so more incoming activity is needed before another spike can occur. If input remains strong, the membrane potential rises again and may cross the threshold again. If input weakens, leak may pull the potential down before it reaches threshold. Spike timing therefore depends on the balance between input strength, leak, threshold, and reset level. Stronger input tends to make the membrane potential climb faster. A higher threshold requires more accumulated input before firing. Stronger leak makes it harder for slow or weak input to accumulate.

Consider a simple interpretation. A neuron connected to an image sensor might receive spikes when nearby pixels change. A single tiny change may raise the membrane potential a little, then leak away. A cluster of related changes arriving close together may push the potential above threshold, causing the neuron to fire. The outgoing spike then represents a meaningful event: enough recent activity has gathered at that neuron to pass a signal forward. The exact membrane potential before firing helped make the decision, but the communicated output is the spike event.

A common beginner confusion is to picture the membrane potential itself as the final message. The better mental picture is that membrane potential is the neuron's private running evidence, while the spike is the public signal it sends onward. The private state can rise and fall at every time step. The public signal appears only when the threshold is crossed. This is how the neuron connects continuous internal accumulation with discrete external communication.

**Question.** If a neuron receives several weak spikes spread far apart in time, why might it never fire?

**Answer.** Each incoming spike can raise the membrane potential, but leak lowers the membrane potential between inputs. When weak spikes arrive far apart, much of the earlier buildup can fade before the next spike arrives. The membrane potential may repeatedly rise a little and then drift downward, staying below the firing threshold. The neuron fires only when recent input accumulates quickly enough to cross that threshold.

**Question.** What does the reset after a spike accomplish?

**Answer.** The reset brings the membrane potential back down after the neuron emits a spike. This lets the neuron begin a new accumulation cycle instead of remaining at or above threshold. After reset, another spike requires another buildup of incoming activity, so the timing of future spikes reflects new input rather than the leftover potential from the previous firing event.

The LIF neuron gives SNNs a compact mechanism for event-based computation. Incoming spikes do not automatically become outgoing spikes. They first shape an internal membrane potential that integrates recent activity while leak gradually removes stale evidence. When that internal level reaches the firing threshold, the neuron converts the buildup into a spike and resets. The whole mechanism turns "how much recent input has arrived?" into "when should an event be sent?", which is why the LIF model fits naturally with spike-based networks that compute through events in time.
