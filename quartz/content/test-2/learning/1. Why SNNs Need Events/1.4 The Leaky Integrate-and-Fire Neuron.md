## The Leaky Integrate-and-Fire Neuron

A spike event gives an SNN a compact way to say, "something happened at this time." Now imagine one neuron receiving a stream of those events from earlier neurons. A single incoming spike may be too weak to justify sending a new spike onward, but several nearby spikes may together form strong evidence. The neuron therefore needs a small internal memory of recent input. That memory is called the **membrane potential**: a changing internal value that rises when input arrives and determines whether the neuron is ready to fire.

A useful everyday picture is a cup placed under a dripping faucet. Each drop adds water to the cup. If the water reaches a marked line, the cup tips and empties. The LIF neuron works in a similar spirit. Incoming spikes add to the membrane potential. The membrane potential accumulates these additions over time. When it reaches a chosen level, called the **firing threshold**, the neuron emits one output spike. After that spike, the membrane potential is brought back down by a **reset**, so the neuron can begin accumulating evidence again.

The name **leaky integrate-and-fire** describes the three parts of this behavior. **Integrate** means the neuron adds incoming activity into its membrane potential. **Fire** means it sends a spike when the membrane potential reaches threshold. **Leaky** means the stored potential fades over time when input is weak or absent. The leak gives the neuron a sense of recency: recent spikes count more strongly than old spikes because old contributions slowly drain away.

This leak is what makes timing meaningful. Suppose two input spikes arrive very close together. The first spike raises the membrane potential, and the second arrives before much of that rise has leaked away. Their effects combine, so the neuron may cross threshold and fire. If the same two spikes arrive far apart, the rise from the first spike has mostly faded before the second arrives. The neuron then treats them as separate weak hints rather than one strong pattern. In this way, a LIF neuron turns "how many spikes arrived?" and "when did they arrive?" into one evolving internal signal.

The membrane potential can be read as accumulated evidence. A low value means recent input has been weak, sparse, or spread out in time. A rising value means input is arriving strongly enough that the neuron is building toward a decision. The firing threshold is the boundary between a private internal state and a public event. Below threshold, the neuron keeps its changing membrane potential to itself. At threshold, it converts that gradual buildup into a discrete spike that other neurons can receive.

The graph below shows this cycle directly. The membrane potential rises as input current pushes it upward, drops or slows when leak dominates, crosses the threshold when accumulated input is strong enough, and then resets after the spike. The spike is tied to the crossing point: it happens when the internal buildup reaches the threshold line. The reset after firing prevents the same accumulated evidence from causing repeated spikes without new input.

Read the curve as a story in time. At the beginning, the neuron is quiet but ready. Incoming activity raises the membrane potential, so the curve climbs. Between pushes, leakage pulls the potential downward or keeps it from climbing as quickly. When enough input arrives close enough together, integration wins over leakage and the curve reaches the threshold. The neuron fires one spike, then resets to a lower value. The next spike will require a new buildup, so each output event represents a fresh crossing caused by recent input.

A beginner often expects the neuron to spike every time it receives an input spike. The LIF mechanism gives a more selective picture. An input spike is a contribution, not a command. It nudges the membrane potential upward, and the neuron fires only if the total recent contribution is large enough to overcome leak and reach threshold. This is why LIF neurons support sparse event-driven computation: many small or isolated inputs can be absorbed silently, while strong patterns become output spikes.

The firing threshold also controls how easily the neuron responds. A lower threshold means fewer or weaker inputs can trigger a spike. A higher threshold means the neuron requires stronger accumulated evidence. Input strength and leak interact with that threshold. Stronger input makes the membrane potential rise faster. Greater leak makes stored potential fade faster. A neuron with strong input and mild leak may fire quickly; a neuron with weak input and strong leak may never reach threshold during the same time window.

**Question.** If a LIF neuron receives several spikes but never fires, did those spikes have no effect?

**Answer.** They still affected the neuron by raising its membrane potential for a time. The neuron stayed silent because the accumulated potential did not reach the firing threshold before enough of it leaked away. The inputs were registered internally, but they were not strong or timely enough to become an output spike.

The reset is part of the event-making process. Once the neuron fires, its membrane potential is lowered so the spike marks a completed decision rather than a continuing overflow. Returning to a lower potential lets the neuron separate one event from the next. If more input arrives after the reset, the neuron begins integrating again. This creates a rhythm of accumulation, threshold crossing, spike emission, and renewed accumulation.

The mechanism also explains why SNNs can save energy. A neuron can receive weak or scattered input without forcing continuous output activity. Computation becomes concentrated around the moments when membrane potential crosses threshold and spikes are produced. Since spikes are discrete events, later neurons only need to respond when those events occur. The LIF neuron is therefore a bridge between continuous internal change and event-based communication: it quietly tracks recent evidence through membrane potential, lets leak remove stale evidence, and sends a spike only when the evidence becomes strong enough.

**Question.** Why does the leak make the neuron more sensitive to timing?

**Answer.** Leak causes older input to fade. When spikes arrive close together, their effects overlap in the membrane potential and can combine to reach threshold. When spikes arrive far apart, the earlier rise has time to drain away, so the later spike starts from a lower potential. The same number of input spikes can therefore produce different outcomes depending on their timing.

The mental model is a balance. Input pushes the membrane potential upward, leak pulls it back down, threshold marks the point where private accumulation becomes a public spike, and reset prepares the neuron for the next buildup. A LIF neuron turns streams of incoming spike events into new spike events by asking one repeated question over time: has recent input accumulated enough, despite leakage, to cross the firing threshold?
