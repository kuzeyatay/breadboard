## The Leaky Integrate-and-Fire Neuron

Imagine a sensor watching a mostly still scene. For many moments, the incoming signal changes only a little. A spiking system can stay quiet during those moments, then send a spike when the evidence becomes strong enough to deserve an event. The question is how a neuron can decide when "strong enough" has arrived. A single input pulse might be small, but several small inputs arriving close together can carry a clear message. A Leaky Integrate-and-Fire neuron gives that decision a simple internal mechanism: it keeps a running internal state, lets that state fade over time, and emits a spike when the state crosses a threshold.

That running internal state is called the **membrane potential**. You can think of it as the neuron's current level of built-up evidence. Incoming spikes push the membrane potential upward. Each input contributes some amount, so the neuron does more than react to one instant in isolation; it carries information forward through time. If several inputs arrive close together, their effects can add together before the earlier ones disappear. This adding process is called **integration over time**. The neuron is integrating because it accumulates multiple incoming contributions into one internal value.

A useful analogy is a bucket under a dripping tap. Each drop raises the water level. If drops arrive quickly, the level rises. If drops arrive slowly, the bucket may never reach the mark that triggers action. The LIF neuron behaves in a similar way, except the "water level" is membrane potential and the "mark" is the firing threshold. The neuron turns a stream of incoming events into a new event by asking whether the accumulated potential has become large enough.

The "leaky" part makes the bucket analogy slightly more realistic: the bucket has a small hole. The membrane potential fades when input stops or arrives too slowly. This fading is **leakage**. Leakage prevents old evidence from remaining equally strong forever. A spike that arrived a long time ago should usually influence the current decision less than a spike that arrived just now. With leakage, timing becomes meaningful: the same number of input spikes can have different effects depending on whether they are tightly clustered or spread out.

A simple way to express this idea is:

$V_{\text{new}} = V_{\text{old}} + \text{input} - \text{leak}$

Here, $V_{\text{old}}$ is the membrane potential before the current moment, $\text{input}$ is the added effect of incoming spikes or current, $\text{leak}$ is the amount of potential lost over time, and $V_{\text{new}}$ is the updated membrane potential after accumulation and decay. This formula lets you compute the neuron's next internal state from its previous internal state. The exact mathematical form can vary across implementations, but the idea stays the same: input raises the potential, leakage lowers it, and the neuron keeps updating this value across time.

Suppose a neuron starts with membrane potential $0$, has a threshold of $5$, receives input worth $2$ units at one moment, then loses $1$ unit through leakage before the next moment. Its potential becomes $1$. If another input worth $3$ arrives soon after and leakage again removes $1$, the potential becomes $3$. The neuron has received two inputs, but it still has no spike because its membrane potential has stayed below $5$. If a third input worth $3$ arrives before much more leakage occurs, the potential can rise past $5$. At that crossing, the neuron emits a spike.

The graph shows the membrane potential climbing as input current accumulates, then falling or slowing because leakage pulls it back down. The horizontal threshold line gives the neuron a clear rule for turning a continuous internal buildup into a discrete spike event. The curve does not produce a spike merely because input exists; it produces a spike when accumulated potential crosses the threshold. After the spike, the membrane potential resets, which prepares the neuron to begin accumulating evidence again for the next event. The reset makes each spike a distinct signal rather than an endlessly increasing voltage.

This threshold crossing is the "fire" step in Leaky Integrate-and-Fire. The neuron fires by emitting a spike, a brief event at a particular time. That spike can then become input to later neurons. In this way, membrane potential turns accumulated input into a discrete event. The internal state can vary smoothly across time, but communication between neurons happens through spikes. The timing of the spike carries information because it reveals when the accumulated evidence became sufficient.

A beginner confusion often appears here: if input raises the membrane potential, why does every input spike fail to cause an output spike? The answer is that the LIF neuron separates receiving evidence from making a decision. An input spike contributes to the membrane potential, but the output spike appears only when the total accumulated potential crosses the threshold. A small input can matter by moving the neuron closer to firing, even when it does not immediately produce a visible output. The membrane potential makes those partial state changes meaningful inside the neuron, and the spike makes the completed decision visible to the rest of the network.

**Question.** If two neurons receive the same total input, can one spike earlier than the other?

**Answer.** Yes. Spike timing depends on how input, leakage, and threshold interact over time. If one neuron receives its inputs close together, the membrane potential can accumulate quickly before much leakage occurs, so it may cross threshold early. If another neuron receives the same total input spread across a longer interval, leakage can remove part of the earlier buildup before later input arrives, so the neuron may spike later or may never spike at all.

The threshold also controls how selective the neuron is. A low threshold allows a smaller amount of accumulated evidence to trigger a spike. A high threshold requires stronger or more closely timed input. Leakage controls how long evidence remains useful. Strong leakage makes the neuron sensitive to recent input because old contributions fade quickly. Weak leakage lets evidence persist longer, allowing slower input patterns to combine. Input strength controls how large each push upward is. These three forces-input, leak, and threshold-jointly determine whether the neuron stays silent, spikes quickly, or spikes only after prolonged stimulation.

This mechanism supports sparse activity. Since the neuron fires only after threshold crossing, many neurons can remain silent when their accumulated evidence stays weak. Silence still carries meaning: it indicates that the neuron's internal potential has not crossed the firing condition. When a spike does occur, the event marks a moment when enough evidence has gathered despite leakage. That makes spike count related to activity cost, because fewer spikes mean fewer event transmissions through the network.

**Question.** What does the membrane potential represent if no spike has happened yet?

**Answer.** It represents accumulated evidence that has not yet crossed the firing threshold. The neuron may be partway toward producing a spike, and that partial progress can rise with new input or fade through leakage. A missing spike does not mean nothing has happened inside the neuron; it means the internal state has remained below the level required to emit an event.

The LIF neuron therefore gives an SNN a compact rule for event-based computation. Incoming spikes arrive across a time series. The membrane potential stores their recent influence. Leakage makes old influence fade. The threshold defines the moment when accumulated evidence becomes strong enough to send onward. Spike emission converts that hidden buildup into a visible event, and reset begins the cycle again. A spiking neuron can then be understood as a small timing-sensitive decision device: it listens over time, forgets gradually, and speaks only when its accumulated state crosses the line.
