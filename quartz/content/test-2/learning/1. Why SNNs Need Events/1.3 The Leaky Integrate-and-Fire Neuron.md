### The Leaky Integrate-and-Fire Neuron

Event-driven sparsity explains why spiking networks can be efficient. The next question is mechanical: what does a single spiking neuron actually do to turn incoming current into a spike? Consider the membrane of one neuron as it integrates input, leaks charge, and fires when it crosses a threshold.

The quantity that keeps track of this buildup is the **membrane potential**. You can think of it as the neuron's internal "charge level" or "readiness to fire." Each incoming spike nudges this level upward. A stronger or more frequent input produces larger or repeated nudges, so the membrane potential climbs more quickly. The neuron is therefore doing **integration**, which means it is accumulating input over time instead of reacting to each input in isolation.

A useful analogy is a cup under a dripping faucet. Each drop raises the water level a little. If the drops arrive quickly, the cup fills faster. If the drops arrive slowly, the level may rise only slightly before other effects pull it back down. In a leaky integrate-and-fire neuron, the membrane potential is like that water level, and incoming spikes are like drops. The neuron does not immediately fire just because one drop arrived; it fires when the accumulated level reaches a chosen boundary.

That boundary is the **threshold**. The threshold is the membrane potential level that must be crossed before the neuron emits a spike. Once the membrane potential reaches this level, the neuron produces a discrete spike event. This is how threshold crossing turns accumulation into events: a gradually changing internal state becomes a clear "now" signal. The spike is the neuron's way of saying that the evidence has become strong enough at this moment in time.

*LIF neuron membrane potential model with threshold over time* *(p. 4)*

The graph shows the mechanism in one picture. The membrane potential rises over time as input current pushes it upward. A horizontal threshold line marks the firing boundary. When the rising membrane potential reaches that line, the neuron emits a spike, and the membrane potential resets. The shape teaches an important timing idea: the spike is produced at the moment the accumulated potential crosses the threshold, so the timing of the spike depends on how quickly input builds up and how much potential is lost along the way.

The word **leaky** explains that loss. A neuron does not keep every bit of input forever. Its membrane potential gradually decays when input weakens or pauses. In the cup analogy, leak is a small hole near the bottom: drops still raise the water level, but water also drains away. If drops arrive too slowly, the cup never reaches the firing line. If drops arrive quickly enough, the level climbs despite the leak and eventually crosses the threshold.

This leak gives the neuron a simple kind of memory with forgetting. Recent input has more influence than old input, because old input has had more time to fade. A spike that arrived just now may still push the membrane potential close to threshold. A spike that arrived long ago may have almost disappeared from the neuron's internal state. That is why a leaky integrate-and-fire neuron works naturally with time-varying signals: it can respond to patterns that are strong over a short window, while letting isolated weak changes fade away.

**Question.** If one incoming spike raises the membrane potential, why does the neuron sometimes stay silent?

**Answer.** One incoming spike may raise the membrane potential only partway toward the threshold. If no more input arrives soon, leak pulls the membrane potential back down before it crosses the firing boundary. Silence means the accumulated evidence stayed below threshold, so the neuron had no spike event to send.

After the neuron fires, its membrane potential resets. Resetting prepares the neuron to begin accumulating again from a lower level. Without this reset, a neuron that crossed threshold once would remain above the firing boundary and keep signaling continuously. Reset makes each spike a distinct event caused by a particular buildup of input. The neuron can then start a new cycle: receive spikes, integrate them into membrane potential, lose some potential through leak, cross threshold if the buildup is strong enough, emit a spike, and reset.

This cycle explains how membrane potential makes state changes visible as spikes. The membrane potential itself is an internal, time-varying state. It can rise, fall, and hover below threshold without producing an output event. The outside network sees a spike only when that internal state crosses the firing boundary. In this way, the neuron converts a continuous internal process into sparse communication. Many small changes can be absorbed silently; a large enough or timely enough pattern becomes an event that downstream neurons can receive.

A beginner confusion often comes from imagining the spike as a value whose height carries the message. In this model, the spike is best understood as an event at a time. The important information is that the neuron fired, and when it fired. The changing membrane potential decides that timing. Strong input can push the membrane potential to threshold quickly, producing an earlier spike. Weaker input may produce a later spike, or no spike if leak prevents threshold crossing. The neuron's output is therefore discrete, while the decision process that leads to it unfolds over time.

**Question.** What changes if the threshold is higher?

**Answer.** A higher threshold means the membrane potential must accumulate more input before the neuron fires. With the same input and leak, the spike will usually happen later, or it may never happen during the observed time window. A lower threshold makes firing easier because less accumulated potential is needed to produce a spike.

The leaky integrate-and-fire neuron gives a compact mechanism for event-based computation. Input spikes push the membrane potential upward. Leak pulls it downward over time. The threshold defines the point where accumulated evidence becomes strong enough to communicate. Spike emission turns that crossing into a discrete event, and reset clears the state for the next buildup. The whole neuron can be pictured as a small accumulator with forgetting and a firing line: it listens to time-varying input, keeps only a fading trace of recent activity, and speaks only when that trace becomes strong enough to matter.
