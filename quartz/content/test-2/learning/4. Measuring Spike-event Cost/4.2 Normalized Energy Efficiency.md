## Normalized Energy Efficiency

Classification accuracy tells us how often a model predicts correctly, while energy per inference tells us how much electrical energy it spends on one prediction. Model selection often requires considering both quantities together. Imagine choosing a spiking network for a battery-powered device: one candidate reaches slightly higher accuracy, while another consumes far less energy. Reading the two measurements separately leaves the choice unresolved because each candidate looks better along a different axis. A combined ratio answers a more focused question: how much predictive accuracy does the model deliver for each joule it consumes?

This ratio is called **normalized energy efficiency**, or **accuracy per joule**. To build it, begin with the model's classification accuracy $A$ and divide it by its energy consumption per inference $E$:
$$
\eta_{\text{norm}}=\frac{A}{E}
$$
Here, $\eta_{\text{norm}}$ is the normalized energy efficiency, $A$ is the classification accuracy, and $E$ is the energy consumed for one inference, measured in joules. A **joule**, abbreviated $J$, is a unit of energy. The formula computes the amount of accuracy delivered per joule of energy expenditure.

Accuracy should be written as a fraction when calculating the ratio. For example, an accuracy of $97.8\%$ becomes $0.978$. Energy must be expressed in joules. Because $1$ millijoule equals $0.001$ joules, an energy cost of $15\text{ mJ}$ becomes $0.015\text{ J}$. Using a fraction for accuracy gives the ratio units of inverse joules, written $J^{-1}$, which can be read as "accuracy fraction per joule."

Consider a surrogate-gradient spiking neural network with an accuracy of approximately $97.8\%$ and an energy cost of $15\text{ mJ}$ per inference. Converting both values into the required form gives
$$
A=0.978
$$
And
$$
E=15\text{ mJ}=0.015\text{ J}
$$
Substituting these values into the ratio gives
$$
\eta_{\text{norm}}=\frac{0.978}{0.015}=65.2\text{ J}^{-1}
$$
The value $65.2\text{ J}^{-1}$ expresses the model's accuracy relative to its energy use. It provides a common scale for comparing models whose accuracy and energy costs differ. If accuracy were left in percentage form, the same calculation would produce $6520$ percentage points per joule. Both conventions encode the same relationship, but comparisons remain meaningful only when every model uses the same convention.

Now consider an STDP-based spiking network with approximately $95.5\%$ accuracy and an energy cost of $5\text{ mJ}$ per inference. Its normalized energy efficiency is
$$
\eta_{\text{norm}}=\frac{0.955}{0.005}=191\text{ J}^{-1}
$$
The STDP-based model therefore delivers a larger accuracy fraction per joule, even though its raw accuracy is lower. Its energy consumption falls from $15\text{ mJ}$ to $5\text{ mJ}$, a threefold reduction, while its accuracy decreases from about $97.8\%$ to $95.5\%$. The large energy reduction has a stronger effect on the ratio than the comparatively small accuracy reduction. For an energy-constrained application, that profile may be attractive. An application with a strict accuracy target may still favor the surrogate-gradient model.

The ratio changes through either of its two components. Raising $A$ while holding $E$ fixed increases normalized energy efficiency because more predictions are correct for the same energy cost. Lowering $E$ while holding $A$ fixed also increases it because the same predictive performance is obtained with less energy. Improving both quantities produces the strongest increase. A loss of accuracy can still accompany a higher ratio when energy falls by a sufficiently large proportion, as the worked comparison demonstrates.

**Question.** If two models have the same accuracy per joule, are they equally accurate and equally energy-efficient?

**Answer.** Equal ratios can come from different combinations of accuracy and energy. A model with $A=0.90$ and $E=0.01\text{ J}$ has an accuracy-per-joule value of $90\text{ J}^{-1}$. A model with $A=0.45$ and $E=0.005\text{ J}$ has the same value. Their normalized scores match, yet one provides much higher accuracy and consumes more energy per inference. The ratio supports comparison, while the component measurements reveal the operating profile behind that comparison.

This creates a **normalization tradeoff**. Combining two measurements makes broad efficiency patterns easier to see, but compression into one number removes information about how the score was achieved. A very low-energy model can receive a high normalized score despite an accuracy level that fails an application's requirements. A highly accurate model can receive a lower score because its energy cost is larger, even when that extra accuracy is essential. Model evaluation therefore begins with constraints: the system may require a minimum accuracy, a maximum energy budget, or both. Accuracy per joule then helps rank candidates that satisfy those constraints.

**Question.** Why must energy values be converted from millijoules to joules before comparing accuracy per joule?

**Answer.** The denominator determines the scale of the result. Dividing by $15$ while treating millijoules as joules would make the reported ratio one thousand times too small. Since $15\text{ mJ}=0.015\text{ J}$, the correct denominator for an accuracy-per-joule calculation is $0.015$. Every candidate must use the same energy unit so that its normalized score lies on the same comparison scale.

A useful mental model is to picture accuracy as the useful predictive result and energy as the resource spent to obtain it. Normalized energy efficiency asks how much result is delivered for each unit of that resource. The ratio rises when prediction improves, energy expenditure falls, or both occur together. Its two ingredients remain visible during interpretation: accuracy establishes whether the model performs well enough, energy establishes whether it fits the computational budget, and their ratio shows how efficiently the model turns energy into predictive performance.
