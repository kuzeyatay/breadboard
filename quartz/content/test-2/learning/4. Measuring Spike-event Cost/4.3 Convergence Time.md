## Convergence Time

Once training has been organized into epochs and accuracy can be measured after each epoch, a practical question appears: how long does the model need to reach a useful level of performance? Imagine that a spiking neural network will be considered ready for further evaluation when it correctly classifies 90% of the validation examples. The training process may eventually reach that level, yet reaching it after 5 epochs demands far less training than reaching it after 50. Accuracy describes the performance achieved at a particular moment; **convergence time** records how quickly training reaches a chosen performance requirement.

An **epoch** is one complete pass through the training data. After each epoch, the model can be evaluated on a fixed validation set to obtain its classification accuracy. If 900 out of 1,000 validation examples are classified correctly, the measured accuracy is $900/1000=0.90$, or 90%. Repeating this measurement after every epoch produces a sequence such as
$$
0.63,\ 0.74,\ 0.82,\ 0.88,\ 0.91,\ 0.92
$$
Each value corresponds to one epoch. Here, the model has an accuracy of 63% after epoch 1, 74% after epoch 2, and so on. The sequence reveals progress, but a convergence-time metric requires one additional ingredient: a performance level that counts as sufficient.

That level is the **target accuracy**, a selected accuracy value that training is expected to reach. Denote it by $A_{\text{target}}$. A target might come from an application requirement or from an evaluation protocol used to compare several training methods. Using the same target for every model makes the comparison meaningful because each model is being timed against the same destination.

Let $A(e)$ represent the measured accuracy after epoch $e$. Convergence time can then be defined as
$$
T_{\text{conv}}
=
\min \left\{e \mid A(e)\geq A_{\text{target}}\right\}
$$
In this formula, $T_{\text{conv}}$ is the **convergence time**, measured in epochs; $e$ is an epoch number; $A(e)$ is the model's measured accuracy after epoch $e$; and $A_{\text{target}}$ is the selected target accuracy. The vertical bar inside the braces means "such that," so the set contains every epoch whose measured accuracy reaches or exceeds the target. The operator $\min$ selects the smallest epoch number in that set. The formula therefore computes the earliest qualifying epoch.

Consider the accuracy sequence above with $A_{\text{target}}=0.90$. Epoch 4 produces $A(4)=0.88$, which remains below the target. Epoch 5 produces $A(5)=0.91$, which reaches the requirement because $0.91\geq0.90$. Epoch 6 also qualifies with an accuracy of 0.92, but the minimum qualifying epoch is 5. The convergence time is therefore
$$
T_{\text{conv}}=5\text{ epochs}
$$
The word "minimum" gives the metric its precise meaning. Training may continue after the target is reached, and later epochs may achieve higher accuracy, but convergence time preserves the moment when the chosen requirement was first satisfied. It acts like a stopwatch that stops at the first crossing of a finish line.

**Question.** What happens if accuracy rises above the target and then falls below it during a later epoch?

**Answer.** The definition still uses the earliest epoch at which the target is reached. Suppose the target is 90% and the measured accuracies are 88% at epoch 6, 91% at epoch 7, and 89% at epoch 8. Epoch 7 is the minimum qualifying epoch, so $T_{\text{conv}}=7$. A stricter evaluation could separately require accuracy to remain above the target for several epochs, but that would define a different stability-based metric. The convergence-time formula here records the first target crossing.

The chosen target directly affects the reported convergence time. For the sequence $0.63, 0.74, 0.82, 0.88, 0.91, 0.92$, a target of 0.80 gives a convergence time of 3 epochs, while a target of 0.90 gives a convergence time of 5 epochs. Raising the target moves the finish line farther along the training process. A reported convergence time therefore needs its target accuracy beside it: "5 epochs to 90% accuracy" communicates more than "5 epochs" alone.

A model may also finish the allowed training period without reaching the target. In that case, the set
$$
\left\{e \mid A(e)\geq A_{\text{target}}\right\}
$$
Contains no epochs, so it has no minimum. The result should be reported as "target accuracy not reached within the training budget," together with the number of epochs allowed. This wording distinguishes an unobserved crossing from an observed late crossing.

**Question.** Can two models have the same convergence time even if their later accuracies differ?

**Answer.** Yes. If both models first reach an 85% target at epoch 4, both have $T_{\text{conv}}=4$ for that target. One may continue to 88% while the other reaches 93%. Convergence time answers when each model first met the selected requirement, while later accuracy describes performance after additional training. Keeping those measurements separate allows a fair multi-metric evaluation.

The complete mental model is a race toward a declared accuracy line. Epochs provide the clock ticks, validation accuracy provides the model's position after each tick, and the target accuracy provides the finish line. Convergence time is the earliest tick at which the model reaches that line. With the target stated explicitly and the minimum qualifying epoch identified consistently, training speed becomes a measurable quantity that can be compared across learning methods.
