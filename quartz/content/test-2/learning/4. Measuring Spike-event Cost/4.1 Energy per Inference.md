## Energy per Inference

Once spike events and synaptic operations can be counted, their computational cost can be estimated for one complete inference. Imagine presenting a single input to a spiking neural network and following its activity until the network produces an output. Some neurons emit spikes, and each spike may travel across several synaptic connections. The network therefore spends energy both on handling spike events and on performing the synaptic operations triggered by those events. Energy per inference combines these two kinds of activity into one metric.

An **inference** is one complete use of a trained network to produce an output from an input. Energy is measured in **joules**, abbreviated J, although small values are often expressed in millijoules, where $1\ \text{mJ}=10^{-3}\ \text{J}$. The central measurement question is: how much energy does the network consume while processing one input?

Counting spikes alone cannot fully answer that question. Suppose one neuron emits a spike that reaches two downstream synapses, while another emits a spike that fans out to one hundred downstream synapses. Both cases add one spike to the spike-event count, yet the second spike causes far more synaptic work. A useful estimate must therefore track the number of spike events and the number of synaptic operations separately.

A **spike event** is the occurrence or handling of one discrete spike. Its energy cost can include the work needed to generate, transmit, route, or register that event within the evaluation model. If the system handles $N_{\text{spike}}$ spike events and assigns an average energy cost $e_{\text{spike}}$ to each one, then the estimated energy associated with spikes is
$$
E_{\text{spike}} = N_{\text{spike}}e_{\text{spike}}
$$
Here, $E_{\text{spike}}$ is the estimated energy consumed by spike events during one inference, $N_{\text{spike}}$ is the spike-event count, and $e_{\text{spike}}$ is the assumed average energy cost per spike event. Multiplication appears because the same average cost is charged once for every counted event. For example, if an inference produces $4{,}000$ spike events, the model includes $4{,}000$ units of spike-event cost. The value of each unit depends on the energy assumption used for $e_{\text{spike}}$.

A **synaptic operation** occurs when activity arriving through a synaptic connection contributes to the state of a receiving neuron. One spike can trigger many such operations because a neuron may connect to many other neurons. If $N_{\text{syn}}$ synaptic operations occur and each operation has an average energy cost $e_{\text{syn}}$, their estimated energy is
$$
E_{\text{syn}} = N_{\text{syn}}e_{\text{syn}}
$$
Where $E_{\text{syn}}$ is the estimated energy associated with synaptic processing, $N_{\text{syn}}$ is the synaptic-operation count, and $e_{\text{syn}}$ is the assumed average energy cost per synaptic operation. This term captures the work that spike count alone misses.

The estimated energy consumption for the whole inference is obtained by adding the two contributions:
$$
\boxed{
E_{\text{inference}}
=
N_{\text{spike}}e_{\text{spike}}
+
N_{\text{syn}}e_{\text{syn}}
}
$$
In this expression:

- $E_{\text{inference}}$ is the estimated energy consumption for one inference.
- $N_{\text{spike}}$ is the number of spike events during that inference.
- $e_{\text{spike}}$ is the average energy cost assigned to one spike event.
- $N_{\text{syn}}$ is the number of synaptic operations during that inference.
- $e_{\text{syn}}$ is the average energy cost assigned to one synaptic operation.

The formula lets you convert an activity profile into an energy estimate. First count the relevant events and operations. Then multiply each count by its assigned unit cost. Finally add the resulting energy contributions.

Consider a small network that generates $500$ spike events during one inference. Each spike reaches an average of four synapses, so those spikes trigger approximately
$$
N_{\text{syn}} = 500 \times 4 = 2{,}000
$$
Synaptic operations. Suppose the evaluation model assigns a cost of $2$ energy units to each spike event and $1$ energy unit to each synaptic operation. The estimate becomes
$$
E_{\text{inference}}
=
(500)(2)+(2{,}000)(1)
=
1{,}000+2{,}000
=
3{,}000
$$
Energy units per inference. The calculation reveals that synaptic processing contributes twice as much as spike handling in this example. Reducing the spike count could lower both terms because fewer spikes are handled and fewer connections are activated. Reducing fan-out-the average number of synapses reached by each spike-primarily lowers the synaptic-operation term.

This relationship explains why sparse, event-driven activity can reduce estimated energy. During quiet periods, neurons emit fewer spikes. Fewer spikes generally trigger fewer synaptic operations, so both activity counts can fall. Yet sparsity must be considered across the whole network. A modest spike count can still produce substantial synaptic work when each spike reaches many destinations, while a larger spike count can produce less work when connectivity is narrow.

**Question.** Why does the model need a synaptic-operation count if it already has a spike count?

**Answer.** Spike count records how many discrete events occur, while synaptic-operation count records how much downstream work those events trigger. One spike may activate many synapses, so two networks with the same number of spikes can perform very different numbers of synaptic operations and receive different energy estimates.

The unit costs also shape the result. If spike handling is expensive relative to synaptic accumulation, $e_{\text{spike}}$ gives the spike term more weight. If synaptic operations dominate the assumed cost, connectivity and fan-out become especially influential. Meaningful comparisons therefore use the same definitions and unit-cost assumptions for every network being compared.

**Question.** Does $E_{\text{inference}}$ give the exact energy that any physical device will consume?

**Answer.** It gives an estimate under a specified cost model. The counts describe network activity, while the per-event and per-operation costs translate that activity into energy. A physical measurement can also reflect memory access, communication, control logic, idle power, and device-specific behavior. The estimate remains useful because it exposes how much energy is associated with the network's event-driven computation under consistent assumptions.

A practical mental model is to picture each inference as an itemized energy bill. Every spike adds a spike-event charge, and every activated synapse adds a synaptic-operation charge. The total bill depends on how often neurons fire, how widely their spikes propagate, and what cost the evaluation model assigns to each kind of work.
