After comparing SNNs, ANNs, converted SNNs, surrogate-gradient SNNs, and STDP-based SNNs across accuracy, latency, energy, spike count, and convergence, the natural temptation is to turn the strongest numbers into a simple verdict: SNNs save huge amounts of energy, so SNNs are the future choice for low-power intelligence. A responsible reading is more careful. The comparison gives a useful decision picture, but that picture lives inside the conditions that produced it: the chosen models, datasets, metrics, training methods, hardware assumptions, and reporting boundaries. A benchmark result is evidence about a particular tested situation before it becomes guidance for a broader design choice.

Imagine an engineer choosing a model for a small gesture-recognition device. The device has a battery, a sensor that changes over time, and a strict response-time requirement. The reported SNN results look attractive: energy use can drop by roughly 90-97% compared with an ANN, surrogate-gradient SNNs can approach ANN accuracy within about 1-2%, latency can reach around 10 ms, and STDP-based SNNs can reduce energy to about 5 mJ per inference with sparse spiking activity. Those numbers are valuable because they show that event-based computation can trade dense continuous processing for sparse spike events. Silence costs little, and spikes appear mainly when information changes. For an edge device, that can turn into less energy, lower heat, and faster reactions.

The careful part begins when the engineer asks what exactly those numbers license them to claim. A single-source limitation means the comparison is grounded in one provided evidence base rather than in many independent replications across many labs, datasets, chips, and deployment settings. The finding can support a sentence such as "under these reported comparison conditions, these SNN methods showed large energy savings with modest accuracy tradeoffs." It gives weaker support for a sweeping sentence such as "SNNs generally outperform ANNs." The safer sentence keeps the claim attached to the tested setting, while the sweeping sentence jumps from one evidence window to a field-wide rule.

A reported finding is a result presented inside a defined experiment or comparison. Field consensus is a broader judgment that emerges when many studies, methods, benchmarks, and real deployments point in the same direction. The energy advantage reported here is strong within the comparison: an ANN/CNN is described around 200 mJ per inference, a converted SNN around 20 mJ, a surrogate-gradient SNN around 15 mJ, and an STDP-based SNN around 5 mJ. Interpreted directly, this means spike-based models can reduce energy by making computation sparse, and STDP can become especially efficient when very few spikes are generated. Interpreted too broadly, the same numbers could be mistaken for a permanent ranking that holds for every task and every hardware platform. The supported mental move is to treat the ranking as a design clue: when energy is the tight constraint, SNN variants deserve serious consideration.

A scope boundary is the line around what a result actually covers. Here, the comparison covers several model families and several metrics: accuracy, energy consumption, latency, spike count, and convergence behavior. That multi-metric view is stronger than looking at accuracy alone because SNNs are often chosen for a bundle of constraints rather than for one score. A surrogate-gradient SNN may be attractive when accuracy and response speed both matter, since it is reported near ANN accuracy with latency around 10 ms and convergence by about the 20th epoch. An STDP-based SNN may be attractive when energy and sparse activity dominate the design problem, since it is reported with the lowest spike count and lowest energy, while giving up some accuracy. A converted SNN may be useful when starting from an ANN, while its higher spike count and longer simulation window can reduce some of the efficiency advantage.

**Question.** If SNNs can use 90-97% less energy, why can't we simply call them better than ANNs?

**Answer.** "Better" depends on the goal. If the goal is ultra-low energy on temporal or event-based data, the reported SNN results are very strong. If the goal is maximum raw accuracy, easiest training, mature tooling, or performance on a task where dense representations work well, an ANN may remain preferable. The comparison supports energy-aware model selection rather than a universal winner.

Truncated source context adds another layer of caution. A truncated context means the learner sees selected claims, results, and descriptions without every possible experimental detail that would help judge reproducibility. For example, latency values depend on hardware configuration, model complexity, simulation windows, and deployment platform. Energy values depend on how inference energy is measured and what operations are counted. Accuracy gaps depend on the dataset, preprocessing, model size, and training procedure. When context is incomplete, the right response is to preserve the useful pattern while resisting extra precision. It is reasonable to learn that converted SNNs can carry extra spike traffic because rate-coded propagation may need many spikes to approximate ANN activations. It is also reasonable to learn that surrogate-gradient training can shape spiking patterns more directly, improving the balance between accuracy, latency, and spike efficiency. The unsupported leap would be to assume the exact same milliseconds, millijoules, and spike counts will transfer unchanged to every future device.

A beginner may also wonder whether a benchmark is "real" if it has boundaries. A benchmark is real because it creates a controlled comparison. Its boundaries are what make the comparison interpretable. If four model types are compared with the same set of metrics, the learner can see how design choices move the tradeoff: continuous ANN computation tends toward higher energy; converted SNNs reduce energy but may need many spikes; surrogate-gradient SNNs learn spike patterns that balance speed and accuracy; STDP-based SNNs can become extremely sparse and energy efficient. The boundary turns the result into a map of tradeoffs. It becomes misleading only when the map is treated as the whole territory.

**Question.** How should I phrase the result if I want to stay accurate?

**Answer.** Say that the comparison reports large energy savings for SNNs, especially STDP-based and surrogate-trained variants, with tradeoffs in accuracy, latency, spike count, and convergence depending on the training method. Then name the intended setting: low-power, real-time, temporal, or edge-oriented computing. That phrasing keeps the claim tied to the tested comparison and avoids turning a bounded benchmark into a universal law.

The responsible mental model is to treat SNN evidence as a strong signal about where spike-based computation fits: systems with changing signals, tight energy budgets, and value in temporal precision. The numbers teach a direction, not a guarantee. Energy drops when computation becomes sparse; latency improves when useful spikes arrive quickly; accuracy depends on how the spiking model is trained; convergence depends on the learning rule; and adoption depends on whether the whole deployment setting matches the conditions behind the result. A careful learner carries the comparison forward as a design lens: SNNs are promising when the problem rewards event-driven, low-power, time-sensitive computation, and every strong claim should keep its scope boundary attached.
