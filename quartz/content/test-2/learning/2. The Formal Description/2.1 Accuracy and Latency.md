A spiking model has already turned an input stimulus into a decision: a gesture is classified, a spoken sound is recognized, or an event from a sensor is assigned to a category. Once a model can make predictions, two different questions immediately appear. One question asks, "Did it choose the right answer?" The other asks, "How long did it take to choose?" Accuracy answers the first question. Latency answers the second. A useful evaluation keeps these two measurements separate because a model can be very reliable but too slow for a real-time task, or very fast but make too many wrong decisions to trust.

Imagine a hand-gesture sensor controlling a robot. The sensor receives a short burst of visual events when a person waves left. The model observes that input stimulus and eventually decides "left wave." If the true gesture was a left wave, that prediction is correct. If the model decides "right wave," the prediction is incorrect. Accuracy measures the fraction of decisions that land on the correct answer across many trials:
$$
\text{Accuracy} = \frac{\text{number of correct predictions}}{\text{total number of predictions}}
$$
Here, a **prediction** is one completed model decision for one input example. A **correct prediction** is a prediction that matches the true label or desired answer for that example. The **total number of predictions** is the number of examples the model was asked to classify. The formula lets you compute how often the model succeeds as a proportion of all attempts. If the model sees 100 gesture examples and classifies 96 of them correctly, then
$$
\text{Accuracy} = \frac{96}{100} = 0.96
$$
An accuracy of $0.96$ is often written as $96\%$, because multiplying by 100 converts the fraction into a percentage. The meaning stays the same: out of every 100 similar decisions, about 96 are expected to be correct under the same evaluation conditions.

This measurement is deliberately blind to how the model reached the answer. It counts the final decision, then compares that decision with the target. That makes accuracy especially useful when the central requirement is reliability. A medical signal classifier, a safety monitor, or a speech recognizer must produce the correct label often enough to be useful. If two models process the same test set, the model with higher accuracy makes fewer classification mistakes on that test set.

A beginner may wonder why accuracy uses a fraction instead of simply counting the number of correct answers. The reason is that raw counts only make sense when the number of trials is the same. A model that gets 90 examples correct out of 100 has performed better than a model that gets 90 examples correct out of 150. The fraction keeps the comparison fair by asking what share of all predictions were correct.

**Question.** If a spiking model classifies 240 sound samples and gets 210 correct, what is its accuracy?

**Answer.** Its accuracy is $\frac{210}{240} = 0.875$, which is $87.5\%$. The numerator counts correct predictions, and the denominator counts all predictions made during the evaluation.

Accuracy gives one part of the evaluation story, but spiking neural networks are often built for situations where timing carries value. A sensor on a drone, a robot hand, or an edge device may need a decision quickly because the world keeps moving while the model computes. Latency measures that response time. It begins when the input stimulus is presented and ends when the model has produced its decision.
$$
\text{Latency} = t_{\text{decision}} - t_{\text{stimulus}}
$$
In this formula, $t_{\text{stimulus}}$ is the **input stimulus time**, the moment the input begins or becomes available to the model. $t_{\text{decision}}$ is the **decision time**, the moment the model has enough output activity to choose a label. **Latency** is the difference between those times, usually measured in milliseconds for fast inference systems. The formula lets you compute how long the model took to respond after receiving the input.

Suppose an event-based camera begins sending the spike pattern for a hand gesture at $t_{\text{stimulus}} = 0$ ms. The model accumulates spiking activity and produces the label at $t_{\text{decision}} = 10$ ms. Its latency is
$$
\text{Latency} = 10\text{ ms} - 0\text{ ms} = 10\text{ ms}
$$
That number describes speed, not correctness. If the model decides in 10 ms and chooses the wrong class, the latency is still 10 ms. If it decides correctly after 50 ms, its accuracy for that example is good but its response is slower. Accuracy and latency therefore measure different kinds of success: one measures the quality of the chosen label, and the other measures the time needed to produce that label.

This separation becomes especially important for spiking neural networks because their computation unfolds over time. A conventional dense network often processes a fixed array of values through layers and then emits an output. A spiking network may wait for enough spikes to arrive before the output neurons reveal a clear decision. If a class's output neuron spikes strongly and early, the system can decide quickly. If the evidence arrives gradually, the system may need a longer observation window before the decision is stable.

For example, surrogate-gradient spiking networks can reach low-latency responses around $10$ ms while keeping accuracy competitive, with reported accuracy around $97.8\%$ in an integrated comparison. STDP-based spiking networks can use very little energy and stabilize below about $15$ ms under optimized conditions, while reaching lower accuracy around $95.5\%$ in the same kind of comparison. Converted spiking networks may need longer simulation windows, sometimes around $20$ ms, because they approximate activation rates over time. These numbers are useful because they show that "good" depends on the job. A robot avoiding an obstacle may prefer a slightly less accurate model that responds within a tight time window. An offline classifier may tolerate more delay if it gains more correct predictions.

**Question.** A model receives a stimulus at $3$ ms and produces its decision at $18$ ms. On the same trial, the decision is correct. What measurements can you report?

**Answer.** The latency for that trial is $18\text{ ms} - 3\text{ ms} = 15\text{ ms}$. The prediction also counts as one correct prediction for the accuracy calculation. Latency describes how fast the response was; accuracy counts whether the response matched the target.

A common confusion is to treat a faster decision as automatically better. Speed only helps when the decision remains useful. A model that always answers instantly by guessing would have excellent latency and poor accuracy. Another confusion is to treat high accuracy as complete success. A model that reaches high accuracy after a long simulation window may struggle in real-time neuromorphic or edge settings, where the device must act quickly and efficiently. The formal description avoids mixing these ideas: accuracy is a ratio of correct decisions to total decisions, while latency is a time interval from stimulus to decision.

The mental model is simple: after each input stimulus, the model travels along a short timeline. At the start of the timeline, the input appears. During the middle, spikes and internal states evolve. At the end, the model commits to a prediction. Accuracy checks whether that endpoint lands on the right label across many examples. Latency measures how far along the timeline the endpoint occurs. A strong spiking system is therefore evaluated by both where it lands and how quickly it gets there.
