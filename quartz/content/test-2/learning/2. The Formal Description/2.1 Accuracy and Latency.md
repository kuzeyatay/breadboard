### Accuracy and Latency

Once a spiking network has received an input stimulus and produced a decision, two different questions immediately arise. Imagine a small gesture-recognition system watching an event-based camera feed. A hand moves, the camera sends a stream of change events, spikes move through the network, and the model eventually chooses a label such as "swipe left" or "circle." One kind of success asks whether the chosen label matches the real gesture. Another kind of success asks how long the system took to choose. A robot arm, a hearing aid, or an edge sensor needs both ideas, because a correct answer that arrives too late can be unusable, while a fast answer that is often wrong cannot be trusted.

Accuracy measures the correctness side of this story. Each time the model receives an input stimulus, it makes a prediction. A **prediction** is the model's chosen output, such as a class label. A prediction becomes a **correct prediction** when that chosen output matches the true answer for that input. If the system sees 100 gesture samples and labels 98 of them correctly, then it made 98 correct predictions. The **total predictions** count is the full number of predictions being evaluated, including both correct and incorrect ones.

That gives the accuracy formula:
$$
\text{Accuracy}=\frac{\text{Number of correct predictions}}{\text{Total number of predictions}}
$$
Here, the **number of correct predictions** is how many decisions match the true labels, and the **total number of predictions** is how many decisions the model made overall. The fraction tells you what portion of the model's decisions were right. If you want a percentage, multiply the fraction by 100.

For example, suppose a surrogate-gradient spiking neural network classifies 1,000 event-based inputs and gets 978 of them right. Its accuracy is
$$
\text{Accuracy}=\frac{978}{1000}=0.978=97.8\%
$$
The number 97.8% means that, over this evaluation set, about 978 out of every 1,000 decisions were correct. It says nothing by itself about how quickly those decisions arrived. A model can have strong accuracy while still requiring a long simulation window before enough spikes accumulate to support a confident decision.

Latency measures the timing side of the same process. A spiking system unfolds over time: the input stimulus begins, neurons emit spikes, downstream neurons integrate those spikes, and the model eventually reaches a decision. **Latency** is the time between the input stimulus and the model's decision. The **input stimulus time** is the moment the relevant input begins, such as the first event from a gesture or the start of a sound sample. The **decision time** is the moment the model produces its output label or reaches its decision threshold.

That gives the latency formula:
$$
\text{Latency}=t_{\text{decision}}-t_{\text{stimulus}}
$$
Here, $t_{\text{stimulus}}$ is the time when the input stimulus begins, and $t_{\text{decision}}$ is the time when the model makes its decision. The subtraction computes the elapsed response time. If the stimulus begins at $0$ milliseconds and the model decides at $10$ milliseconds, then the latency is
$$
10\text{ ms}-0\text{ ms}=10\text{ ms}
$$
A latency of 10 ms means the model took ten thousandths of a second to respond after the input began. In event-based and neuromorphic settings, this number is often central because the world keeps changing while the model computes. Gesture recognition, robotics, and edge AI systems gain practical value from responses in the range of a few to tens of milliseconds.

Accuracy and latency therefore measure different kinds of success in the same decision loop. Accuracy asks, "Was the decision right?" Latency asks, "How quickly did the decision arrive after the stimulus began?" A directly trained surrogate-gradient SNN can reach competitive accuracy around 97.8% while responding in about 10 ms. An STDP-based SNN can use very low energy and stabilize under optimized conditions with sub-15 ms latency, while sometimes giving lower accuracy such as about 95.5%. A converted SNN can preserve much of the behavior of an artificial neural network, but it may need a longer simulation window, leading to delays around 20 ms. These numbers illustrate how the two measures separate correctness from speed.

A useful way to picture the difference is to imagine a finish line and an answer sheet. Accuracy grades the answer sheet: did the model write the right label? Latency times the race: how long did the model take to write it? The same model evaluation can give both measurements. If a model classifies 500 samples, you can count how many labels were correct to compute accuracy, and you can record the elapsed time from each stimulus to each decision to compute latency. The two measurements then describe two axes of behavior rather than one blended score.

**Question.** If a model reaches 99% accuracy but usually waits 50 ms before deciding, is it always better than a model with 97.8% accuracy and 10 ms latency?

**Answer.** The better choice depends on the application. For an offline task where timing is relaxed, 99% accuracy may be preferred. For a real-time sensor, robot, or neuromorphic edge device, the 10 ms model may be more useful because it responds quickly while still making highly reliable predictions. Accuracy measures correctness; latency measures response speed.

A beginner confusion often comes from treating latency as though it were another name for low error. A low-latency model is fast, and a high-accuracy model is correct often; those are separate properties. A model can decide quickly by using early spikes, but those early spikes may contain limited evidence. Another model can wait for more spikes to accumulate, which may improve confidence while increasing delay. In spiking neural networks, this tradeoff appears naturally because information is carried through spike timing and spike counts over a simulation window. Shorter windows can produce earlier decisions. Longer windows can allow more evidence to arrive.

This distinction becomes especially clear when comparing spiking systems with conventional CNN-based artificial neural networks on event-based tasks. SNNs deployed on neuromorphic platforms can show inference latencies around 5-10 ms per sample, while CNN-based ANNs often require about 20-50 ms depending on model complexity and hardware. The shorter latency comes from using sparse event-driven computation: when the input changes, spikes carry information forward, and the system can respond without repeatedly processing dense arrays at every moment. In practical terms, a gesture-recognition sensor can begin forming a decision as soon as meaningful events arrive.

**Question.** Why does latency use $t_{\text{decision}}-t_{\text{stimulus}}$ instead of just recording $t_{\text{decision}}$?

**Answer.** The decision time alone only tells when the model decided on a clock. Latency needs the elapsed time after the input began. Subtracting $t_{\text{stimulus}}$ from $t_{\text{decision}}$ removes the arbitrary starting point of the clock and leaves the response duration. If one stimulus begins at 100 ms and the decision occurs at 110 ms, the latency is still 10 ms.

Together, accuracy and latency turn one model output into a richer description of behavior. The input stimulus starts the process, spikes carry evidence through time, the model reaches a decision, and evaluation asks two linked questions: how often the decision matches the truth, and how much time passes before that decision appears. Accuracy gives the reliability of the answers across many predictions. Latency gives the speed of each response from stimulus to decision. A strong mental model is to see every prediction as both an answer and an event in time: the answer can be right or wrong, and the event can arrive early or late.
