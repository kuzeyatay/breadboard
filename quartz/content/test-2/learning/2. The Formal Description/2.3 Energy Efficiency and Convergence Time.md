## Energy Efficiency and Convergence Time

By now, accuracy tells us how often a spiking neural network makes the right decision, and energy consumption tells us how much physical work the system spends to make that decision. Imagine choosing a model for a small battery-powered vision sensor. One model recognizes objects correctly very often, but it burns through energy quickly. Another model makes slightly more mistakes, but it can run for much longer on the same battery. Accuracy alone cannot tell you which model is more useful, because the useful model depends on both what it gets right and what it costs to get there.

A simple way to combine those two ideas is to ask: how much accuracy do we get per unit of energy? This gives the **normalized energy efficiency** metric:
$$
\eta_E = \frac{A}{E}
$$
Here, $\eta_E$ is the normalized energy efficiency, $A$ is the model's accuracy, and $E$ is the energy consumption. Accuracy $A$ measures the fraction or percentage of predictions that are correct. Energy consumption $E$ measures how much energy the model uses, often per inference, such as millijoules per classification. The formula lets you compute a "usefulness per energy cost" score: a larger value means the model produces more correct performance for each unit of energy it spends.

For example, suppose a surrogate-gradient SNN reaches $97.8\%$ accuracy and uses about $15$ mJ per inference. If we write accuracy as a percentage, its normalized energy efficiency is
$$
\eta_E = \frac{97.8}{15} \approx 6.52
$$
That number means the model gives about $6.52$ percentage-points of accuracy per millijoule. Now compare an STDP-based SNN with about $95.5\%$ accuracy and $5$ mJ per inference:
$$
\eta_E = \frac{95.5}{5} = 19.1
$$
The STDP-based model has lower accuracy, but its efficiency score is much higher because it spends far less energy. This is the kind of situation where a single metric changes how you reason about a model. If the task is a medical diagnosis system where every extra fraction of accuracy is valuable, the more accurate model may be preferred. If the task is a remote sensor that must operate for months, the lower-energy model may be the better engineering choice.

A beginner confusion often appears here: if one model has the best normalized energy efficiency, does that make it the best model overall?

**Question.** If an STDP-based SNN has much better normalized energy efficiency than a surrogate-gradient SNN, should it always be chosen?

**Answer.** No. Normalized energy efficiency answers one specific question: how much accuracy is obtained per unit of energy. It does not include every deployment requirement. A surrogate-gradient SNN can be preferable when the application needs higher classification accuracy, lower latency, or faster training. The efficiency metric is powerful because it exposes the energy tradeoff, but the final choice still depends on the task.

Energy efficiency describes usefulness after or during inference, when the trained model is making predictions. Training introduces a different kind of cost: time. A model can look attractive at the end of training, but if it takes too long to reach good performance, it may be difficult to use in situations where models must adapt quickly. This is especially relevant for spiking systems because different training strategies can learn at very different rates. Surrogate-gradient SNNs can reduce training loss quickly and stabilize around the twentieth epoch, while STDP-based SNNs can require more epochs and may settle at a higher loss value.

An **epoch** is one complete pass through the training data. If a model trains for 20 epochs, it has had 20 full opportunities to adjust its parameters using the available training examples. To measure training speed, we choose a **target accuracy**, meaning the accuracy level we consider good enough for the task. Then we ask for the earliest epoch where the model reaches that target. This gives **convergence time**.

![Convergence time as minimum epoch reaching target accuracy](/test-2/assets/source-visuals/2510-27379v1-page-6-equation-e6-convergence-time-as-minimum-epoch-reaching-targe.png)

*Convergence time as minimum epoch reaching target accuracy* *(p. 6)*

The equation shown here defines convergence time as a minimum: it searches through the training epochs and selects the earliest one where accuracy reaches the target level. The word "minimum" carries the main meaning. If a model reaches the target at epoch 12, drops slightly, and later reaches it again at epoch 18, the convergence time is still 12, because epoch 12 was the first success. The metric is about how quickly the training method becomes good enough, not about the final accuracy after every possible epoch has been used.

We can write the idea in words as:
$$
T_{\text{conv}} = \min \{t : A(t) \ge A_{\text{target}}\}
$$
Here, $T_{\text{conv}}$ is the convergence time, $t$ is an epoch number, $A(t)$ is the accuracy measured after epoch $t$, and $A_{\text{target}}$ is the target accuracy. The expression inside the braces means "all epochs $t$ where the model's accuracy has reached or exceeded the target." The $\min$ operation then picks the smallest such epoch. The formula lets you compute the earliest training point where the model becomes acceptable for the chosen goal.

Suppose the target accuracy is $95\%$. A surrogate-gradient SNN might reach $95\%$ by epoch 14 and continue improving toward about $97.8\%$. Its convergence time for that target is 14 epochs. An STDP-based SNN might reach $95\%$ at epoch 28, or it might approach the target more slowly and stabilize near a lower value depending on the setup. If it reaches $95\%$ at epoch 28, its convergence time is 28 epochs. Both models may eventually become usable, but the first one becomes usable sooner.

**Question.** Does convergence time measure how energy-efficient the trained model is during inference?

**Answer.** Convergence time measures training speed: how many epochs are needed to reach a target accuracy. It does not measure inference energy. A model can converge quickly but use more energy per inference, or converge slowly while being very energy-efficient once trained. That is why convergence time and normalized energy efficiency answer different questions.

The two metrics work together because SNN usefulness has more than one dimension. Normalized energy efficiency asks how much accuracy the model delivers for each unit of energy after it is operating. Convergence time asks how quickly training reaches an acceptable accuracy. A surrogate-gradient SNN can look strong when fast adaptation and high accuracy are needed, because it can approach ANN-like accuracy while stabilizing relatively quickly. An STDP-based SNN can look strong when energy is scarce, because its sparse spike activity can reduce energy consumption dramatically, even if training progresses more slowly and final accuracy is slightly lower.

A good mental model is to imagine two clocks running at different stages. During training, the epoch clock measures how soon the model reaches a target level of competence; convergence time reads that clock. During deployment, the energy meter measures how much it costs to make each prediction; normalized energy efficiency reads that meter while keeping accuracy attached to the cost. A practical SNN is judged by both readings: how quickly it becomes useful, and how efficiently it stays useful once it is making decisions.
