## Energy Efficiency and Convergence Time

Once accuracy, energy consumption, latency, spike count, and training progress are on the table, a practical question appears: if two spiking neural networks both classify inputs reasonably well, which one is more useful for a real device that must learn, respond, and conserve power? Imagine a small camera on a battery-powered robot. The robot needs a model that recognizes what it sees, but every inference uses energy, and every training epoch takes time before the model becomes reliable. Accuracy alone tells you how often the model is right. Energy consumption tells you how costly each decision is. Convergence behavior tells you how long training must continue before the model reaches a useful level of performance. A deployment-ready comparison needs metrics that tie these ideas together.

A simple way to connect correctness and cost is to ask how much accuracy the model delivers per unit of energy. This gives the normalized energy efficiency:
$$
\eta = \frac{A}{E}
$$
Here, $\eta$ is the normalized energy efficiency, $A$ is the model's accuracy, and $E$ is the energy consumption per inference. Accuracy $A$ measures the fraction or percentage of inputs classified correctly. Energy consumption $E$ measures how much energy the model spends to process one input, commonly reported in millijoules per inference. The formula lets you compute a "usefulness per energy cost" score: larger $\eta$ means the model gives more correct performance for each unit of energy it spends.

For example, suppose one SNN reaches $97.8\%$ accuracy while using $15$ mJ per inference. Its efficiency score is
$$
\eta = \frac{97.8}{15} \approx 6.52
$$
If another SNN reaches $95.5\%$ accuracy while using $5$ mJ per inference, its score is
$$
\eta = \frac{95.5}{5} = 19.1
$$
The second model has lower raw accuracy, but it delivers much more accuracy per millijoule. That interpretation fits energy-constrained settings such as sensors, wearable devices, and neuromorphic hardware, where a small accuracy reduction may be acceptable if the energy savings are large. STDP-based SNNs illustrate this pattern: they can use around $5$ mJ per inference with sparse activity, while surrogate-gradient SNNs may use a moderate amount of energy, around $15$ mJ per inference, while achieving higher accuracy. The efficiency metric makes that tradeoff visible in one number.

A beginner can easily mix up "more accurate" with "more efficient," because both sound like better performance. The formula separates them. A highly accurate model can have a lower $\eta$ if it spends much more energy to get that accuracy. A slightly less accurate model can have a higher $\eta$ if it reaches its performance with very little energy. The metric does not replace accuracy; it tells you how expensive that accuracy is.

**Question.** If Model A has $98\%$ accuracy and uses $20$ mJ per inference, while Model B has $96\%$ accuracy and uses $8$ mJ per inference, which model is more energy efficient?

**Answer.** Model A has $\eta = 98/20 = 4.9$. Model B has $\eta = 96/8 = 12$. Model B is more energy efficient because each millijoule buys more correct classification performance, even though Model A has higher raw accuracy.

Energy efficiency describes usefulness after a model is running, but training introduces another kind of cost. A model may eventually become accurate, yet take many epochs to get there. An epoch is one full pass through the training data. If a model reaches a useful accuracy after fewer epochs, it trains faster and becomes deployable sooner. This is measured by convergence time.

The convergence time is defined as the earliest epoch at which the model reaches a chosen target accuracy:

![Convergence time as minimum epoch reaching target accuracy](/test-2/assets/source-visuals/2510-27379v1-page-6-equation-e6-convergence-time-as-minimum-epoch-reaching-targe.png)

*Convergence time as minimum epoch reaching target accuracy* *(p. 6)*

The equation uses the idea of a minimum epoch. The minimum epoch is the smallest training epoch number that satisfies the condition you care about. The target accuracy is the accuracy level chosen as "good enough" for the task, such as $95\%$, $97\%$, or another threshold set by the application. If $A(e)$ means the accuracy measured at epoch $e$, and $A_{\text{target}}$ means the target accuracy, then convergence time asks for the smallest $e$ such that
$$
A(e) \geq A_{\text{target}}
$$
This gives a concrete answer to the training-speed question: how many epochs are needed before the model first becomes useful by the chosen standard?

Suppose a surrogate-gradient SNN reaches $95\%$ accuracy by epoch $12$, $97\%$ by epoch $18$, and then stabilizes around epoch $20$. If the target accuracy is $97\%$, the convergence time is $18$ epochs, because epoch $18$ is the first epoch where the model reaches the target. If an STDP-based SNN reaches only $94\%$ by epoch $20$, then crosses $95\%$ at epoch $35$, its convergence time for a $95\%$ target is $35$ epochs. The STDP-based model may still be very energy efficient during inference, but its convergence time says that training reaches the chosen accuracy more slowly.

This distinction is especially useful for SNNs because different training strategies trade off learning speed and deployment cost. Surrogate-gradient training often reaches strong accuracy quickly, with convergence around the early tens of epochs and inference latency around the sub-15 ms range. Converted SNNs can preserve competitive accuracy, but they may require longer simulation windows and higher spike counts. STDP-based SNNs can be extremely energy efficient, with low spike counts and low per-inference energy, while often converging more slowly and plateauing at lower accuracy. A single accuracy number would flatten these differences. Energy efficiency and convergence time keep two separate questions visible: how costly is each trained inference, and how quickly does training reach the target?

**Question.** If a model reaches $90\%$ accuracy at epoch $8$, $95\%$ at epoch $15$, and $97\%$ at epoch $24$, what is its convergence time for a $95\%$ target?

**Answer.** Its convergence time is $15$ epochs. The target is $95\%$, and epoch $15$ is the first epoch where the model reaches that level. The later $97\%$ result shows continued improvement, but it does not change the convergence time for the $95\%$ target.

The two metrics work together because usefulness has both an after-training side and a during-training side. Normalized energy efficiency looks at the trained model as a device making decisions: it compares the accuracy gained to the energy spent per inference. Convergence time looks at the learning process: it finds the earliest epoch when accuracy becomes acceptable. A model with high $\eta$ is attractive when energy is scarce. A model with low convergence time is attractive when training speed, adaptation, or rapid deployment matters. Thinking with both metrics gives a cleaner mental model of SNN evaluation: accuracy says how correct the model is, energy says how expensive each decision is, normalized energy efficiency combines those two into accuracy per energy cost, and convergence time says how quickly training reaches the chosen standard of correctness.
