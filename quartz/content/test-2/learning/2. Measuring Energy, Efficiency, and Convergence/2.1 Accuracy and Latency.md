### Accuracy and Latency

Imagine a small spiking vision system watching the entrance to a room. Most of the time, the scene is quiet. Then a person steps through the doorway, the sensor produces a pattern of spike events, and the network must decide what it is seeing: person, pet, shadow, or empty doorway. Two different questions immediately appear. Did the network choose the right label? And how long did it take to choose? Accuracy answers the first question. Latency answers the second. A useful SNN evaluation needs both because a correct decision that arrives too late can fail in a time-sensitive setting, while a fast decision that is often wrong cannot be trusted.

A prediction is the output decision made by the model for one input stimulus. The stimulus is the presented input, such as an image frame, an event-camera pattern, or a short spike sequence produced by a sensor. In a classification task, each stimulus usually has a correct target label. When the network's predicted label matches that target label, the prediction is counted as correct. Accuracy turns that counting process into a single rate:
$$
\text{Accuracy} = \frac{\text{Number of correct predictions}}{\text{Total number of predictions}}
$$
The numerator, the number of correct predictions, counts how many times the model chose the right answer. The denominator, the total number of predictions, counts how many test cases the model attempted. The fraction tells you the share of attempts that were correct. If the result is multiplied by $100$, the same quantity is expressed as a percentage.

For example, suppose an SNN classifies $1{,}000$ sensor inputs and gets $955$ of them right. Its accuracy is
$$
\text{Accuracy} = \frac{955}{1000} = 0.955 = 95.5\%
$$
This means that, across the evaluated inputs, about 96 out of every 100 predictions match the correct label. The number is easy to interpret because it comes directly from measurable outcomes: count the successes, count all attempts, and divide. In SNN comparisons, accuracies around the mid-to-high 90% range can already indicate strong task performance, especially when the model also gains advantages from sparse spiking activity or low power use. A surrogate-gradient SNN, for instance, may reach accuracy close to a conventional neural network while still using spike-based computation, which makes accuracy a useful first check of whether the spiking model has learned the task well enough.

Accuracy becomes less complete when the task has a deadline. Return to the doorway system. If the network correctly identifies a person after $10$ milliseconds, that may be fast enough for a responsive device. If it reaches the same correct answer only after a long simulation window, the decision may arrive too late for a robot, drone, or neuromorphic edge device that must react in real time. This is where latency enters.

Latency measures the delay between when the stimulus is presented and when the network makes its decision. The stimulus time is the moment the input becomes available to the model. The decision time is the moment the model has produced its output decision. The latency formula is
$$
\text{Latency} = \text{Decision time} - \text{Stimulus time}
$$
Here, latency is the elapsed time between input and output. If the stimulus appears at $t = 0$ milliseconds and the model decides at $t = 10$ milliseconds, then
$$
\text{Latency} = 10\text{ ms} - 0\text{ ms} = 10\text{ ms}
$$
The formula lets you compute how long the model took to respond. In an SNN, that delay often reflects how much spike evidence the network needs before its output becomes stable enough to count as a decision. Some SNNs can decide quickly because their training shapes useful spike patterns directly. Others, especially converted SNNs that imitate a conventional network through rate-coded spikes, may need longer simulation windows because the model waits for enough spikes to accumulate before the output represents the intended value clearly.

A helpful way to think about latency is to imagine listening for evidence in a dark room. One sharp sound may be enough to tell you that a glass fell. A faint pattern of sounds may require several moments before you can tell whether it is rain, footsteps, or static. The SNN faces a similar timing problem: each spike is a discrete event, and the model may need a short sequence of events before the output neuron or output population carries a reliable decision. A latency near $10$ milliseconds means the model reaches its decision quickly after the stimulus begins; a longer delay means the model waits through more time before producing its answer.

**Question.** If two SNNs both reach $97\%$ accuracy, should they be treated as equally successful?

**Answer.** They have the same correctness rate on the evaluated predictions, but they may differ greatly in usefulness. If one reaches its decisions in about $10$ milliseconds and the other requires a much longer simulation window, the faster model is better suited to real-time tasks. Accuracy tells how often the model is right; latency tells how quickly those answers arrive.

Accuracy and latency can move independently. A model can become more accurate by waiting longer for more spike evidence, but that extra waiting increases latency. A model can decide very quickly, but if it decides before enough evidence has arrived, accuracy may suffer. The best choice depends on the job. A medical monitoring device, a mobile robot, or an event-based camera system may need low latency because the world changes while the model is still computing. A slower offline analysis system may tolerate more delay if the extra time improves correctness.

This separation also explains why SNN performance is usually evaluated across several metrics rather than by accuracy alone. Accuracy reports whether the model's predictions match the desired labels. Latency reports whether those predictions arrive soon enough to be useful. When a surrogate-gradient SNN reaches high accuracy with latency around $10$ milliseconds, the combination suggests that the model has learned spike patterns that support both reliable and fast decisions. When a converted SNN remains competitive in accuracy but needs a longer simulation window, the accuracy number still shows task learning, while the latency number reveals the cost of waiting for rate-coded spike activity to settle.

A beginner confusion often comes from treating latency as another kind of accuracy score. Latency has a different unit and a different meaning. Accuracy is a fraction or percentage based on counts of predictions. Latency is a time duration, often measured in milliseconds. A high accuracy value is usually desirable; a high latency value usually means slower response. So $97\%$ accuracy and $10$ milliseconds latency describe two separate dimensions of performance: correctness and delay.

**Question.** A model sees $500$ inputs, predicts $475$ correctly, receives each stimulus at $0$ ms, and usually decides at $20$ ms. What do its accuracy and latency say?

**Answer.** Its accuracy is $\frac{475}{500} = 0.95 = 95\%$, so it gets 95 out of every 100 predictions correct on average. Its latency is $20\text{ ms} - 0\text{ ms} = 20\text{ ms}$, so its typical decision delay is 20 milliseconds after the stimulus begins. The model is fairly accurate, and whether it is fast enough depends on the timing demands of the application.

The mental model is simple: accuracy starts after many decisions have been made and asks, "What fraction were correct?" Latency starts at a single stimulus and asks, "How long did this decision take?" Together they separate success into two measurable parts. A spiking model must produce the right output, and it must produce that output on a useful timescale. Accuracy counts correctness across predictions; latency measures the time path from stimulus to decision.
