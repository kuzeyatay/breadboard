## Convergence Time

After measuring accuracy, latency, energy, and spike count, one practical question remains: how long does training take before a spiking neural network becomes useful? Imagine training several SNNs for the same classification task. During early epochs, each model is still adjusting its weights, so its predictions may be unreliable. After more epochs, one model may reach a dependable accuracy quickly, while another may keep improving slowly for a long time. If both eventually become accurate, the faster one can still be more useful when training time, experimentation time, or adaptation speed matters.

An **epoch** is one full pass through the training data. If a dataset contains many labeled examples, then one epoch means the model has had one opportunity to learn from every example in that training set. After each epoch, we can measure **training accuracy**, which is the fraction of training examples the model classifies correctly at that point. Training accuracy usually changes over epochs because the model's weights are being updated. Early values may be low, later values may be higher, and eventually the curve may stabilize when additional training gives only small improvements.

To decide when a model has become "good enough," we set a **target accuracy threshold**. This threshold is a chosen accuracy level that represents usefulness for the task. For example, if a model must classify at least 95 out of every 100 training examples correctly before we trust it, then the target accuracy is 95%. The target is not discovered by the formula; it is chosen from the needs of the problem. A relaxed prototype might use 90%, while a more demanding classification task might require 98% or higher.

Once the target is chosen, **convergence time** measures the earliest epoch at which the model reaches that target. The word "convergence" here refers to training moving toward a satisfactory level of performance. The measurement is simple because it comes from counts we already track: epoch number and accuracy at each epoch. If accuracy is recorded after every epoch, convergence time is the first epoch where the recorded accuracy is at least the chosen target.

A compact way to write this is:
$$
T_c = \min \{ e : A(e) \ge A_{\text{target}} \}
$$
Here, $T_c$ is the **convergence time**, measured in epochs. The symbol $e$ represents an epoch number, such as 1, 2, 3, and so on. The function $A(e)$ means the training accuracy measured after epoch $e$. The value $A_{\text{target}}$ is the target accuracy threshold. The expression $\min$ means "choose the smallest one." So the formula says: look through the epochs, find every epoch where the model's accuracy has reached or passed the target, and report the smallest epoch number in that set.

For a concrete example, suppose an SNN has training accuracies of 72%, 84%, 91%, 95%, and 96% after epochs 1 through 5. If the target accuracy is 95%, then the model first reaches the target at epoch 4. Its convergence time is therefore $T_c = 4$ epochs. The accuracy at epoch 5 is also above the target, but convergence time asks for the earliest useful moment, so epoch 5 does not change the answer.

This measurement separates two ideas that are easy to blend together: how good a model eventually becomes and how quickly it becomes useful. A training method might reach 96% accuracy by epoch 20, while another reaches 95% by epoch 8 and then improves only slightly. If the task only needs 95%, the second method has the shorter convergence time even if the first method later edges ahead. In SNN comparisons, this distinction is especially useful because different learning approaches can have different training behaviors. Surrogate gradient-trained SNNs can approach competitive accuracy quickly and stabilize by about the 20th epoch, while STDP-based SNNs can be slower to stabilize even when they offer advantages in sparse spiking and low energy use. Convergence time gives a direct way to describe that training-speed difference without reducing the whole comparison to final accuracy.

A useful mental image is a runner crossing a qualifying line. Final accuracy is like the runner's eventual finishing position, but convergence time is the moment the runner first crosses the qualifying mark. If the application only needs the model to qualify, then reaching the line early is valuable. In training, that early crossing can mean fewer epochs, fewer weight updates, and a shorter path from an untrained model to a usable one.

**Question.** If two SNN training methods both reach 97% accuracy, why might the one with shorter convergence time be preferred?

**Answer.** The method with shorter convergence time reaches the required level of usefulness in fewer epochs. If the target accuracy is 95%, a model that reaches 95% by epoch 10 becomes usable earlier than one that reaches 95% by epoch 20, even if both later reach 97%. This can make experimentation faster and can be especially helpful when models must adapt quickly.

The target accuracy threshold controls the meaning of the convergence-time number. Suppose the same model reaches 90% at epoch 5, 95% at epoch 12, and 97% at epoch 18. If the target is 90%, then $T_c = 5$. If the target is 95%, then $T_c = 12$. If the target is 97%, then $T_c = 18$. The model did not change; the definition of "useful enough" changed. That is why convergence time should always be reported together with the target accuracy threshold. Saying "the model converged in 12 epochs" is incomplete unless the accuracy target is known.

A beginner confusion often comes from thinking convergence time means the model has stopped learning. The formula does not require training to stop changing. It only asks when the model first reaches the chosen accuracy level. A model can continue improving after convergence time has been reached. If it crosses 95% at epoch 12 and reaches 98% at epoch 30, the convergence time for a 95% target is still 12 epochs. The later improvement belongs to final or peak accuracy, while convergence time belongs to early usefulness.

**Question.** What happens if the model never reaches the target accuracy during the measured training run?

**Answer.** Then convergence time is undefined for that run and that target, because there is no epoch $e$ where $A(e) \ge A_{\text{target}}$. In practice, the result should be reported as "not reached within the measured epochs," such as "did not reach 95% within 20 epochs." That statement is more precise than forcing a number that the measurements do not support.

Convergence time fits naturally into a broader evaluation of SNNs because training speed is one dimension of usefulness. Accuracy tells us how well the model performs, latency tells us how quickly it responds during inference, energy and spike count describe how costly that response is, and convergence time tells us how many training passes are needed before the model reaches a chosen standard. The whole measurement rests on a simple chain: record accuracy after each epoch, choose the target threshold, find the first epoch that meets it, and interpret that earliest crossing as the training method's speed toward usefulness.
