### Energy and Energy Efficiency

Imagine choosing a neural model for a small edge device that must recognize images while running on a limited battery. Accuracy alone tells you how often the model is correct, and spike count tells you how many discrete events the spiking network produces during an inference. Those two measurements now need to meet a practical question: how much useful correctness do you get for the energy spent each time the model makes a prediction?

An inference is one complete forward use of the model: one input goes in, the network processes it, and one prediction comes out. During that inference, an SNN spends energy mainly when events cause computation. A spike has a cost because generating and routing a spike consumes energy. A synaptic operation also has a cost because a spike arriving at a connection must be processed through that connection. The total energy metric turns these pieces into one measurable quantity, so the model can be compared by what it spends per prediction rather than only by how accurate it is.

A compact way to express this is:
$$
E_{\text{total}} = E_{\text{spike}} + E_{\text{synop}}
$$
Here, $E_{\text{total}}$ is the total energy consumed during one inference. $E_{\text{spike}}$ is the energy associated with spike-related activity, meaning the cost of producing and communicating spikes. $E_{\text{synop}}$ is the energy associated with synaptic operations, meaning the cost of processing spike events across connections. The formula lets you compute a single energy cost for a model run by adding the energy spent on spiking activity and the energy spent on synaptic processing.

The intuition is simple: if a network fires more spikes, it tends to create more event traffic; if those spikes pass through many connections, they create more synaptic work. A model with sparse activity can therefore spend less energy because fewer events need to be generated, moved, and processed. This is why spike count becomes more than a descriptive statistic. Spike count helps explain where energy goes.

A useful worked interpretation comes from comparing models by energy per inference. A conventional ANN or CNN can reach very high accuracy, but its continuous-valued operations lead to much higher energy use, around $200\text{ mJ}$ per inference in the reported comparison. A converted SNN lowers that cost to about $20\text{ mJ}$, while still producing a relatively large number of spikes, around $20{,}000$ per inference, because rate-coded propagation represents values through many spike events. A surrogate-gradient SNN lowers energy further to about $15\text{ mJ}$ and reduces spike activity to about $12{,}000$ spikes by learning spiking patterns more directly. An STDP-based SNN reaches about $5\text{ mJ}$ per inference and about $4{,}000$ spikes, giving the sparsest activity and the lowest energy use among the compared models.

Those numbers show how the energy formula becomes a measurable claim. The STDP-based model spends less total energy because its spike-related and synaptic-operation costs are lower during inference. The surrogate-gradient model spends more energy than the STDP-based model, but it can preserve higher accuracy. The converted SNN improves substantially over the ANN, yet its larger spike count keeps its energy cost above the more spike-efficient SNN methods. Energy therefore follows the activity pattern of the model: fewer spikes and fewer resulting synaptic operations usually mean lower energy per inference.

A common beginner confusion is to treat low spike count as automatically equivalent to the best model. Spike count is one driver of energy, but the evaluation still needs accuracy. A network that saves energy by producing very little useful activity may become less correct. A practical SNN is judged by both sides together: it should spend little energy while still making reliable predictions.

That leads to normalized energy efficiency. Once total energy has been measured, the next question is how much accuracy the model achieves for that energy cost. The efficiency metric is written as:
$$
\eta = \frac{\text{Accuracy}}{E_{\text{total}}}
$$
Here, $\eta$ is normalized energy efficiency. $\text{Accuracy}$ is the model's correctness score, such as classification accuracy expressed as a percentage or as a decimal fraction. $E_{\text{total}}$ is the energy consumed per inference. The ratio tells you how much accuracy is obtained per unit of energy. If accuracy is written as a percentage and energy is measured in millijoules, then $\eta$ can be read as "percentage points of accuracy per millijoule." If accuracy is written as a decimal, then $\eta$ is "fraction of correctness per millijoule." The units depend on how accuracy is represented, but the comparison logic stays the same.

For example, suppose one model reaches $99.2\%$ accuracy while consuming $200\text{ mJ}$ per inference. Its efficiency is:
$$
\eta = \frac{99.2}{200} = 0.496
$$
That means the model gives about $0.496$ percentage points of accuracy per millijoule. Now suppose an STDP-based SNN reaches $95.5\%$ accuracy while consuming $5\text{ mJ}$ per inference. Its efficiency is:
$$
\eta = \frac{95.5}{5} = 19.1
$$
This model gives $19.1$ percentage points of accuracy per millijoule. The accuracy is slightly lower, but the energy cost is dramatically lower, so the accuracy-per-energy ratio is much higher. This is the kind of comparison normalized energy efficiency is designed to reveal.

**Question.** If a model has the highest accuracy, does it automatically have the highest normalized energy efficiency?

**Answer.** No. Normalized energy efficiency depends on the ratio $\text{Accuracy}/E_{\text{total}}$. A highly accurate model can have low efficiency if it consumes a large amount of energy per inference. A slightly less accurate model can have much higher efficiency when its energy consumption is far smaller.

The ratio also explains why application goals change which model looks best. A surrogate-gradient SNN can balance high accuracy, low latency, and moderate energy use, making it attractive when correctness and fast response both matter. An STDP-based SNN can be preferable in ultra-low-power settings because its energy cost is especially small, even when its accuracy is slightly reduced. A converted SNN may be useful when transferring from an ANN-style model is convenient, though its spike count and simulation demands can keep energy higher than more directly trained spiking approaches.

Energy efficiency therefore links three ideas from earlier measurements. Accuracy measures how well the model predicts. Spike count and synaptic operations describe how much event-driven work the model performs. Total energy combines the costs of that work during inference. Normalized energy efficiency then asks how much correctness is purchased with that energy. A strong mental model is to picture each inference as a budgeted event process: spikes are the messages, synaptic operations are the places where messages do work, total energy is the bill for that activity, and energy efficiency is the amount of correct prediction obtained for each unit of that bill.

**Question.** Why does the energy metric include both spike cost and synaptic operation cost?

**Answer.** A spike consumes energy when it is generated and communicated, and it can also trigger processing at synaptic connections. Counting only spikes would miss part of the computational work caused by those spikes. Adding spike-related cost and synaptic-operation cost gives a fuller estimate of the energy consumed during one inference.
