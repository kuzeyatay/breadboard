### Latency as Time to Decision

Imagine a small robot rolling toward a doorway. Its camera receives a new stimulus: the doorway is open, blocked, or partly blocked. The robot can conserve energy by using sparse spike events, and it can eventually make an accurate decision, but usefulness also depends on when that decision arrives. A perfect decision that comes after the robot has already reached the doorway is too late for control. Latency gives a way to measure this timing pressure directly: it asks how much time passes between the arrival of the stimulus and the model's decision.

A spiking neural network computes through time because spikes occur at particular moments. When an input arrives, the network does not instantly hold one finished value in every layer. Spikes propagate, neuron states change, and evidence accumulates until the output becomes clear enough to choose a class or action. That makes time part of the computation itself. Latency measures the delay from the beginning of that process to the moment the system produces its decision.

The metric begins with two events. The **stimulus time** is the moment the input becomes available to the system. If a sensor frame, event stream, or signal begins at time 0, then the stimulus time is the starting point for measuring the response. The **decision time** is the moment the model has produced the decision being measured. In a classification setting, this could be the time when one output class wins clearly enough to be reported. In a robotic setting, it could be the time when the controller chooses "turn," "stop," or "move forward." Latency is the elapsed time between those two moments:
$$
\text{Latency} = t_{\text{decision}} - t_{\text{stimulus}}
$$
Here, $\text{Latency}$ is the time the model takes to turn an input into a decision. The symbol $t_{\text{decision}}$ means the decision time, or the clock time at which the model's output is ready. The symbol $t_{\text{stimulus}}$ means the stimulus time, or the clock time at which the input begins. The minus sign computes a **time difference**, which means "how much later one event happens than another." When this difference is measured in **milliseconds**, each unit is one thousandth of a second. A latency of 10 milliseconds means the decision arrives 0.010 seconds after the stimulus begins.

A concrete calculation makes the metric feel less abstract. Suppose a sensor detects a stimulus at $t_{\text{stimulus}} = 35$ ms, and the SNN produces its decision at $t_{\text{decision}} = 45$ ms. The latency is
$$
45\text{ ms} - 35\text{ ms} = 10\text{ ms}
$$
This number says that the model needed 10 milliseconds of temporal processing before the decision was available. If another model receives the same stimulus at 35 ms but decides at 70 ms, its latency is 35 ms. Both models may eventually choose the same answer, but the first one supports a faster response.

Latency defines exactly which quantities can change the metric. If the stimulus time stays fixed and the decision comes earlier, latency decreases. If the decision comes later, latency increases. If two experiments use different stimulus start times, the raw clock values alone do not tell the response speed; the subtraction is what matters. A decision at 110 ms sounds slower than a decision at 60 ms, but if the first stimulus began at 100 ms and the second began at 0 ms, the first latency is 10 ms while the second is 60 ms. Latency links the variables to a measurable claim by forcing every timing statement into the same form: when did the input begin, when did the decision happen, and what is the difference?

**Question.** If a model receives a stimulus at 120 ms and makes a decision at 132 ms, what is its latency?

**Answer.** The latency is $132\text{ ms} - 120\text{ ms} = 12\text{ ms}$. The model takes 12 milliseconds to turn that stimulus into a decision.

Real-time response depends on latency because the world keeps moving while the network computes. A real-time system must act within the time window allowed by its task. For a slowly changing monitoring task, tens or hundreds of milliseconds may be acceptable. For a fast control task, the useful window can be much shorter. A spiking model with latency as low as 10 milliseconds can be attractive for settings where temporal precision matters, because the decision can arrive quickly enough to guide an action while the situation is still current.

The phrase **real-time response** means that the model's decision arrives soon enough to be used in the ongoing situation. It does not mean the model is magically instantaneous. It means the latency fits the task. Think of a person catching a falling object. The eye receives changing visual information, the nervous system processes it, and the hand moves before the object passes by. The response is useful because the decision and action happen inside the available time. An SNN used at the edge of a system faces the same kind of constraint: a low-power device near a sensor may need to classify, detect, or control without waiting for a long remote computation.

A common beginner confusion is to treat latency as another name for accuracy. Accuracy asks whether the decision is correct. Latency asks when the decision becomes available. These two measurements answer different parts of deployment. A model that becomes accurate only after a long simulation window may be acceptable for offline analysis, where delay is tolerable. A model intended for real-time robotics or edge intelligence needs decisions that are both useful and timely. When surrogate-gradient-trained SNNs are described as reaching strong accuracy with low latency, the timing part means the network can settle on decisions quickly, not merely that its final answers are close to those of another model.

Latency also helps compare training approaches and model designs without hiding the temporal nature of SNN computation. A converted SNN may need a longer simulation window because information can be represented across many spikes over time. A surrogate-trained SNN can be shaped during training to produce useful spiking patterns sooner. An STDP-based SNN may emphasize sparse activity and low energy, while its decision timing must still be checked against the task's response window. The latency formula keeps this comparison grounded: regardless of the training method, the measurement is still the delay between stimulus and decision.

**Question.** Why can two models with the same final decision have different usefulness in a real-time task?

**Answer.** The models can make the same final decision at different times. If one model decides after 10 ms and another after 80 ms, the 10 ms model can support faster action. In a real-time task, the decision must arrive before the useful response window closes.

Latency turns temporal computation into an operational measurement. A stimulus starts the clock, spikes carry and transform evidence through the network, and a decision stops the clock for that trial. The formula $t_{\text{decision}} - t_{\text{stimulus}}$ gives the elapsed time between those two events, usually in milliseconds. Once that number is known, the model can be judged against the timing demands of the application: a low-latency SNN is one whose spikes lead to a decision quickly enough for the system to act while the input still matters.
