Imagine a spiking neural network has just watched a small image and must choose one label: "cat," "dog," "car," or "tree." For that one image, the evaluation is simple. If the correct label is "dog" and the network outputs "dog," the prediction counts as correct. If it outputs "cat," the prediction counts as incorrect. Accuracy begins from this plain counting idea: after many predictions, how often did the model choose the right class?

This measure is needed because classification produces many individual decisions, and a single success or failure gives too narrow a view. A model might guess one image correctly by luck, or miss one image even though it usually behaves well. To judge the model's usual behavior, we collect a batch of examples whose correct answers are already known. Each example has a target label, sometimes called the ground-truth label, and the model produces one predicted label. Accuracy compares those two labels example by example, then turns the count of matches into a percentage.

The mechanism has three parts. First, decide what counts as one prediction. In image classification, one prediction might be one image label. In a spoken-command task, one prediction might be one recognized word. In a sensor task, one prediction might be one event category. Second, count how many of those predictions match the correct answers. Third, divide by the total number of predictions so the result is a rate rather than a raw count. A raw count such as "92 correct" means different things depending on whether there were 100 examples or 1,000 examples. A rate makes the result comparable.

The accuracy metric is written as
$$
\text{Accuracy} = \frac{\text{number of correct predictions}}{\text{total number of predictions}} \times 100\%
$$
Here, the **number of correct predictions** is the count of cases where the predicted class equals the correct class. The **total number of predictions** is the full number of evaluated cases. The factor $100\%$ converts the fraction into a percentage, so a value like $0.95$ becomes $95\%$. This formula lets you compute the correct prediction rate: the share of decisions that were right.

Suppose a network classifies 200 test images. If 190 predicted labels match their correct labels, then
$$
\text{Accuracy} = \frac{190}{200} \times 100\% = 95\%
$$
The interpretation is direct: out of every 100 predictions made under similar test conditions, about 95 are expected to match the correct answer. The formula also shows exactly which quantities can change the metric. If the number of correct predictions rises while the total stays fixed, accuracy rises. If the total number of predictions rises while the number of correct predictions stays fixed, accuracy falls. If both change, the ratio decides the result. Accuracy is therefore a measurable claim about prediction correctness, tied to two counts that can be checked.

In spiking neural networks, this same metric still applies. Spikes may happen over time, and different SNN training methods may produce different spike patterns, but the classification result can still be judged by the final predicted label. A surrogate-gradient SNN that reaches about $97.8\%$ accuracy is being described by this same idea: most of its evaluated predictions match the correct labels. An STDP-based SNN with about $95.5\%$ accuracy is also being measured by the same rule: a slightly smaller share of its predictions match. The numbers are close enough that both systems may appear strong if correctness is the only thing being counted.

That is where accuracy becomes incomplete as a performance description. Accuracy measures correctness, and it stays silent about the cost of producing that correctness. A model can be highly accurate while using many spike events, consuming more energy per inference, taking longer to settle on an answer, or requiring more training time to reach stable behavior. In SNN evaluation, those missing dimensions are especially visible because spikes are events that can be counted, and event counts relate to energy use. For example, an ANN-style model may reach strong raw accuracy while consuming about $200$ mJ per inference. A converted SNN may reduce energy to around $20$ mJ but still produce roughly $20{,}000$ spikes per inference. A surrogate-gradient SNN may use about $15$ mJ and roughly $12{,}000$ spikes while keeping accuracy near the high end. An STDP-based SNN may use about $5$ mJ and around $4{,}000$ spikes, with accuracy around $95.5\%$. Accuracy alone sees only the correctness percentages; it does not record the different energy and spike-count paths taken to reach them.

A useful analogy is grading two students only by final answers. If both answer 95 out of 100 questions correctly, they have the same accuracy. One may have solved the test quickly with little scratch work, while the other may have taken far longer and filled many pages. The accuracy score captures the match between answers and answer key. It leaves the work process invisible. In the same way, classification accuracy captures the match between predicted labels and correct labels, while energy, spike count, latency, and convergence describe how the network got there.

**Question.** If two spiking neural networks both reach $96\%$ accuracy, are they equally good?

**Answer.** They are equally good on correct prediction rate for the evaluated task, because the same percentage of predictions matched the correct labels. They may still behave very differently in practice. One network could use fewer spikes, consume less energy, respond faster, or stabilize during training sooner. Accuracy answers "How often was the label right?" It does not answer "How costly was that answer?"

A common beginner confusion comes from treating a percentage as a complete score. A percentage feels final because it compresses many trials into one clean number. In classification, though, that number is deliberately narrow. It ignores confidence, timing, energy use, spike activity, and training behavior. If a network predicts 98 labels correctly out of 100, its accuracy is $98\%$, even if the two mistakes are severe, even if the network used a large number of spikes, and even if it took a long simulation window to make each decision. The metric was designed to count matching labels, so it should be read as a correctness measure.

Accuracy also depends on the evaluation set. If the test examples are easy, a high percentage may overstate how well the model will handle harder cases. If the test examples reflect the real task well, the percentage becomes more meaningful. The formula itself stays the same, but the interpretation depends on what predictions were counted. "Correct predictions" and "total predictions" are simple quantities, yet they acquire meaning from the dataset, the task, and the class labels being evaluated.

**Question.** Why multiply by $100\%$ instead of leaving accuracy as a fraction?

**Answer.** The fraction already contains the measurement. If $190$ out of $200$ predictions are correct, the fraction is $190/200 = 0.95$. Multiplying by $100\%$ expresses the same rate as $95\%$, which is easier to read as "95 correct out of every 100 predictions." The multiplication changes the format, not the underlying comparison.

The mental model is a tally sheet. Every prediction adds one mark to the total. Every match between predicted label and correct label adds one mark to the correct count. Accuracy divides the correct marks by all marks and expresses the result as a percentage. That makes accuracy a clean first measurement for classification performance. It tells whether the network's answers are usually right. For spiking neural networks, that measurement is only one coordinate in a larger picture: correctness must be read beside the time, energy, spike activity, and convergence behavior that shape how practical the network is.
