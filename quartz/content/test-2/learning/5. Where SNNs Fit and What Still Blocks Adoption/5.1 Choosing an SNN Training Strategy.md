After seeing how surrogate gradients, ANN-to-SNN conversion, and STDP each train spiking networks, the practical question becomes a deployment question: which compromise fits the job in front of you? Imagine a small device recognizing gestures from an event camera. It has a limited battery, a strict response deadline, and a task where wrong predictions are costly. A training method that wins on accuracy but spends too much energy may fail the deployment. A method that saves energy but responds too slowly may also fail. Choosing an SNN training strategy means choosing among several useful pressures at once: accuracy, latency, energy, spike count, and convergence.

A spiking neural network spends computation when spikes move through the model. Each spike is a discrete event, so the number of spikes gives a rough sense of how much activity the network creates during one inference. Energy use follows that activity because sparse spike traffic lets the system avoid continuous dense computation. Latency measures how long the model needs before it can make a decision. Convergence describes how quickly training settles into a useful solution, often tracked by how training loss decreases over epochs. Accuracy measures how often the final answer is correct. These measurements pull against one another because a model can become more accurate by using more spikes or longer simulation windows, while a very sparse model can save energy by accepting a harder learning problem.

A multi-objective choice begins by naming the deployment priority before ranking the methods. If the main priority is accurate low-latency inference, surrogate-gradient training is often the strongest starting point. Surrogate-gradient SNNs train the spiking model directly while using a smooth substitute for the spike's hard threshold during learning. That substitute lets gradient descent adjust weights even though real spikes are all-or-nothing events. The result is a model that learns spike patterns for the task instead of inheriting them from a separate ANN. In the reported comparison, surrogate-gradient SNNs closely approach ANN accuracy, within about 1-2%, while reaching roughly 10 ms latency and converging strongly by the 20th epoch. That combination makes them attractive when the device must respond quickly and still preserve high task performance.

A concrete way to read that choice is to imagine two gesture-recognition models running on an edge device. One model waits for a long spike window so it can average many events before deciding. The other learns to make earlier decisions from a smaller, more informative spike pattern. The surrogate-trained model is closer to the second picture: training shapes when spikes happen, so the network can use timing efficiently. Its energy figure also sits in the efficient range, around 15 mJ per inference with about 12,000 spikes in the comparison. That is higher than the sparsest STDP case, but far below the approximately 200 mJ reported for an ANN CNN baseline and below the converted SNN's roughly 20 mJ.

ANN-to-SNN conversion becomes appealing when an existing ANN already performs well and the goal is to transfer that performance into a spiking form. A converted SNN starts from a trained conventional network, then maps its continuous activations into spike rates. The central convenience is that the training burden has already been handled by the ANN. The cost is that rate coding often needs many spikes over a simulation window to approximate the original activation values. In the comparison, converted SNNs reduce energy substantially compared with the ANN baseline, reaching about 20 mJ per inference, but they still produce around 20,000 spikes and may require about 20 ms latency because the model needs enough time steps for spike rates to represent activation magnitudes.

Think of rate coding like estimating how strongly someone is pressing a button by counting taps over a short interval. A larger activation becomes a higher tap rate. If the interval is too short, the estimate can be noisy. If the interval is longer, the estimate improves, but the response arrives later and uses more events. Converted SNNs inherit this tradeoff. They can be a sensible choice when a reliable ANN is already available, when accuracy is the dominant metric, and when the deployment can tolerate a longer simulation window and higher spike count than a directly trained SNN.

STDP-based SNNs sit at the other end of the decision space. Spike-Timing Dependent Plasticity changes a synaptic weight based on the relative timing of spikes. When a presynaptic spike consistently helps trigger a postsynaptic spike at the right time, that connection can strengthen; when timing patterns fail to support useful firing, the connection can weaken. This makes STDP attractive for sparse, local, timing-based learning. In the comparison, STDP-based SNNs achieve the lowest energy, around 5 mJ per inference, and the fewest spikes, about 4,000 per inference. Those numbers point toward ultra-low-power settings where battery life or energy harvesting dominates the design.

The tradeoff is visible in convergence and accuracy. STDP-based training tends to converge more slowly and can plateau early, which reflects the limits of a local unsupervised learning rule when the task demands strong supervised discrimination. Its latency can stabilize below 15 ms under optimized conditions, although early inference phases may be slower because spike adaptation develops gradually. A beginner may wonder why the sparsest model does not automatically become the best model. Sparsity saves activity, but prediction still requires enough task-relevant information to reach the output layer in time. STDP can produce beautifully economical spike traffic, yet a harder supervised task may need the extra guidance that surrogate gradients provide.

**Question.** If STDP uses the least energy, why would anyone choose surrogate-gradient training?

**Answer.** Surrogate-gradient training is often chosen when accuracy and fast convergence carry more weight than the absolute minimum energy. It can approach ANN-level accuracy within about 1-2%, converge strongly by around 20 epochs, and produce low-latency responses near 10 ms. STDP can be the better fit when ultra-low energy and sparse spike activity dominate the requirements, but surrogate gradients are usually better aligned with accuracy-sensitive, low-latency deployments.

A head-to-head protocol makes these tradeoffs visible because every method is judged across the same set of metrics. If one evaluation reports only accuracy, converted SNNs and surrogate-gradient SNNs can look similar enough to choose by convenience. If another reports only energy, STDP can look like the obvious winner. Placing accuracy, latency, energy, spike count, and convergence side by side shows the real shape of the decision. The best method depends on which metric has the least flexibility in the deployment.

For example, suppose a wearable sensor must classify simple event patterns all day on a tiny power budget. The priority order may be energy first, spike count second, acceptable accuracy third, and latency only within a moderate limit. STDP-based training becomes a strong candidate because 5 mJ per inference and about 4,000 spikes preserve the device's operating life. Now suppose an event-camera safety system must react quickly and accurately. The priority order changes: latency and accuracy move ahead of absolute energy minimization. Surrogate-gradient training becomes the stronger candidate because it balances competitive accuracy, roughly 10 ms latency, and much lower energy than a conventional ANN. For a team with a high-performing ANN already trained, conversion offers a practical bridge: energy drops by an order of magnitude compared with the ANN baseline, while the cost appears as more spikes and a longer decision window.

**Question.** How should a team compare SNN methods when every method wins at least one metric?

**Answer.** The team should set a priority order before looking for a winner. A useful protocol compares the same candidates on accuracy, latency, energy, spike count, and convergence, then asks which metric is hardest to relax. Surrogate-gradient SNNs fit accuracy-sensitive low-latency goals, converted SNNs fit reuse of strong ANN models with tolerable spike and latency costs, and STDP-based SNNs fit ultra-low-power sparse activity goals.

The mental model is a design triangle with more than three corners: accuracy asks for reliable information, latency asks for early decisions, energy asks for fewer operations, spike count measures event traffic, and convergence asks how easily training reaches a useful model. Surrogate gradients spend training sophistication to shape accurate, quick spike patterns. Conversion spends spike activity and time to preserve behavior learned by an ANN. STDP spends very little activity by relying on local timing rules, accepting slower convergence and reduced accuracy in exchange for extreme efficiency. A good SNN choice begins with the deployment's hardest constraint, then selects the training strategy whose natural tradeoff matches that constraint.
