After comparing accuracy, energy, latency, spike count, and convergence, the safest way to think about SNN results is to treat each number as a result inside a particular testing situation. Imagine two models being compared for a small edge device: one model is an ANN-style CNN that processes continuous numerical activations, and another is an SNN that sends sparse spike events over time. If the SNN uses far less energy on the chosen task, that is useful evidence for choosing it in similar low-power settings. It does not automatically prove that every SNN will beat every ANN on every task, every chip, every dataset, or every accuracy target. A careful claim keeps the result attached to the conditions that produced it.

A **source-bounded claim** is a claim whose strength is limited by the evidence behind it. In this garden, that means an SNN claim should stay tied to the stated model types, training methods, datasets, metrics, and hardware assumptions. For example, a comparison between an ANN/CNN, a converted SNN, a surrogate-gradient SNN, and an STDP-based SNN can support a claim about those four kinds of systems under the measured conditions. It can support a statement such as: "In the reported comparison, SNN variants used less energy per inference than the ANN/CNN baseline." It cannot safely become: "SNNs are always more efficient than ANNs." The second version drops the boundary around the evidence and turns a measured comparison into a universal rule.

The need for bounded claims becomes clear when one metric improves while another worsens. Energy consumption, latency, spike count, accuracy, and convergence each describe a different part of model behavior. **Energy consumption** measures how much energy a model uses per inference, often reported in millijoules. **Latency** measures how long the model takes to produce an output, often reported in milliseconds. **Spike count** measures how many spike events are generated during inference. **Accuracy** measures how often the model predicts correctly on the evaluated task. **Convergence behavior** describes how quickly training settles into useful performance. A model can look excellent on one metric while being less attractive on another. STDP-based SNNs may use very low energy and generate sparse spike activity, while also showing slightly reduced accuracy or slower convergence. Surrogate-gradient SNNs may balance competitive accuracy with low latency, while still using more spikes than the sparsest STDP case. Converted SNNs may preserve much of an ANN's performance, while requiring longer simulation windows and higher spike counts.

A useful way to organize such comparisons is to ask what the application is allowed to trade. A battery-powered sensor may gladly accept a small accuracy drop if it cuts energy use from hundreds of millijoules to a much smaller value. A safety-critical classifier may reject that trade if the accuracy loss affects important decisions. A real-time robot may care about latency as much as energy, because a delayed response can make control unstable. The same measured result can therefore support different design choices depending on which constraint dominates.

A formula can make the boundary of an efficiency claim visible. If an ANN uses energy $E_{\text{ANN}}$ per inference and an SNN uses energy $E_{\text{SNN}}$ per inference under the same comparison setup, the percentage energy reduction is
$$
\text{Energy reduction} =
\frac{E_{\text{ANN}} - E_{\text{SNN}}}{E_{\text{ANN}}} \times 100\%
$$
Here, $E_{\text{ANN}}$ is the energy used by the ANN baseline for one inference, $E_{\text{SNN}}$ is the energy used by the SNN for one inference, and the result tells you how much lower the SNN's energy use is relative to the ANN baseline. If the ANN uses $200\text{ mJ}$ and an STDP-based SNN uses $5\text{ mJ}$, then
$$
\frac{200 - 5}{200} \times 100\% = 97.5\%
$$
That calculation supports a strong energy-efficiency claim for that measured comparison. The formula also carries a caveat: changing either energy value changes the conclusion. A different dataset, hardware platform, spike encoding, simulation window, or implementation could change $E_{\text{ANN}}$, $E_{\text{SNN}}$, or both. The calculation is precise, but its interpretation remains attached to the measurement conditions.

This is the role of a **formula caveat**: a formula can compute exactly what its inputs specify, while the meaning of the answer depends on how those inputs were obtained. A percentage reduction formula does not prove why the reduction occurred by itself. It does not prove that the same reduction appears at larger scale, on a different device, or under a different workload. It tells you the size of the reduction for the compared values. The reasoning around the formula must then ask whether the compared systems were matched fairly enough for the intended conclusion.

Benchmark results need the same care. A **benchmark limitation** is a boundary created by the test setting. Event-based datasets such as gesture or speech-style spike datasets are especially relevant for SNNs because they contain temporal structure that spike-based processing can use naturally. Low inference latencies such as 5-10 ms on neuromorphic platforms show that SNNs can be very fast in suitable event-driven settings. Surrogate-gradient SNNs around 10 ms, STDP-based SNNs stabilizing below 15 ms under optimized conditions, and converted SNNs around 20 ms in some cases give a useful comparison among training approaches. Those numbers should be read as evidence about those evaluation conditions, not as a guarantee that the same ranking appears for every vision, language, robotics, or control task.

**Question.** If an SNN uses 90-97% less energy than an ANN in a comparison, can I say SNNs are better than ANNs?

**Answer.** You can say the evaluated SNNs are more energy-efficient than the evaluated ANN baseline under the reported conditions. "Better" needs a metric. If the goal is low energy, the SNN result is strong. If the goal is maximum raw accuracy, fastest training, simplest deployment, or best performance on an unrelated dataset, the energy result alone does not decide the comparison.

An **unsupported extrapolation** happens when a claim travels beyond its evidence. For example, SNNs are well suited to energy-constrained applications, temporal data, low-latency event processing, robotics, visual tracking, object recognition, and locomotion-style control when timing and power use are central constraints. That is a supported application direction. A stronger claim such as "SNNs will replace conventional neural networks across AI" would require broader evidence than these comparisons provide. The careful mental move is to keep the application language conditional: SNNs are promising where sparse events, temporal precision, low power, and fast reaction align with the task.

Beginner confusion often comes from treating "brain-inspired" as a performance guarantee. Brain inspiration explains the design pattern: information is carried by spikes, silence can save computation, and timing can become part of the signal. It does not automatically determine accuracy, energy, latency, or ease of training. Those properties still depend on the neuron model, learning rule, dataset, hardware, encoding strategy, and evaluation metric. A spiking model trained with surrogate gradients behaves differently from one converted from an ANN, and both differ from an STDP-based system. The category "SNN" contains several design choices, so claims should name the relevant design whenever the distinction affects the result.

**Question.** Why can two SNNs have different conclusions if both use spikes?

**Answer.** Spikes describe the communication style, not the whole system. A converted SNN may need many spikes and a longer simulation window to approximate ANN activations. A surrogate-gradient SNN may learn efficient spike patterns with competitive accuracy and low latency. An STDP-based SNN may generate very few spikes and use very little energy while giving up some accuracy or converging more slowly. The training method and timing requirements shape the outcome.

The safest interpretation is a chain: a model is evaluated on a task, under a training method, on a platform or assumed computation style, using chosen metrics. The result supports a claim that fits that chain. When the chain says low energy per inference and sparse spike counts, the supported lesson is that event-driven computation can reduce wasted activity in suitable settings. When the chain says latency is low on event-based benchmarks or neuromorphic platforms, the supported lesson is that SNNs can be strong candidates for real-time temporal processing. When the chain includes accuracy gaps, higher spike counts, longer simulation windows, or slower convergence, those limitations remain part of the claim. A mature view of SNNs keeps the promise and the boundary together: they offer a distinctive path for low-power, time-sensitive intelligence, and each result should be read through the exact metrics and conditions that produced it.
