After comparing models by accuracy, energy, spike count, and latency, one question naturally comes up: how do we make a fair judgment when one model is a little more accurate and another uses far less energy? Imagine choosing a vision model for a small battery-powered sensor. One model recognizes objects correctly 98 times out of 100, but it burns through energy quickly because it performs dense computation on every input. Another model recognizes objects correctly 95 times out of 100, but it spends only a small fraction of the energy because most neurons stay silent unless a spike event occurs. Accuracy alone favors the first model. Energy alone favors the second. A practical evaluation needs one number that asks: how much useful correctness do we get for the energy we spend?

That question leads to **normalized energy efficiency**. The core intuition is accuracy per unit of energy. If accuracy measures how often the model gives the right answer, and energy consumption measures how much energy the model uses to produce an inference, then their ratio tells us how much correct performance is delivered for each unit of energy spent.

A simple form is:
$$
\text{Normalized Energy Efficiency}=\frac{\text{Accuracy}}{\text{Energy Consumption}}
$$
Here, **normalized energy efficiency** is the efficiency score being computed, **accuracy** is the model's task performance, usually written as a fraction or percentage of correct predictions, and **energy consumption** is the energy used per inference, often measured in millijoules. A **ratio** compares one quantity to another by division. In this case, the ratio asks how much accuracy is obtained for each unit of energy.

If accuracy is written as a percentage, the score has units of "percentage points per millijoule." If accuracy is written as a decimal, such as $0.95$, the score has units of "correct-fraction per millijoule." The exact scaling can differ depending on how a study normalizes the numbers, but the underlying idea stays the same: larger accuracy raises the score, and larger energy consumption lowers it.

Consider a dense ANN that reaches about $99\%$ accuracy but uses $200$ mJ per inference. Its simple accuracy-per-energy score is:
$$
\frac{99}{200}=0.495
$$
Now consider an SNN that reaches $95\%$ accuracy but uses $20$ mJ per inference:
$$
\frac{95}{20}=4.75
$$
The ANN wins if the only question is "Which model makes the most correct predictions?" The SNN wins if the question is "Which model gives the most correct prediction performance for each millijoule spent?" The second question is often the better one for edge devices, neuromorphic systems, and battery-limited sensors, because every inference has an energy cost that accumulates over time.

This measure also makes the event-based nature of SNNs easier to evaluate. A spiking network saves energy when fewer neurons fire and fewer synaptic operations are triggered. A model that uses sparse spikes can reduce energy consumption while keeping accuracy close to the dense model. For example, reported comparisons include ANNs around $200$ mJ per inference, converted SNNs around $20$ mJ, surrogate-gradient SNNs around $15$ mJ, and STDP-based SNNs around $5$ mJ. Those numbers show why an energy-aware metric is needed. A small change in accuracy can sit beside a huge change in energy.

The ratio also explains why spike count belongs in the same conversation. Spike count is a count of how many spike events are generated during inference. More spikes usually mean more event handling and more synaptic updates, which tends to increase energy use. A converted SNN may lower energy compared with an ANN, yet still generate many spikes because rate coding can require repeated spike events over a simulation window. A surrogate-trained SNN can often reduce both energy and spike count by learning spiking patterns more directly. An STDP-based SNN can be especially sparse, producing fewer spikes and using very little energy, while sometimes giving up some accuracy. Normalized energy efficiency gives a way to see whether that tradeoff is favorable.

**Question.** If a model has slightly lower accuracy, can it still have better normalized energy efficiency?

**Answer.** Yes. The efficiency score depends on both accuracy and energy consumption. Suppose Model A has $98\%$ accuracy and uses $100$ mJ, giving $98/100=0.98$. Model B has $94\%$ accuracy and uses $10$ mJ, giving $94/10=9.4$. Model B is less accurate in raw terms, but it delivers much more accuracy per millijoule. For an energy-limited system, that can make Model B the better choice.

The word "normalized" signals that the score is arranged so different models can be compared on a common basis. Raw energy values alone can be misleading when accuracy differs. Raw accuracy values alone can be misleading when energy differs. Normalization places the two quantities into the same evaluation frame. It turns two separate measurements into a tradeoff-aware score.

This score follows from measurable counts. Accuracy comes from counting correct predictions over a test set. If a model answers $950$ examples correctly out of $1000$, its accuracy is:
$$
\text{Accuracy}=\frac{950}{1000}=0.95=95\%
$$
Energy consumption comes from measuring or estimating how much energy one inference uses. If the model consumes $15$ mJ per inference, then the simple normalized energy efficiency using decimal accuracy is:
$$
\frac{0.95}{15}\approx0.0633
$$
Using percentage accuracy, the same comparison would be:
$$
\frac{95}{15}\approx6.33
$$
Both versions express the same ordering if every model is evaluated with the same convention. The scale changes, but the comparison remains consistent. The score is meaningful only when accuracy and energy are measured in a consistent way across the models being compared.

**Question.** Why does the efficiency score change only when accuracy or energy changes?

**Answer.** The formula contains only two inputs: accuracy and energy consumption. If accuracy stays fixed and energy drops, the denominator becomes smaller, so efficiency rises. If energy stays fixed and accuracy rises, the numerator becomes larger, so efficiency rises. If both stay the same, the score stays the same. The metric moves only when one of its measured inputs moves.

This makes normalized energy efficiency a useful mental tool for SNNs. A spike is a small event, and silence saves work. When a network learns to solve a task with fewer spikes, it can reduce energy consumption. If it keeps accuracy nearly the same, the ratio improves sharply. If it loses too much accuracy, the ratio may improve less or even become unattractive for the task. The metric keeps both sides visible at once.

A good way to think about the measure is like judging a vehicle by distance per unit of fuel. A car that travels slightly farther but burns a huge amount of fuel may be less practical than one that travels nearly as far on much less fuel. For SNNs, the "distance" is useful predictive performance, and the "fuel" is energy per inference. Event-driven computation becomes valuable when sparse spikes preserve enough accuracy while cutting the energy bill. Normalized energy efficiency turns that tradeoff into a single, comparable number: how much correct behavior the model buys with each unit of energy it spends.
