## The Leaky Integrate-and-Fire Neuron

A spike event already gives an SNN a compact way to say, "something happened at this moment." The next question is how a neuron decides that enough has happened to send one of those events onward. Imagine a small sensor watching a mostly still scene. A few weak changes may flicker in the input: a shadow shifts, a pixel brightens slightly, then another nearby pixel changes. Sending a spike for every tiny disturbance would make the network noisy and busy. Waiting forever would miss real changes. A spiking neuron solves this by keeping a temporary internal state that rises when input arrives, fades when input stops, and produces a spike only when the accumulated evidence becomes strong enough.

That temporary internal state is called the **membrane potential**. You can think of it as the neuron's running level of excitement. Incoming spike events from earlier neurons push this level upward. Stronger or more frequent input pushes it upward faster. If the input is weak or spread far apart in time, the level may rise a little and then drift back down before anything is sent. The membrane potential is the bridge between many small incoming events and one outgoing event: it lets the neuron collect evidence over time before making a discrete decision.

The word **integration** means this collecting process. Each input contributes to the membrane potential, so the neuron is not reacting to a single instant alone. It is carrying a short memory of recent activity. A useful analogy is filling a leaky cup under a faucet. Each incoming spike adds a little water. If drops arrive quickly, the cup fills. If drops arrive slowly, water escapes before the level reaches the mark. The cup's water level is like membrane potential, the drops are incoming spikes, and the mark on the cup is the firing threshold.

The **firing threshold** is the level the membrane potential must reach before the neuron emits a spike. Once the membrane potential crosses that threshold, the neuron "fires": it sends one spike event to the next layer. The output is therefore a discrete event created by a continuous rise inside the neuron. The neuron can have a changing internal level without continuously broadcasting that level. Other neurons receive the spike, not a live stream of every internal fluctuation.

*LIF neuron membrane potential over time with firing threshold* *(p. 4)*

The graph shows the membrane potential climbing over time as input current arrives. The threshold line marks the point where accumulated input becomes enough to trigger a spike. The important pattern is the shape: the potential rises gradually, touches the threshold, produces a spike, and then drops back down. The spike is caused by threshold crossing. The neuron is not emitting a steadily larger output as the curve rises; it stays silent until the curve reaches the firing level, then sends a single event.

After the spike, the neuron performs a **reset**. Resetting means the membrane potential is brought back down to a lower level so the neuron can begin accumulating input again. Without reset, one threshold crossing would leave the neuron already at or above the firing level, and the same stored evidence could keep producing spikes. Reset gives each spike a clean boundary: this much input has been counted, one event has been sent, and the neuron is ready for the next accumulation cycle.

The **leak** gives the model its name: **Leaky Integrate-and-Fire**, often shortened to **LIF**. Leak means the membrane potential naturally decays over time when input is absent or too weak. In the cup analogy, leak is the hole in the cup. Recent input matters most because older input gradually loses influence. This makes the neuron sensitive to patterns that are strong enough and close enough in time. A burst of incoming spikes can push the membrane potential to threshold, while scattered spikes may fade before they combine into a firing event.

This leak is essential for turning changing input into meaningful events. Suppose a neuron receives one input spike at time 1, another at time 20, and another at time 40. If the membrane potential kept every contribution forever, those unrelated events could eventually add up and cause a spike long after the original signal had passed. With leak, the neuron treats widely separated inputs as weaker evidence for one shared event. Now suppose several input spikes arrive close together around time 10. Their effects overlap before much leakage occurs, the membrane potential rises quickly, and the neuron fires. The timing of the output spike therefore tells the next layer that a concentrated pattern appeared.

**Question.** If the membrane potential is changing over time, why call the neuron event-driven?

**Answer.** The neuron uses a changing internal state to decide when to communicate, but communication happens through spike events. The membrane potential can rise and leak silently. Other neurons are activated when a spike is emitted after threshold crossing, so computation is organized around events rather than around continuously sending every internal value.

The LIF mechanism also explains why spike timing carries information. A stronger input current makes the membrane potential climb faster, so the threshold is reached sooner. A weaker input may reach threshold later or fail to reach it. A lower threshold also makes firing easier, while a higher threshold demands more accumulated input. A stronger leak makes the neuron forget recent input faster, which can delay firing or prevent it. A lower reset level means the neuron must climb farther before the next spike. These settings shape when spikes occur, how often they occur, and whether a neuron remains silent.

Consider two neurons receiving similar input from a sensor. One has a low threshold and weak leak. Its membrane potential rises easily and may fire quickly. The other has a higher threshold and stronger leak. It needs a more sustained input pattern before it fires. In a network, these different responses allow some neurons to react to faint or early evidence while others respond only to stronger, more persistent patterns. The same basic mechanism can therefore support a range of sensitivities without changing the idea of spike-based communication.

A common beginner confusion is to imagine the spike as the same thing as the membrane potential peak. The membrane potential is the internal accumulation curve. The spike is the outgoing event produced when that curve reaches threshold. The curve explains why the spike happened; the spike is the message sent forward. This distinction helps keep the SNN picture clear: internal state evolves over time, while communication remains sparse and event-based.

**Question.** What happens when input raises the membrane potential but it never reaches the firing threshold?

**Answer.** The neuron stays silent. The membrane potential may rise for a while, then leak downward if the input is too weak or too spread out in time. No outgoing spike is produced because the accumulated evidence never becomes strong enough to cross the threshold.

The LIF neuron gives a simple mechanical answer to how SNNs turn activity into events. Incoming spikes add to membrane potential through integration. Leak makes old input fade so timing matters. A firing threshold marks the point where accumulated evidence becomes strong enough to send a spike. Reset clears the state after firing so the next event must be earned by new input. In this mental model, a spiking neuron is a small evidence accumulator with a fading memory and a trigger line: it listens over time, stays quiet while evidence is incomplete, and makes state changes visible by releasing a spike at the moment the threshold is crossed.
