A single accuracy number hides the costs that make spiking networks worthwhile, so evaluation has to weigh several quantities at once. Consider comparing two models that reach similar accuracy at very different energy and latency costs.

A **stimulus** is the input event or signal presented to the model. In a vision task, the stimulus could be a new gesture sample from an event camera. In an auditory task, it could be a short sound pattern. A **decision** is the model's output after processing that stimulus, such as "swipe left," "clap," or "spoken digit three." The useful question is how long the model keeps the learner, robot, sensor, or control system waiting before that decision becomes available.

That waiting time is called **latency**. It is measured as a time interval:
$$
\text{Latency} = t_{\text{decision}} - t_{\text{stimulus}}
$$
Here, $\text{Latency}$ is the elapsed time between input and output. The symbol $t_{\text{stimulus}}$ means the time when the stimulus is presented to the model. The symbol $t_{\text{decision}}$ means the time when the model produces its decision. The subtraction gives the delay between those two moments, usually reported in milliseconds for fast inference systems.

Suppose a gesture stimulus reaches a model at $t_{\text{stimulus}} = 100$ ms, and the model produces the gesture label at $t_{\text{decision}} = 112$ ms. The latency is
$$
112\text{ ms} - 100\text{ ms} = 12\text{ ms}
$$
That number means the model needed 12 milliseconds to turn the incoming stimulus into a decision. If another model sees the same stimulus at 100 ms but decides at 130 ms, its latency is 30 ms. Both models might be accurate, but the 12 ms model responds sooner. For a system that must react while the world is changing, the faster decision can be as valuable as a slightly higher accuracy score.

Spiking Neural Networks make latency especially meaningful because time is part of their computation. A spiking neuron communicates through discrete spike events, and the pattern of those events unfolds over time. The model may need only a few early spikes to form a confident decision, or it may need a longer simulation window to collect enough evidence. A **simulation window** is the amount of modeled time allowed for spikes to occur before the system reads out a decision. A longer window can give the network more spike evidence, but it also pushes the decision later. Latency turns that behavior into a measurable quantity: it tells us how much delay the network's temporal processing creates.

This is where accuracy, correct predictions, and total predictions need a companion metric. Accuracy can be written as
$$
\text{Accuracy} = \frac{\text{correct predictions}}{\text{total predictions}}
$$
The numerator counts how many decisions match the true labels. The denominator counts how many decisions were made overall. This ratio answers, "How often is the model right?" Latency answers, "How quickly does the model become ready to act?" A model that is correct on 97 out of 100 samples has strong accuracy, but if each decision arrives after the useful response window has passed, the model is poorly matched to a real-time task. A model that is slightly less accurate but responds within a few milliseconds may be more useful for gesture recognition, auditory event detection, or neuromorphic sensing.

The useful interpretation is model-by-model. CNN-based ANNs often require roughly 20-50 ms depending on complexity and hardware, because their computation processes continuous-valued activations through layers. Converted SNNs can reduce the delay but may still require around 20 ms when they need enough spikes over a longer window to represent ANN-like activation levels. Surrogate gradient SNNs can reach about 10 ms while keeping competitive accuracy, because training directly shapes the spike-based computation used at inference time. STDP-based SNNs may be slower during early adaptation, but optimized versions can stabilize below about 15 ms while using sparse spike activity.

A beginner confusion often comes from treating latency as another name for low accuracy. It is better to picture two separate scoreboards. Accuracy counts whether the final answer is right. Latency measures how long the answer takes to arrive. A model can be accurate and slow, fast and inaccurate, or both fast and accurate enough for the task. Real-time inference requires looking at both scoreboards because the system must decide correctly while the decision is still useful.

**Question.** If two models both classify 95 out of 100 samples correctly, why might one still be better for a real-time gesture system?

**Answer.** Their accuracy is the same because both make 95 correct predictions out of 100 total predictions. The better real-time model is the one with lower latency, because it produces each correct decision sooner after the gesture stimulus appears. If one model responds in 10 ms and the other responds in 35 ms, the 10 ms model is more likely to keep pace with a changing gesture stream.

Latency also explains why spiking systems are attractive for event-based sensors. Event-based inputs already arrive as changes over time rather than as fixed frames. A spiking model can use the timing of those events directly, allowing early spikes to contribute to an early decision. When enough evidence accumulates quickly, the model can respond without waiting for a long, dense computation over an entire frame sequence. This is the practical meaning of real-time inference: the model's internal processing rhythm stays close to the rhythm of the incoming world.

**Question.** What does a latency of 15 ms mean in the formula $\text{Latency} = t_{\text{decision}} - t_{\text{stimulus}}$?

**Answer.** It means the decision time occurs 15 milliseconds after the stimulus time. If the stimulus appears at 40 ms, then a 15 ms latency means the model outputs its decision at 55 ms. The number measures delay, so it tells you how long the system waits between receiving the input and producing the result.

A good mental model is to imagine every prediction as a small race that starts when the stimulus arrives. Correctness decides whether the runner reaches the right destination. Latency measures the time on the clock when the runner gets there. Spiking Neural Networks are designed so that spike timing can carry useful evidence during the race itself, which gives them a path toward fast, event-driven decisions. For real-time applications, the best model is judged by the combination of arriving at the right answer and arriving while the answer can still be used.
