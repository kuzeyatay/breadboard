### Accuracy, Latency, Energy, and Spike Tradeoffs

Now that event-driven sparsity has been established, the next question is how to measure whether it actually helps. A single accuracy number hides the costs that make spiking networks worthwhile, so evaluation has to weigh several quantities at once. Consider comparing two models that reach similar accuracy at very different energy and latency costs.

Accuracy tells you how often the model gives the correct answer. Latency tells you how long the model takes to produce that answer. Energy consumption tells you how much power is spent per inference, meaning per single input processed. Spike count tells you how many spike events the model generates while making that inference. Convergence time tells you how long training takes before the model reaches its target behavior. A performance profile is the full shape made by these metrics together. It is the answer to a practical question: "What kind of model is this, once I include both what it achieves and what it costs?"

A spiking model makes this profile especially meaningful because spikes are both signals and expenses. Each spike carries information at a particular time, but producing and transmitting spikes also costs energy. A model that fires constantly may behave more like a dense conventional network, while a model that stays quiet until useful events occur can save energy. That is why spike count sits close to energy consumption in the comparison. Fewer spikes usually mean fewer synaptic operations and less energy per inference. A beginner confusion often comes from treating spikes as free because they are simple events. A spike is small, but many spikes across many neurons and time steps accumulate into real computational work.

Surrogate-gradient SNNs form one performance profile. They are trained in a way that lets gradient-based learning adjust spiking behavior, so they tend to keep much of the accuracy associated with conventional neural networks while also gaining the timing advantages of spike-based computation. In the reported comparison, surrogate-gradient SNNs reach about 97.8% accuracy, approach ANN accuracy within roughly 1-2%, converge by around the 20th epoch, and can make decisions at about 10 ms latency. Their energy use is moderate among the spiking options, around 15 mJ per inference, with roughly 12,000 spikes per inference.

Those numbers describe a model that is built for balanced speed and correctness. If the system must respond quickly and cannot give up much accuracy, the surrogate-gradient profile is attractive. The cost is that it does not minimize energy as aggressively as the sparsest spiking approach. A useful analogy is a skilled courier network in a busy city: messages are routed quickly and accurately, and the system avoids some waste, but it still keeps enough couriers active to guarantee fast delivery. The model spends more spikes than the most energy-frugal option because it is buying a stronger combination of accuracy, latency, and training efficiency.

Converted SNNs occupy a different region of the tradeoff space. A converted SNN begins from an ANN-like computation and represents activations through spikes over a simulation window. This can preserve competitive performance, but the model may need more time steps and more spikes to approximate the continuous activation values it came from. In the comparison, converted SNNs reduce energy far below an ANN, around 20 mJ instead of about 200 mJ per inference, but they still produce a relatively high spike count, roughly 20,000 spikes per inference, and can require longer latency, around 20 ms, because the spike rates need time to express the original activation levels.

This profile suits situations where conversion from an existing ANN is useful and some extra delay or spiking activity is acceptable. The model gains the energy advantage of event-based computation, but it carries a memory of its origin: continuous values are being approximated through spike activity, and that approximation can demand a longer observation window. The practical interpretation is that converted SNNs can be a bridge from conventional models to spiking models, but their performance profile may be less clean than models trained directly for spiking behavior.

STDP-based SNNs form the most energy-sparse profile. Spike-Timing Dependent Plasticity, or STDP, adjusts connections using the relative timing of spikes. When one neuron tends to fire shortly before another, their connection can be strengthened; when the timing relation is less useful, the connection can weaken. This local timing-based learning encourages sparse spike patterns. In the reported comparison, STDP-based SNNs achieve the lowest energy consumption, about 5 mJ per inference, and the lowest spike count, about 4,000 spikes per inference. They also reach about 95.5% accuracy, which is slightly lower than the surrogate-gradient result, and they converge more slowly.

The tradeoff becomes clear when energy is the limiting resource. If a device must run for a long time with minimal power, the STDP profile is compelling because it spends very few spikes. The cost appears in training behavior and accuracy. Slower convergence means the model takes longer to settle into its target performance during training. Slightly reduced accuracy means it gives up some correctness compared with the strongest accuracy-oriented option. A useful way to picture STDP is a quiet room where only the most meaningful timing relationships become part of the conversation. The room stays quiet, so energy stays low, but learning the best conversation pattern takes patience.

**Question.** If STDP-based SNNs use the least energy, why would anyone choose a surrogate-gradient SNN?

**Answer.** A surrogate-gradient SNN gives a stronger balance when accuracy and latency are both high priorities. It can reach about 97.8% accuracy, respond at about 10 ms, and converge by around the 20th epoch, while still using much less energy than an ANN. STDP is better when ultra-low energy and sparse spiking matter most, but surrogate-gradient training is better when the application needs fast, accurate decisions with a moderate energy budget.

The conventional ANN or CNN profile gives the comparison a useful reference point. ANNs can dominate raw accuracy, but they rely on continuous-valued operations rather than sparse spike events. In the reported energy comparison, the ANN/CNN consumes around 200 mJ per inference, much higher than the spiking alternatives. The difference is large because a dense network continues processing numerical activations across many operations, while a spiking network can treat silence as meaningful and spend computation only when spikes occur. This is why an SNN can give up a small amount of accuracy and still become attractive: the saved energy can be enormous for edge or neuromorphic settings.

A metric tradeoff only becomes meaningful when the benefit is placed beside its cost. Saying "this model has high accuracy" is incomplete until you ask how much latency, energy, spike activity, and training time it requires. Saying "this model has low energy" is incomplete until you ask how much accuracy and convergence speed it gives up. Model selection is the act of matching this performance profile to the job. A gesture recognizer that must respond immediately may prefer the surrogate-gradient profile. A long-running sensor with a tiny power budget may prefer the STDP profile. A system that starts from a trained ANN may consider conversion, accepting more spikes or longer windows as the price of reuse.

**Question.** Does the best SNN model mean the one with the highest accuracy?

**Answer.** The best model is the one whose performance profile fits the application. Highest accuracy is valuable when mistakes are costly, but a low-power device may need energy and spike count to carry more weight. A fast interactive system may care strongly about latency. A training pipeline may care about convergence time. The comparison shows that no single model wins across every axis, so "best" depends on which cost the application can afford.

The overall mental model is a map rather than a ranking. Accuracy measures correctness, latency measures decision delay, energy measures power spent, spike count measures event activity, and convergence time measures training progress. Surrogate-gradient SNNs sit near the fast-and-accurate region of the map, with moderate energy and spike use. STDP-based SNNs sit near the sparse-and-efficient region, with the lowest energy and spike count but slower convergence and slightly lower accuracy. Converted SNNs sit between conventional ANN behavior and native spiking behavior, saving energy while often needing more spikes and longer simulation windows. Reading the metrics together turns scattered numbers into a practical design choice: every model offers a bundle of strengths, and every strength is understood by the cost it asks the system to pay.
