## The Leaky Integrate-and-Fire Neuron

Once information has been represented as spike events arriving at particular timesteps, a neuron needs a rule for deciding what to do with those events. Imagine several input neurons sending spikes toward one receiving neuron. A single incoming spike may be too weak to produce an output, yet several spikes arriving close together may signal a meaningful pattern. The receiving neuron therefore needs a short-lived memory of recent input and a clear moment at which accumulated activity becomes strong enough to send a new spike.

The leaky integrate-and-fire neuron, usually shortened to **LIF neuron**, provides this mechanism through one changing internal quantity: the **membrane potential**. You can picture the membrane potential as a running measure of how strongly the neuron has recently been driven toward firing. Each incoming spike contributes some amount to this measure. Excitatory input raises it, and a larger or more influential input raises it more. As timesteps pass, the neuron combines these contributions rather than judging every spike in isolation. This accumulation is called **integration**.

Consider a neuron whose membrane potential begins at a low resting level. One input spike arrives and pushes the potential upward. Soon afterward, another spike arrives and raises it again. If a third spike follows while the earlier contributions still have an effect, the combined rise may become large enough to trigger an output. The neuron has therefore recognized a cluster of activity spread across time. Integration gives the neuron a simple form of temporal memory: recent events influence how it responds to the current event.

That memory fades. During timesteps with little or no incoming activity, the membrane potential falls toward its resting level. This gradual loss is the **leak** in leaky integrate-and-fire. A useful analogy is a container with a small hole near its bottom. Incoming spikes pour small amounts of water into the container, while the hole continually lets water escape. Rapid input can fill the container faster than it drains. Widely separated input pulses allow much of the earlier water to escape before the next pulse arrives.

Suppose three equally strong spikes arrive in three nearby timesteps. The first raises the membrane potential, the second builds on the remaining potential, and the third may complete the buildup needed for firing. If those same three spikes are separated by long quiet intervals, leakage can erase much of each contribution before the following spike arrives. The total number of input spikes is the same in both cases, but their timing produces different outcomes. The LIF neuron therefore responds to both input strength and temporal concentration.

The neuron needs a boundary that turns this gradual internal buildup into a discrete output event. That boundary is the **firing threshold**. At every timestep, the neuron's membrane potential can be compared with the threshold. When accumulated input pushes the potential to the threshold, the neuron emits a spike. The output is a timed event that can become input to other neurons.

*LIF neuron membrane potential over time compared with firing threshold* *(p. 4)*

The changing curve represents the membrane potential, while the horizontal reference level represents the firing threshold. Rising portions of the curve show integration: incoming activity is adding to the neuron's stored potential. Downward or relaxing portions show leakage: the stored effect of earlier input is fading as time passes. A crossing occurs when the rising membrane-potential curve reaches the threshold. That crossing identifies the timestep at which the neuron emits a spike.

Immediately after emission, the membrane potential drops to a lower level. This **post-spike reset** prepares the neuron to begin accumulating input again. Without this reset, a potential that remained above threshold could repeatedly trigger output without requiring a fresh buildup. Reset creates a new integration cycle: input arrives, potential accumulates while leaking, threshold is reached, a spike is emitted, and the potential returns to its reset level.

Tracing the graph across multiple cycles shows how continuous accumulation inside the neuron produces discrete output times. Before a threshold crossing, the exact height of the membrane potential records how close recent input has brought the neuron to firing. At the crossing, that internal state is converted into a spike. After the reset, earlier accumulated activity no longer carries the neuron immediately into another firing event. A later spike requires renewed input strong enough, or concentrated enough in time, to overcome leakage and reach the threshold again.

Input strength, leak, and threshold work together. Stronger input pulses create larger upward changes, so the threshold can be reached sooner. A faster leak removes accumulated potential more quickly, making separated inputs less likely to combine. A higher threshold requires a larger buildup before emission. These parameters shape when the neuron fires and whether it fires during a given sequence of inputs. Firing time emerges from their interaction rather than from any one input event alone.

**Question.** If two neurons receive the same number of input spikes, why might only one of them fire?

**Answer.** The spikes may have different timing or strength, or the neurons may have different leak rates and firing thresholds. Closely spaced or stronger inputs can accumulate faster than leakage removes their effects, allowing the membrane potential to reach threshold. More widely spaced or weaker inputs may fade before they can combine. A neuron with faster leakage or a higher threshold also requires a more concentrated buildup to emit a spike.

A common beginner confusion is to treat the membrane potential itself as the spike. The membrane potential is the neuron's evolving internal state, while the spike is the discrete event produced when that state reaches the firing threshold. The potential can rise and fall many times without crossing the boundary. Only a threshold crossing creates an output spike.

**Question.** What happens when the membrane potential rises close to the firing threshold and then input activity stops?

**Answer.** Leakage gradually pulls the membrane potential downward toward its resting level. Because the threshold is never reached, the neuron emits no spike. If another input arrives soon enough, some of the earlier potential may remain and help the new input reach threshold. If the quiet interval is longer, much of that accumulated effect will have faded.

The LIF neuron can therefore be understood as a leaky accumulator with a trigger and a reset. Incoming spikes add evidence over successive timesteps, leakage gives recent evidence greater influence than distant evidence, the firing threshold marks the required buildup, spike emission communicates the threshold crossing, and post-spike reset begins a fresh cycle. This chain turns patterns of incoming activity into precisely timed output spikes, providing a compact bridge from event-based signals to biologically inspired computation.
