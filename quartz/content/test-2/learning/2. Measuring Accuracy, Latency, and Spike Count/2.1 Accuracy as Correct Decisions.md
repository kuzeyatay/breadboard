After you already know what a prediction is, accuracy gives you a compact way to judge a model's decisions across many prediction attempts. Imagine an SNN-based vision system classifying small sensor inputs one after another: one input arrives, the model chooses a label, and that choice is compared with the correct label. A single choice can be right or wrong, but a comparison between SNN variants needs a way to combine many choices into one score. Accuracy is that score: it asks, "Out of all the predictions the model made, what fraction were correct?"

Start with the smallest possible record of model behavior. For each input, write down one mark if the predicted label matches the correct label. If the model sees an image of a digit 3 and predicts "3," that prediction adds one to the correct prediction count. If it predicts "8," the total number of predictions still increases, but the correct prediction count stays the same. The correct prediction count is the number of successful decisions. The total prediction count is the number of decisions made overall. Accuracy compares those two counts.

The formula records that relationship directly:
$$
\text{Accuracy} = \frac{\text{Correct Predictions}}{\text{Total Predictions}}
$$
Here, **accuracy** is the fraction of all predictions that were correct. **Correct Predictions** means the count of outputs that matched the expected answer. **Total Predictions** means the count of all outputs being evaluated, whether they were right or wrong. The formula lets you compute the model's correctness rate from raw counts. If the result is written as a percentage, multiply the fraction by 100:
$$
\text{Accuracy Percentage} = \frac{\text{Correct Predictions}}{\text{Total Predictions}} \times 100\%
$$
If an SNN makes 1,000 predictions and 955 of them are correct, its accuracy is
$$
\frac{955}{1000} = 0.955
$$
As a percentage, that becomes
$$
0.955 \times 100\% = 95.5\%
$$
This means that, across the evaluated predictions, 95.5 out of every 100 decisions were correct on average. It does not mean the model is correct for every future group of exactly 100 inputs. It means the observed batch of predictions had that proportion of correct decisions.

A useful way to feel the formula is to imagine a jar containing one token for each prediction. A green token means the model was correct, and a gray token means it made an error. Accuracy is the share of green tokens in the jar. If the jar has 100 tokens and 98 are green, the accuracy is 98%. If the jar has 10,000 tokens and 9,800 are green, the accuracy is also 98%. The percentage is the same because the relationship between correct decisions and total decisions is the same, even though the second evaluation involved many more examples.

The denominator, the total prediction count, is what keeps the score honest. A model with 9 correct predictions out of 10 has 90% accuracy. A model with 900 correct predictions out of 1,000 also has 90% accuracy. The same fraction appears in both cases, but the larger evaluation usually gives a more stable picture because it contains more prediction attempts. The formula itself only computes the ratio; judging how reliable that ratio feels also depends on how many total predictions were used.

The numerator, the correct prediction count, carries the claim about decision quality. When that count rises while the total stays fixed, accuracy rises. If a model is evaluated on 1,000 inputs, improving from 950 correct predictions to 978 correct predictions changes the accuracy from 95.0% to 97.8%. That is why an SNN variant with about 97.8% accuracy is being credited with stronger raw correctness than one around 95.5% accuracy under the same kind of comparison. The number is easy to interpret because it comes from a simple count: more correct labels out of the same evaluation size produce a higher accuracy.

Accuracy becomes especially useful when several SNN approaches are compared side by side. A surrogate-gradient SNN around 97.8% accuracy is making a larger fraction of correct decisions than an STDP-based SNN around 95.5%. That difference says something precise about labels: the surrogate-gradient model is more often matching the expected answer. The same comparison also includes other quantities such as energy per inference, spike count, latency, and convergence behavior. Those quantities answer different questions. Accuracy answers the correctness question; energy answers how much computation cost was spent per inference; latency answers how long the decision took; spike count reflects how much spiking activity occurred. A model can have stronger accuracy while using more energy, and another can accept a smaller accuracy value while becoming attractive for ultra-low-power settings.

This separation is central to reading SNN results clearly. An ANN may dominate in raw accuracy, while SNNs can achieve large energy reductions with reasonable performance tradeoffs. A surrogate-gradient SNN can balance high accuracy with moderate energy and low latency, while an STDP-based SNN can reach very low energy with slightly lower accuracy. The accuracy formula does not hide those tradeoffs; it simply refuses to mix them into one number. It fixes which variables carry the correctness claim: correct predictions in the numerator, total predictions in the denominator. Cost belongs to separate measurements.

**Question.** If two models both report 95% accuracy, did they make the same number of mistakes?

**Answer.** They made the same fraction of mistakes, but the raw number depends on the total prediction count. On 100 predictions, 95% accuracy means 95 correct and 5 incorrect. On 10,000 predictions, 95% accuracy means 9,500 correct and 500 incorrect. The formula preserves the proportion, while the total count tells you the scale of the evaluation.

Training curves use the same idea repeatedly over time. When accuracy is plotted across epochs, each point represents the fraction of correct predictions at that stage of training. A curve that rises steeply toward about 98% shows that the model is learning to make correct decisions more often as training proceeds. A curve that rises slowly or plateaus earlier shows that the correct prediction count is improving more gradually relative to the total evaluated predictions. The curve's shape describes how the correctness fraction changes over training, while the formula underneath remains the same count-based ratio.

**Question.** If a model has the highest accuracy, should it always be chosen for an edge or neuromorphic application?

**Answer.** It should be chosen when correct labeling is the dominant requirement and its other costs fit the application. For a battery-constrained or real-time setting, energy, spike count, latency, and convergence can affect suitability alongside accuracy. Accuracy tells you how often the model is right; the other metrics tell you what the model spends to be right.

The mental model is simple: every evaluation creates a pile of predictions, every prediction either adds to the correct count or only to the total count, and accuracy is the fraction formed by those two counts. In an SNN comparison, that fraction measures decision correctness cleanly, while energy, latency, spike count, and convergence describe the surrounding practical costs. Keeping those roles separate lets you read an accuracy value as exactly what it is: the model's observed rate of correct decisions across the predictions being evaluated.
