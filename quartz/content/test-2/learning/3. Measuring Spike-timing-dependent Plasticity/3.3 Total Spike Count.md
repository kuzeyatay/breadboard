A spike event tells us that a particular neuron fired at a particular time step. Once those events have been recorded, we can move from inspecting individual spikes to measuring the activity of the whole network during one inference. Imagine a network with hundreds of neurons running for dozens of time steps. Its spike record forms a grid: each row corresponds to a neuron, each column corresponds to a time step, and each cell records whether that neuron emitted a spike at that moment. **Total spike count** measures how many spike events appear anywhere in this grid.

Start with a single neuron. Suppose neuron 1 is observed for five time steps and produces the sequence
$$
[0,\;1,\;0,\;1,\;0]
$$
Here, $1$ represents a spike event and $0$ represents silence. Adding these values gives $2$, so the neuron's **per-neuron spike count** is two. The exact timing still appears in the original sequence-the spikes occurred at time steps 2 and 4-but the count compresses that sequence into one activity total.

For neuron $i$, its spike count over $T$ time steps can be written as
$$
C_i=\sum_{t=1}^{T}s_{i,t}
$$
In this formula, $C_i$ is the spike count of neuron $i$, $i$ is the neuron index that identifies one neuron in the network, $t$ is the time-step index, $T$ is the number of observed time steps, and $s_{i,t}$ records the spike activity of neuron $i$ at time step $t$. In a binary spike record, $s_{i,t}=1$ when the neuron emits a spike and $s_{i,t}=0$ when it remains silent. The formula lets us compute how active one neuron was during the full observation window.

Now imagine three neurons observed for four time steps:
$$
\begin{array}{c|cccc|c}
 & t=1 & t=2 & t=3 & t=4 & \text{Neuron count}\\
\hline
I=1 & 0 & 1 & 0 & 1 & 2\\
I=2 & 1 & 0 & 0 & 0 & 1\\
I=3 & 0 & 1 & 1 & 0 & 2
\end{array}
$$
Reading across the rows gives the per-neuron counts. Neuron 1 emits two spikes, neuron 2 emits one, and neuron 3 emits two. These values reveal how activity is distributed among neurons: two neurons contribute equally, while one contributes less.

The same grid can be read down its columns. At time step 1, one neuron spikes. At time step 2, two neurons spike. At time step 3, one neuron spikes. At time step 4, one neuron spikes. This produces the **per-time-step spike count**, which measures how much of the network fires at each moment:
$$
C_t=\sum_{i=1}^{N}s_{i,t}
$$
Here, $C_t$ is the number of spikes emitted across the network at time step $t$, $N$ is the number of included neurons, and the other symbols keep their earlier meanings. This view preserves the changing level of network activity over time. A large value of $C_t$ means many neurons fired during that step, while a small value means activity was sparse at that moment.

To obtain one number for the complete observation window, add every spike event across all included neurons and time steps:
$$
C_{\mathrm{total}}
=
\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{t=1}^{T}s_{i,t}
$$
The symbol $C_{\mathrm{total}}$ is the total spike count, $N$ is the number of neurons being measured, $T$ is the number of time steps in the observation window, $i$ selects a neuron, $t$ selects a time step, and $s_{i,t}$ is the recorded spike event at their intersection. The inner sum counts the spikes produced by one neuron across time. The outer sum then combines those per-neuron counts across the network.

For the three-neuron example, summing the row totals gives
$$
C_{\mathrm{total}}=2+1+2=5
$$
Summing the column totals gives the same result:
$$
C_{\mathrm{total}}=1+2+1+1=5
$$
Both routes count the same five cells containing a $1$. One route groups events by neuron; the other groups them by time. This equality provides a useful check when calculating the metric from an activity record.

Some simulations store a count greater than one in a time bin when a bin covers a wider interval or combines multiple recorded events. The same sum still applies: $s_{i,t}$ then means the number of spike events assigned to neuron $i$ during time bin $t$, rather than a binary indicator. For example, a value of $2$ contributes two events to the total. The counting rule therefore works with binary time steps and with time bins that contain event counts, provided the meaning of each cell is stated clearly.

**Question.** If two networks both produce 5,000 spikes, do they have the same activity pattern?

**Answer.** They have the same total spike count over the chosen measurement windows, while their activity may be distributed differently. One network could spread its spikes evenly across many neurons and time steps. Another could concentrate them in a few neurons or brief bursts. Per-neuron and per-time-step counts expose these differences, whereas the total compresses all events into one number.

**Question.** Why must the number of neurons and time steps be specified when reporting total spike count?

**Answer.** The total grows with the region and duration being observed. Counting more neurons creates more possible sources of spikes, and running for more time steps creates more opportunities for each neuron to fire. A meaningful report therefore identifies the included neurons, the observation window, and whether each recorded value is binary or an event count.

The complete mental model is a neuron-by-time activity grid. Each cell holds a spike indicator or spike-event count. Adding across a row measures one neuron's activity, adding down a column measures the network's activity at one moment, and adding every cell gives total spike count. These three views describe the same recorded events at different levels of compression, allowing network activity to be examined locally before it is reduced to a single performance metric.
