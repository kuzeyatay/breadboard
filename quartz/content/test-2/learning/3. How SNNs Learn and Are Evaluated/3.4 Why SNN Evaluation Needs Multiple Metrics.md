## Why SNN Evaluation Needs Multiple Metrics

Imagine you have already trained several spiking neural networks to recognize the same input, such as a digit, an image, or an event from a sensor. Each model gives a predicted class, so the familiar evaluation question is: "How often is it right?" Accuracy answers that question directly. If a model classifies 978 out of 1000 examples correctly, its accuracy is 97.8%. That number is useful because it tells you how reliable the predictions are. But an SNN is usually chosen because it communicates with spikes, waits over time, and can run efficiently on edge or neuromorphic hardware. Once those constraints matter, accuracy becomes only one part of the judgment.

A conventional neural network often processes dense numerical activity through many layers. Even when the input changes only slightly, the network may still perform a full sequence of numerical operations. A spiking neural network uses discrete spike events instead. A neuron can stay silent, and silence can save work. When a spike occurs, it marks that something has crossed a threshold at a particular moment. The model's computation is therefore tied to both event count and event timing. Evaluation has to measure that behavior, because two SNNs with similar accuracy can place very different demands on time, energy, and hardware.

Accuracy measures prediction quality, but it does not measure deployment cost. Suppose two SNNs both recognize images correctly about 96% of the time. One reaches its answer in 10 milliseconds using a moderate number of spikes. The other needs a longer simulation window and produces many more spikes before its output becomes stable. On a laptop benchmark, those two models may look close. In a battery-powered sensor or robot, they behave very differently. The faster model can respond in real time, while the slower model may miss the moment when a decision is useful. The quieter model consumes less energy, while the spike-heavy model forces more event traffic through the system.

This is why SNN evaluation needs multiple metrics. A metric is a measured quantity used to judge one aspect of performance. Accuracy measures correctness. Latency measures how long inference takes. Energy consumption measures how much power is spent per inference. Spike count measures how much spiking activity the model generates. Convergence behavior measures how the model improves during training, especially how quickly and steadily it approaches useful performance. Together, these metrics turn the behavior of an SNN into something visible and comparable.

Latency is the time between receiving an input and producing a usable output. In an SNN, latency often depends on how long the network must simulate spikes before the decision is clear. A surrogate-gradient SNN can reach low latency, around 10 milliseconds in the reported comparison, while maintaining high accuracy near 97.8%. That combination is valuable for tasks where the answer must arrive quickly, such as gesture recognition, robotics, or edge sensing. A converted SNN may achieve competitive accuracy, but it can require longer simulation windows because it often represents information through firing rates: the model may need enough time to count or accumulate spikes before the output is reliable.

A helpful analogy is listening for applause versus reading a thermometer. A continuous system resembles a thermometer that always gives a value. A spike-based system resembles hearing individual claps. If you only need to know whether the room is excited, a few early claps may be enough. If you need an exact estimate of the crowd's intensity, you may wait longer and count more claps. Waiting longer can improve confidence, but it increases latency and activity. SNN evaluation has to show where that balance lands.

Energy consumption is closely tied to the reason SNNs are attractive. A spike can be treated as an event, and event-driven computation can avoid work during silence. In the reported energy comparison, an ANN CNN uses about 200 millijoules per inference. A converted SNN lowers this to about 20 millijoules, a surrogate-gradient SNN to about 15 millijoules, and an STDP-based SNN to about 5 millijoules. Those values change the practical meaning of model quality. A model with slightly lower accuracy may be preferable if it uses a small fraction of the energy and the application can tolerate the accuracy difference.

Spike count gives a direct window into spiking activity. It counts how many spikes are generated per inference. A converted SNN can use around 20,000 spikes per inference, while a surrogate-gradient SNN may use around 12,000 and an STDP-based SNN around 4,000. The number matters because spikes are the communication events the system must handle. More spikes can mean more computation, more memory traffic, and more energy use. Fewer spikes can mean a sparser and more efficient computation, especially when the hardware is designed to benefit from event-driven activity.

**Question.** If an SNN has the highest accuracy, should it automatically be considered the best SNN?

**Answer.** No. The best SNN depends on the deployment goal. A high-accuracy model that requires many spikes, high energy, or long inference time may be a poor fit for an edge device or real-time task. A slightly less accurate model can be better when it reaches decisions faster and uses far less energy.

Convergence behavior adds another layer because evaluation is not only about the final model. It also asks how the model gets there during training. A model that converges quickly reaches useful performance in fewer training epochs. An epoch is one full pass through the training data. Faster convergence can make training more efficient and can indicate that the learning rule is easier to optimize for the task. Surrogate-gradient SNNs can converge faster by the 20th epoch while maintaining accuracy close to an ANN. STDP-based SNNs can converge more slowly, yet they can produce very low spike counts and very low energy consumption. That difference helps separate training behavior from deployment behavior: one model may be easier to train for accuracy, while another may be more attractive when energy is the dominant constraint.

Multi-metric evaluation separates model quality from deployment cost. Model quality asks whether predictions are correct and whether training is stable. Deployment cost asks whether the model can run within the available time, energy, and hardware limits. These are related, but they are not the same. Accuracy belongs mainly to prediction quality. Latency, energy, and spike count belong mainly to deployment cost. Convergence connects training effort to final usefulness. Looking at all of them prevents one impressive number from hiding a serious weakness.

The tradeoff becomes clearer as tasks scale. On simpler settings, an SNN may stay close to ANN accuracy while using much less energy. On larger image tasks, the accuracy gap can widen: an ANN may reach about 92% while a direct SNN drops closer to 85%. At the same time, energy savings can become even more pronounced, with surrogate-trained SNNs operating at less than 10% of ANN energy costs. A single accuracy number would make the larger-task SNN look weaker. A multi-metric view shows a more precise picture: the model gives up some correctness while gaining major efficiency, and the usefulness of that exchange depends on the application.

Real-time sensing makes this concrete. In gesture-recognition settings on event-based inputs, surrogate SNNs can reach inference latencies around 5-10 milliseconds, while ANN counterparts may take around 20-50 milliseconds. For a robot or edge device, that difference affects whether the system reacts while the event is still happening. A model that is slightly more accurate after a delay may be less useful than a model that is accurate enough immediately. Latency turns prediction into action.

**Question.** Why measure spike count if energy is already measured?

**Answer.** Energy tells you the total cost per inference, while spike count tells you how active the spiking computation was. Two systems might report similar energy under one hardware setup, but the model with fewer spikes usually has a clearer path to efficient event-driven deployment. Spike count also helps explain why one SNN consumes more or less energy than another.

A good mental model is to treat an SNN as both a predictor and an event-processing system. Accuracy asks whether the final answer is right. Latency asks how long the answer takes. Energy asks what the answer costs. Spike count asks how much event traffic the network creates. Convergence asks how the learning process behaves before deployment. An SNN becomes convincing when these measurements fit the intended use together: fast enough for the environment, accurate enough for the task, quiet enough for the hardware, efficient enough for the power budget, and trainable enough to reach that behavior reliably.
