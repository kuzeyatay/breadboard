## The Evaluation Framework

By this point, an SNN already has two connected pieces: a way to represent information as spikes, and a way to learn useful spike patterns through a training method such as surrogate gradients, ANN-to-SNN conversion, or STDP. Now imagine choosing one of these trained networks for a small robot that must recognize a hand gesture while running on a battery. If two models both recognize most gestures correctly, the decision is still unfinished. One model may respond almost immediately but use more energy. Another may use very few spikes and preserve battery life, but need a longer observation window before it becomes confident. A third may train quickly in the lab, but produce so many spikes during inference that the hardware loses much of the efficiency that made spiking computation attractive.

Accuracy gives one answer: "How often is the prediction right?" That answer is valuable, but an SNN is usually built because time, energy, and event sparsity also matter. A conventional dense network tends to compute with continuous-valued activations at every layer, whether the input is changing a lot or only a little. A spiking network tries to gain efficiency by communicating through events. When a neuron stays silent, it spends less communication effort; when it spikes, that spike becomes a discrete piece of computation. The evaluation framework therefore has to ask whether the network's learned behavior actually preserves the benefits of spiking. A model that reaches high accuracy by firing enormous numbers of spikes may behave less like an efficient event-based system and more like a dense computation disguised in spiking form.

A unified comparison starts by placing different SNN paradigms inside the same measurement space. Surrogate-gradient SNNs, converted SNNs, and STDP-based SNNs learn in different ways, so comparing only their final accuracy would be like comparing vehicles only by top speed. A delivery bike, an electric car, and a racing car can each be "fast enough" for different jobs, but the useful choice depends on distance, energy, traffic, and cost. In the same way, an SNN evaluation becomes meaningful when each model is measured along several axes at once: accuracy, latency, energy consumption, spike count, and convergence behavior.

Accuracy measures the fraction of examples classified correctly. If a network sees 1,000 images and predicts 978 of them correctly, its accuracy is 97.8%. This metric tells us whether the learned spike patterns carry enough information for the task. Surrogate-gradient SNNs can come close to ANN-level accuracy, often within a small margin, because training directly adjusts the network toward useful spiking behavior. Converted SNNs can also preserve strong accuracy from a trained ANN, but the conversion often depends on rate-coded spike activity over a simulation window. STDP-based SNNs can be attractive for low-power learning, especially in unsupervised settings, but may give up some accuracy compared with the most optimized supervised methods.

Latency measures how long the model takes to produce an inference. For an SNN, latency is tied to time because the network may need to observe spikes over a short window before the output becomes reliable. If a surrogate-gradient SNN reaches a decision in about 10 milliseconds, that speed can support real-time edge tasks such as gesture recognition or robotics. If another model needs a longer simulation window to accumulate enough spikes, its accuracy might look acceptable in a static benchmark while its response feels too slow in a live system. Latency therefore turns "Can it answer?" into "Can it answer soon enough?"

Energy consumption measures how much work the model costs per inference, often described in millijoules. This is where spiking computation can show its practical strength. An ANN-style model may consume around 200 mJ per inference because its continuous operations remain dense. A converted SNN may reduce that to around 20 mJ, while still producing a high number of spikes. A surrogate-gradient SNN may lower energy further, around 15 mJ, by learning more efficient spike patterns. An STDP-based SNN may reach about 5 mJ per inference by using especially sparse activity. These numbers reveal a tradeoff that accuracy cannot show: the most accurate model may use far more energy than a slightly less accurate model that is much better suited for a battery-powered device.

Spike count measures how many spikes the network generates during one inference. This metric is closely related to energy, but it gives a more direct view of the network's event activity. A converted SNN can produce around 20,000 spikes per inference because rate coding may require many events to represent activation strength. A surrogate-gradient SNN may reduce this to roughly 12,000 spikes by learning when spikes are useful. An STDP-based SNN may use about 4,000 spikes, making its activity much sparser. A beginner might wonder why fewer spikes are automatically desirable if spikes carry information. The answer is that each spike has a cost: it must be generated, transmitted, and processed. Useful spikes are valuable; unnecessary spikes waste the efficiency that event-based computation is meant to provide.

**Question.** If two SNNs have the same accuracy, should the one with fewer spikes always be chosen?

**Answer.** Fewer spikes are usually helpful because they often reduce energy and communication cost, but the final choice still depends on latency, training behavior, and the application. A very sparse model that needs a long time window may be less useful for real-time control than a slightly spike-heavier model that responds in 10 milliseconds. Spike count becomes most informative when read together with latency and energy.

Convergence behavior measures how the model improves during training. A network that reaches strong performance by the 20th epoch is easier to train and tune than one that improves slowly or unstably. Surrogate-gradient training can converge quickly because it uses gradient-based optimization adapted to spiking neurons. STDP-based learning may converge more slowly because it updates connections through local timing relationships between spikes rather than a global supervised error signal. Converted SNNs inherit much of their ability from a pretrained ANN, so their training story includes both the original ANN training and the conversion process. Convergence tells us how expensive it is to obtain the final model, while energy and latency tell us how expensive it is to use the model after training.

These metrics form a tradeoff space. A tradeoff space is the set of choices created when improving one desirable property can affect another. Surrogate-gradient SNNs often occupy a balanced region: high accuracy, low latency, moderate energy, and manageable spike counts. STDP-based SNNs occupy a different region: very low energy and sparse spiking, with somewhat lower accuracy and slower convergence. Converted SNNs can be competitive in accuracy, but may require longer simulation windows and higher spike counts. No model has to be treated as universally best; each model becomes a candidate for a particular job.

Consider two applications. A drone avoiding obstacles needs fast inference because a delayed answer can cause a collision. A surrogate-gradient SNN with about 10 ms latency may be a strong fit, even if another method uses slightly less energy. A remote environmental sensor that wakes occasionally and runs on a tiny battery may prefer an STDP-based SNN that uses very few spikes and only about 5 mJ per inference, even if its accuracy is modestly lower. The same accuracy number cannot distinguish these two design needs, but a multi-metric evaluation can.

**Question.** Why include convergence if deployment metrics like latency and energy are the main reason to use SNNs?

**Answer.** Convergence describes the cost and reliability of getting the model in the first place. A model that is efficient after deployment but difficult to train may be impractical for some projects. A model that converges quickly can be easier to adapt, compare, and improve, especially when the task or dataset changes.

The evaluation framework turns SNN comparison into a map rather than a scoreboard. Accuracy shows whether the network learned the task. Latency shows whether the learned spikes arrive quickly enough. Energy shows whether the computation fits the power budget. Spike count shows how sparse or event-heavy the inference really is. Convergence shows how the training method behaves on the way to that result. Together, these measurements make the behavior of each SNN paradigm visible: learning method shapes spike patterns, spike patterns shape speed and energy, and the best model is the one whose full profile matches the demands of the application.
