Now that a spike train can be read as a timed pattern of events and accuracy can tell us whether the final answer is often correct, learning becomes the process of shaping those spike trains so the network produces useful events at useful moments. Imagine a small gesture-recognition system on a battery-powered device. A camera-like sensor sees motion, turns changes in the scene into spikes, and passes those spikes through layers of spiking neurons. If the network fires too late, the device feels sluggish. If it fires too often, it wastes energy. If it fires in the wrong pattern, the gesture is misclassified. Training an SNN therefore means more than making a label correct; it means deciding how the network should acquire spike patterns while balancing accuracy, latency, energy, spike count, and convergence.

Three major training paradigms give three different answers to that problem. A training paradigm is a broad way of obtaining the network's weights: the adjustable connection strengths that decide how strongly one neuron's spike influences another neuron. The three paths are surrogate gradient descent, ANN-to-SNN conversion, and spike-timing dependent plasticity, usually called STDP. Each path starts from a different view of what learning should be. Surrogate gradient descent treats the SNN as a trainable model whose spike behavior can be optimized directly. ANN-to-SNN conversion begins with a conventional neural network and translates its learned behavior into spikes. STDP lets timing relationships between spikes shape the connections locally, closer to a biological learning rule.

Surrogate gradient descent is the most direct way to train a spiking network with a goal such as classification accuracy. The difficulty comes from the spike itself. A spiking neuron stays quiet while its internal state builds up, then emits a discrete event when it reaches a threshold. That jump from silence to spike is sharp. Ordinary gradient descent works by asking a smooth question: "If this weight changed a tiny amount, how would the loss change?" A hard spike threshold gives a poor answer to that tiny-change question, because a very small change usually leaves the spike unchanged, while another very small change near threshold can suddenly create or remove a spike.

A surrogate gradient solves this by using a smooth training signal in place of the hard spike's unhelpful derivative during learning. The neuron still communicates with spikes during the forward pass: inputs arrive, membrane activity evolves, and output spikes occur at particular times. During the backward pass, where the network assigns credit or blame to its weights, the sharp threshold is treated as if it had a nearby smooth slope. That temporary slope gives each weight a usable direction for adjustment. A connection that helped produce the correct spike pattern is reinforced; a connection that pushed the network toward the wrong pattern is weakened.

A simple way to picture the weight update is: each weight is nudged in the direction that reduces the loss, and the surrogate gradient supplies the missing slope around the spike threshold. If $w$ is a weight, the update has the form $w \leftarrow w - \eta \, g$. Here $w$ is the connection strength being adjusted, $\eta$ is the learning rate that controls the size of the step, and $g$ is the estimated gradient, meaning the training signal that says how the loss changes when that weight changes. The formula lets you compute the new weight after one learning step. In an SNN, $g$ depends on how spikes flowed through time, and the surrogate part makes that time-dependent credit assignment trainable despite discrete spike events.

A useful analogy is learning to tap a drum at the right moment. The final sound is discrete: either the tap happened or it did not. A teacher still needs to give graded feedback such as "a little earlier" or "a little softer." Surrogate gradients provide that graded feedback for spikes. The result is a training path that can reach competitive accuracy while keeping the network genuinely spiking, with low-latency behavior around 10 milliseconds in the compared setting and convergence that can become strong by about 20 epochs.

**Question.** If spikes are discrete events, how can gradient descent adjust a spiking network at all?
**Answer.** Gradient descent needs a slope during training, so surrogate gradient descent keeps the spike-based forward behavior but uses a smooth approximation around the threshold during the learning step. The network still sends spikes, while the training calculation receives enough graded information to decide whether each weight should increase or decrease.

ANN-to-SNN conversion takes a different route. Instead of training the spiking network from scratch, it first trains a conventional artificial neural network, such as a CNN, using continuous-valued activations. Those activations are then represented with spike rates or spike patterns in an SNN. The attraction is practical: conventional networks have mature training methods and often reach strong accuracy. Conversion tries to preserve that useful behavior while moving inference into a spike-based system.

The cost appears when continuous activation values must be expressed through spikes. A large activation may need a high firing rate, and a small activation may need a lower firing rate. To estimate those rates reliably, the converted SNN often needs a longer simulation window, meaning it waits across more time steps to collect enough spikes. In the compared results, converted SNNs can remain competitive, but they tend to use higher spike counts, around 20,000 spikes per inference in the energy-and-spike comparison, and can require longer delays around 20 milliseconds. The mental picture is a translator that preserves much of the original message but spends extra time and extra events spelling out continuous values in a spike language.

STDP begins from the timing of spikes themselves. Spike-timing dependent plasticity changes a connection according to the relative timing of a presynaptic spike, which arrives from an input neuron, and a postsynaptic spike, which is produced by the receiving neuron. When an input spike repeatedly arrives just before the receiving neuron fires, that input looks causally useful, so the connection can strengthen. When the timing suggests the input arrived too late or failed to help produce the output spike, the connection can weaken. Learning comes from local timing relationships rather than a global error signal sent backward through the whole network.

This makes STDP especially natural for sparse, event-driven learning. If few spikes occur, few updates are needed, and the network can become very energy efficient. In the compared measurements, STDP-based SNNs reach especially low activity, around 4,000 spikes per inference, and energy consumption around 5 millijoules per inference. The tradeoff is that this local timing rule usually converges more slowly and can plateau earlier, especially when the task needs precise supervised correction. It learns like a set of synapses discovering repeated temporal patterns from experience: efficient and biologically inspired, but less directly steered toward a labeled target than surrogate gradient training.

**Question.** Why would anyone use STDP if surrogate gradient training reaches strong accuracy more directly?
**Answer.** STDP is valuable when sparse activity and low energy are central design goals. Its learning rule uses local spike timing, so it can produce networks with very low spike counts and low energy per inference. Surrogate gradient training gives stronger direct control over task loss, while STDP offers a timing-based path that favors efficiency.

Choosing among these three paths means choosing the shape of the whole system's behavior. Surrogate gradient descent directly sculpts spike trains toward a task objective, so it tends to balance competitive accuracy, fast convergence, and low latency. ANN-to-SNN conversion borrows the strength of already-trained continuous networks, then pays for translation with more spikes or longer simulation windows. STDP lets timing relationships organize the network with sparse activity and very low energy, while convergence can be slower because learning is guided locally. A spiking network learns by arranging when neurons fire, how often they fire, and which connections make those events more likely; the training paradigm decides whether that arrangement is driven by a smooth task-loss signal, inherited from a converted continuous model, or built from the timing patterns of the spikes themselves.
