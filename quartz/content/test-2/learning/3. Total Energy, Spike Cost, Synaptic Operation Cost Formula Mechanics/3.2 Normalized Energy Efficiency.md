### Normalized Energy Efficiency

After separating total energy into the costs created by spikes and synaptic operations, one practical question remains: if two spiking systems both solve the same task, how should we compare them when one is slightly more accurate and the other spends much less energy? Imagine a small vision sensor on a battery-powered device. Each inference is one attempt to recognize what it sees. The device can spend energy to move information through synapses, generate spikes, and update neuron states, but its battery does not care whether that energy produced a useful answer. Accuracy tells us how often the answer is correct. Energy per inference tells us how much physical work the system spends for one answer. A useful efficiency measure has to hold those two quantities together.

A model that reaches high accuracy while spending a large amount of energy may be suitable when power is abundant. A model that spends very little energy while losing too much accuracy may be unsuitable when mistakes are expensive. Normalized energy efficiency gives a single ratio for this tradeoff by asking: how much correctness does the system deliver for each joule it spends?
$$
\text{Normalized Energy Efficiency} = \frac{\text{Accuracy}}{\text{Energy}}
$$
Here, **normalized energy efficiency** means accuracy delivered per unit of energy. **Accuracy** is the model's correctness on the task, usually written as a percentage such as $97.8\%$ or as a fraction such as $0.978$. **Energy** is the energy consumed for one inference. A **joule**, written $J$, is the unit of energy used in the denominator. The formula lets you compute an accuracy-per-joule score: the numerator says how much task performance was achieved, and the denominator says how much energy was spent to achieve it.

The denominator has to be in joules for the unit to be clear. If energy is reported in millijoules, written $mJ$, it must be converted before computing accuracy per joule:
$$
1\,mJ = 0.001\,J
$$
So $15\,mJ = 0.015\,J$, and $5\,mJ = 0.005\,J$. This conversion changes the scale of the number but not the meaning of the comparison. The ratio still asks how much accuracy is obtained for one joule of energy.

Consider a surrogate-gradient spiking network with about $97.8\%$ accuracy and $15\,mJ$ of energy per inference. Written as a fraction, the accuracy is $0.978$, and the energy is $0.015\,J$. Its normalized energy efficiency is
$$
\frac{0.978}{0.015\,J} = 65.2 \text{ accuracy-fraction per joule}
$$
This means that, at this operating point, each joule corresponds to about $65.2$ units of fractional accuracy across repeated one-inference energy costs. The number is easiest to use comparatively: another model with a larger value delivers more correctness for the same energy budget, or similar correctness with less energy.

Now consider an STDP-based spiking network with about $95.5\%$ accuracy and $5\,mJ$ per inference. Written as a fraction, the accuracy is $0.955$, and the energy is $0.005\,J$. Its normalized energy efficiency is
$$
\frac{0.955}{0.005\,J} = 191 \text{ accuracy-fraction per joule}
$$
The accuracy is lower than $97.8\%$, but the energy per inference is much smaller. The ratio increases because the denominator shrinks a lot while the numerator remains reasonably high. This is the central mechanics of the measure: normalized energy efficiency rewards preserving accuracy while reducing joules. A small loss in accuracy can still produce a much higher efficiency score if the energy reduction is large enough.

The same formula also explains why a conventional dense artificial network can look strong on accuracy while weak on energy efficiency. If a model spends around $200\,mJ$, that is $0.2\,J$ per inference. Even very high accuracy must be divided by a much larger denominator. A spiking model that spends $20\,mJ$, $15\,mJ$, or $5\,mJ$ starts from a much smaller energy cost, so its ratio can improve dramatically when accuracy stays close enough to the high-energy baseline.

A beginner confusion often comes from treating "energy efficient" as the same as "lowest energy." The formula gives a sharper picture. Lowest energy is only the denominator. Normalized energy efficiency includes the numerator too. If two models spend the same $0.005\,J$, the one with higher accuracy has higher efficiency. If two models have the same accuracy, the one with lower energy has higher efficiency. If one model changes both accuracy and energy, the ratio shows which change dominates.

**Question.** If Model A has $98\%$ accuracy and uses $20\,mJ$, while Model B has $96\%$ accuracy and uses $10\,mJ$, which has higher normalized energy efficiency?

**Answer.** Convert energy to joules first: $20\,mJ = 0.020\,J$, and $10\,mJ = 0.010\,J$. Model A has $0.98 / 0.020 = 49$ accuracy-fraction per joule. Model B has $0.96 / 0.010 = 96$ accuracy-fraction per joule. Model B has higher normalized energy efficiency because its energy use is cut in half while its accuracy decreases only slightly.

The formula also fixes which variables carry the claim. If someone says a spiking network is more energy efficient, the claim should point to both measured accuracy and measured energy per inference. Spike count and synaptic operation cost help explain where the energy came from, because fewer spikes usually mean fewer event-driven operations, and fewer operations usually reduce energy. Yet the normalized efficiency score itself uses the final task accuracy and the final energy in joules. Spike mechanics explain the cause; the ratio records the outcome.

**Question.** Why does the formula divide by energy instead of subtracting energy from accuracy?

**Answer.** Accuracy and energy have different units, so subtracting them would mix unlike quantities: correctness and joules. Dividing accuracy by energy creates a rate-like measure, similar to "distance per liter" for a vehicle. It tells how much useful result is obtained per unit of resource spent.

A good mental model is to imagine each inference as a small purchase. Energy is the price paid in joules. Accuracy is the value received as correct task performance. Normalized energy efficiency asks how much value the system gets for each joule it spends. A spiking system can improve that value by raising accuracy, lowering spike-driven and synaptic energy costs, or doing both together.
