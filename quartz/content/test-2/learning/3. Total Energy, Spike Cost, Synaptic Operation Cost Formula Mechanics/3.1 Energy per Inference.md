A small edge camera has to decide whether a doorway is empty or occupied while running from a limited battery. Most frames show almost the same quiet hallway. If the model spends the same amount of energy on every pixel-like value at every moment, the battery drains even when the scene barely changes. A spiking neural network gives the computation a cheaper rhythm: silence carries information, and activity appears as brief spike events only when neurons have enough input to fire. Energy per inference asks a practical question: how much energy does one completed decision cost?

An **inference** is one full use of the trained model to produce an output, such as classifying one image, recognizing one gesture, or deciding whether a sensor pattern matches a known category. The word **energy** here means the electrical work spent to carry out that one decision. It is often measured in **millijoules**, written **mJ**. One joule is a unit of energy, and one millijoule is one-thousandth of a joule. That small unit is useful because edge and mobile inference often lives in a tiny energy budget: a few millijoules saved on one decision can become a large battery-life difference after thousands or millions of decisions.

A spiking model spends energy in two closely related ways. A neuron can emit a **spike**, which is a discrete event sent at a particular time. That event has a cost because the system must represent it, route it, and use it in later computation. A spike also travels across connections to other neurons. Each time a spike interacts with a connection, the model performs a **synaptic operation**. A synaptic operation is the work of using a connection weight to pass influence from one neuron to another. If the connection is excitatory, the incoming spike pushes the receiving neuron's membrane potential upward; if it is inhibitory, the incoming spike pushes that potential downward. The receiving neuron keeps accumulating these influences over time, leaks some stored potential, and fires when its threshold is crossed.

This gives energy a countable structure. Instead of treating energy use as a vague property of the whole model, energy per inference can be estimated from two quantities that can be counted during the inference: how many spikes occurred, and how many synaptic operations those spikes caused. A sparse inference, where only a small number of neurons fire, naturally has fewer events to pay for. A dense inference, where many neurons fire repeatedly, creates a larger bill because more spikes and more connection updates must be handled.

The relationship can be written as:
$$
E_{\text{total}} = (N_{\text{spikes}} \times E_{\text{spike}}) + (N_{\text{synops}} \times E_{\text{synop}})
$$
Here, $E_{\text{total}}$ is the **total energy** used for one inference, usually expressed in millijoules. $N_{\text{spikes}}$ is the number of spikes generated during that inference. $E_{\text{spike}}$ is the **spike cost**, meaning the energy cost of one spike event. $N_{\text{synops}}$ is the number of **synaptic operations** performed during the inference. $E_{\text{synop}}$ is the **synaptic operation cost**, meaning the energy cost of one operation across a connection. The formula lets you compute the total energy by adding the energy spent on spike events to the energy spent on synaptic operations.

A simple worked interpretation makes the bookkeeping concrete. Suppose one inference produces $4{,}000$ spikes. Suppose those spikes cause $80{,}000$ synaptic operations. If each spike costs $0.0002$ mJ and each synaptic operation costs $0.00005$ mJ, then the spike part is:
$$
4{,}000 \times 0.0002 = 0.8\text{ mJ}
$$
The synaptic-operation part is:
$$
80{,}000 \times 0.00005 = 4.0\text{ mJ}
$$
Adding them gives:
$$
E_{\text{total}} = 0.8 + 4.0 = 4.8\text{ mJ}
$$
That value means one completed decision costs about $4.8$ millijoules under those assumed per-event costs. The example also shows why spike count alone gives an incomplete energy estimate. The number of spikes tells you how active the network was, while the number of synaptic operations tells you how much connection-level work that activity triggered. A single spike can influence many downstream connections, so synaptic operations often form a large part of the total.

**Question.** If two spiking models produce the same number of spikes, do they necessarily use the same energy per inference?

**Answer.** They use the same spike-event energy only if their spike cost is the same, but their total energy can still differ because the spikes may cause different numbers of synaptic operations. A model with more fan-out, more active connections, or longer propagation through layers can perform more synaptic operations even with the same spike count, increasing $N_{\text{synops}} \times E_{\text{synop}}$.

Energy comparisons become easier to interpret once each model is treated as paying for events. A conventional artificial neural network can spend a high amount of energy per inference because it works with continuous-valued operations throughout the model. In the reported comparison, the ANN uses about $200$ mJ per inference. A converted spiking network lowers energy to about $20$ mJ, while still producing a relatively high spike count of about $20{,}000$ spikes because rate-coded activity can require many spikes to express values. A surrogate-gradient spiking network reduces the estimate further to about $15$ mJ with about $12{,}000$ spikes. An STDP-based spiking network reaches about $5$ mJ with about $4{,}000$ spikes.

Those numbers follow the same accounting logic as the formula. When a model can make a decision with fewer spike events and fewer connection updates, the total energy drops. The movement from roughly $200$ mJ to $20$, $15$, or $5$ mJ is a movement from continuous dense computation toward sparse event-driven computation. The lowest-energy case has the sparsest spike activity, which means the network spends less time paying for spike events and less time triggering synaptic operations. The energy value is therefore tied to the model's temporal behavior: when neurons stay silent, the inference avoids unnecessary event costs; when neurons fire often, the inference accumulates more cost.

A beginner confusion often comes from reading "low spike count" as automatically meaning "best model." Low spike count is a strong energy signal, but an inference still has to produce the correct output within a useful time window. A model that fires very little may save energy, while another model may use more spikes to preserve accuracy or reduce latency. Energy per inference is one part of a model-selection picture that also includes accuracy and speed. For battery-powered or always-on systems, the energy part becomes especially visible because every repeated inference spends from the same limited budget.

**Question.** Why measure energy per inference instead of only reporting total energy for a long run?

**Answer.** Energy per inference gives a normalized cost for one decision. If one model is tested on more inputs than another, total run energy can be misleading. Per-inference energy lets you compare how expensive each decision is, then scale that estimate to the expected workload, such as thousands of sensor decisions per hour on an edge device.

The mental model is simple: an inference is a short episode of activity, and the model pays for what happens during that episode. Spikes are the visible events. Synaptic operations are the connection-level consequences of those events. Total energy adds both costs into one number, usually in millijoules, so a sparse spiking network can be evaluated by the actual work it performs rather than by architecture name alone. When fewer neurons fire and fewer connections are used, the energy ledger shrinks; when activity spreads widely, the ledger grows.
