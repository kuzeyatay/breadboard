## Accuracy, Latency, Energy, Spike Count, and Convergence

Having learned how classification accuracy, inference latency, inference energy, total spike count, and convergence epoch describe different parts of an SNN's behavior, imagine choosing a training strategy for a system with a fixed energy budget and a strict response deadline. One strategy produces the most correct classifications, another responds sooner, and another uses fewer spikes and less energy. Declaring a winner requires a purpose: the system's operating requirements determine which strengths carry the most weight.

A five-metric evaluation places all five measurements into one decision process. **Accuracy** measures the proportion of classifications the model gets right. **Latency** measures how long the model takes to produce an inference. **Energy per inference** measures the energy consumed each time the model processes an input. **Spike count** records the total number of spike events generated during that inference. **Convergence epoch** indicates how many passes through the training data are needed before performance settles near its achieved level. Together, these metrics describe predictive quality, response speed, computational cost, event activity, and training behavior.

Each metric answers a distinct practical question. Accuracy asks, "How reliable are the predictions?" Latency asks, "How quickly does a prediction arrive?" Energy asks, "How expensive is each prediction to execute?" Spike count asks, "How much event-based activity does the computation generate?" Convergence asks, "How quickly does learning reach stable performance?" A model can answer one question favorably while giving a less favorable answer to another.

Consider surrogate-gradient and STDP-based SNNs. A surrogate-gradient SNN reaches about 97.8% accuracy with latency around 10 milliseconds and energy consumption around 15 millijoules per inference. It produces roughly 12,000 spikes per inference and approaches its reported performance by the twentieth epoch. This combination supports a model-selection decision that values accurate and rapid responses while accepting moderate energy use and spiking activity.

An STDP-based SNN reaches about 95.5% accuracy while consuming around 5 millijoules per inference and generating roughly 4,000 spikes. Its learning converges more slowly. The lower energy and sparser activity are attractive when the operating budget favors minimal computation, while the reduced accuracy and slower learning shape where that strategy fits. The difference between 97.8% and 95.5% accuracy amounts to about 2.3 percentage points, while the energy changes from 15 to 5 millijoules, a threefold reduction. Spike activity also falls from about 12,000 events to about 4,000, again a threefold reduction. Choosing between these strategies therefore means deciding how much accuracy is worth exchanging for lower energy and fewer spikes.

This is the **accuracy-energy-latency tradeoff**: improving one operational objective can accompany a weaker result on another. The surrogate-gradient model provides the stronger combination of accuracy and latency in the comparison, while the STDP model provides the strongest energy and spike-count results. The metrics describe different resources, so they cannot be merged casually. A percentage point of accuracy, a millijoule of energy, a millisecond of delay, and a spike event have different meanings and consequences.

Converted SNNs add another useful comparison. They reduce energy consumption to about 20 millijoules per inference, yet generate roughly 20,000 spikes and require longer simulation windows. Their performance remains competitive, but their higher activity and longer windows affect both computational cost and response time. Looking only at their energy improvement would hide their spike burden. Looking only at predictive performance would hide the longer temporal processing. Five-metric evaluation keeps these consequences visible within the same judgment.

Spike count and energy often move together because sparse event activity can reduce the amount of computation performed. The reported values illustrate that relationship: approximately 20,000 spikes accompany 20 millijoules for the converted SNN, about 12,000 accompany 15 millijoules for the surrogate-gradient SNN, and about 4,000 accompany 5 millijoules for the STDP-based SNN. Spike count still deserves its own place in the evaluation because it describes internal activity directly, whereas energy measures the resulting consumption per inference. Their connection is useful evidence rather than a guarantee that they always carry identical decision value.

Latency and convergence also describe separate timescales. Latency concerns one completed inference, such as the roughly 10 milliseconds needed by the surrogate-gradient SNN. Convergence concerns the learning process across epochs, with an epoch representing one pass through the training data. A strategy may learn slowly and then perform each inference efficiently, or learn quickly while requiring more activity during inference. Keeping these timescales separate prevents training speed from being mistaken for response speed.

**Question.** If the STDP-based SNN uses the least energy and generates the fewest spikes, why would anyone choose the surrogate-gradient SNN?

**Answer.** The surrogate-gradient SNN offers higher reported accuracy, about 97.8% compared with 95.5%, along with latency around 10 milliseconds and faster convergence by the twentieth epoch. A selection process that gives high priority to prediction quality and rapid responses can therefore favor the surrogate-gradient strategy. A process dominated by energy limits can favor STDP. The preferred strategy follows the priority assigned to each metric.

A disciplined choice begins by identifying the constraints that the system must satisfy. A response deadline turns latency into a threshold: strategies slower than that deadline leave the candidate set. An energy budget creates a similar threshold for millijoules per inference. Accuracy can then distinguish the candidates that remain, while spike count reveals the density of their event activity and convergence indicates the training effort needed to reach stable performance. In another setting, energy may become the first filter, followed by spike count and then accuracy. This ordering is **metric prioritization**: deciding which measurements act as requirements, which guide optimization, and which serve as secondary evidence.

When priorities remain partly flexible, **Pareto-style reasoning** provides a clear mental model. A strategy is attractive when improving one of its metrics would require giving up ground on another important metric. The surrogate-gradient and STDP results occupy different useful positions: one favors accuracy, latency, and convergence, while the other favors energy and spike sparsity. Neither position supplies a universal ranking because each serves a different balance of goals.

**Question.** Should the five metrics be averaged into one score to make selection easier?

**Answer.** A combined score becomes meaningful only after the decision maker assigns justified weights and compatible scales. Otherwise, averaging can conceal decisive constraints. A model that exceeds a latency limit cannot compensate simply by saving energy, and an energy-constrained system may gain little from extra accuracy if each inference consumes too much of its budget. Examining thresholds and priorities before any combined score preserves the meaning of each measurement.

The practical picture is a five-axis profile rather than a single finish line. Accuracy describes correctness, latency describes immediate response, energy describes per-inference cost, spike count exposes event activity, and convergence describes the path taken during learning. Model selection becomes principled when these measurements are read together, filtered through explicit constraints, and prioritized according to the intended use.
