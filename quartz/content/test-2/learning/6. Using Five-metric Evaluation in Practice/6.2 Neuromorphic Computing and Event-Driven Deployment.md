## Neuromorphic Computing and Event-Driven Deployment

The five-metric view developed earlier-accuracy, latency, energy, spike count, and convergence-becomes especially useful when an SNN leaves the training environment and begins performing inference on a physical device. Imagine that the trained network receives a stream of spikes while the device operates under a limited energy budget. Each incoming spike may trigger useful computation, while every unnecessary spike consumes time and energy. Deployment therefore turns the five metrics into connected engineering quantities: accuracy describes the quality of the result, latency describes how quickly it arrives, energy measures the cost of producing it, spike count estimates the amount of event activity involved, and convergence indicates how difficult the network was to train before deployment.

Neuromorphic computing provides a natural execution context for this kind of network. A **neuromorphic system** uses a computing organization inspired by networks of neurons and connections, with support for information represented as discrete events. An SNN already communicates in this form. A neuron accumulates incoming events and emits a spike when its state reaches a firing condition. The receiving neurons then update their own states in response. Computation follows the movement of spikes through the network.

This leads to **event-driven execution**: operations are activated by events rather than performed continuously at every possible location. Consider a short interval in which only three neurons fire. An event-driven implementation can process those three events and the connections they affect. Neurons receiving no events can remain quiet during that interval. The workload therefore follows actual spiking activity.

A useful simplified relationship is
$$
E_{\text{inference}} \approx N_{\text{events}}\,E_{\text{event}}
$$
Where $E_{\text{inference}}$ is the energy used for one inference, $N_{\text{events}}$ is the number of spike-triggered processing events during that inference, and $E_{\text{event}}$ is the average energy cost of handling one event. This formula estimates how total inference energy grows with event activity. Real systems include additional costs, but the relationship gives a practical mental model: more spike-driven operations generally require more energy.

Suppose one network produces 2,000 processing events for an input while another produces 10,000 events under the same deployment conditions. If their average event costs are similar, the second network creates roughly five times as much event-processing work. Its accuracy may still be better, so model selection requires both numbers. Spike count becomes an operational bridge between a network's internal behavior and its likely deployment cost.

Sparse activity strengthens this advantage. **Sparse processing** means that only a small fraction of the possible neurons or connections are active at a given time. A sparse SNN can spend long periods in relative silence and perform concentrated work when spikes arrive. Neuromorphic execution can use that silence as an opportunity to avoid operations. This alignment between sparse representation and event-driven execution explains why SNN energy savings are especially relevant on hardware designed to process spikes.

Energy efficiency still depends on the activity pattern produced by the trained network. A model that emits many spikes over a long simulation window creates a heavier workload than one that reaches a decision with fewer spikes. Converted SNNs illustrate this trade-off: they can preserve competitive accuracy, yet may require higher spike counts and longer inference windows. Directly trained networks can use their temporal dynamics to reduce activity, while STDP-trained networks can reach especially low spike counts and energy use at the cost of slower convergence. The training method therefore shapes the event stream that the deployment platform must execute.

**Question.** If neuromorphic execution processes spikes efficiently, why should we still measure spike count?

**Answer.** Efficient event handling lowers the cost of each event, while spike count determines how many such costs accumulate. A network producing twice as many relevant processing events creates roughly twice the event-driven workload when other conditions remain similar. Spike count also helps explain changes in latency and energy that accuracy alone cannot reveal. Measuring it reveals whether the model is using the sparse behavior that event-driven execution is designed to exploit.

Latency adds the dimension of time. An SNN may need to observe spikes across a simulation window before its output becomes reliable. A short window can support a quick decision, while a longer window gives the network more time to accumulate evidence. The resulting latency can be expressed simply as
$$
L = t_{\text{decision}}-t_{\text{start}}
$$
Where $L$ is inference latency, $t_{\text{start}}$ is the time at which inference begins, and $t_{\text{decision}}$ is the time at which the network produces its usable decision. This computes the elapsed time experienced by the deployment system.

Event-driven hardware can process incoming activity as it occurs, which supports real-time operation when the network reaches a decision quickly. Yet sparse activity and low latency can pull in different directions. Waiting longer may improve the evidence available to the network, while also extending inference and allowing more spikes to occur. A practical evaluation therefore asks how much accuracy is gained by the additional time and activity.

Low-power deployment emerges from balancing the same quantities. A candidate model should reach the required accuracy, do so within an acceptable latency, and keep its energy and spike count within the device's operating budget. Convergence supplies a complementary training perspective. A model that achieves strong inference metrics after a manageable training process may be preferable to one whose learning behavior remains slow or unstable, even when their final accuracies appear similar.

**Question.** Can the model with the lowest energy always be selected for neuromorphic deployment?

**Answer.** Model selection follows the deployment requirement. A very low-energy network may converge slowly during training or provide lower accuracy than the task requires. Another network may consume slightly more energy while producing an accurate decision within a shorter latency. The five metrics reveal these trade-offs together, allowing the designer to choose a model whose complete behavior fits the intended operating conditions.

Neuromorphic computing and SNNs fit together because they share the same basic unit of activity: the discrete spike event. Sparse firing reduces the number of active operations, event-driven execution performs work where those spikes occur, and scalable spike processing carries the events through larger networks without requiring every neuron to be equally active at every moment. The resulting mental model is a chain: training determines temporal activity, temporal activity determines spike count and decision time, spike count and time shape energy and latency, and neuromorphic execution turns that sparse event stream into physical computation. Accuracy remains the measure of whether the chain produces a useful answer, while the other four metrics reveal what it costs to produce that answer in practice.
