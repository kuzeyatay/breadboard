## Threshold Crossing, Spike Emission, and Reset

A Leaky Integrate-and-Fire neuron already has a changing **membrane potential**: incoming signals push it upward, while leakage gradually pulls it back down. Imagine that several input pulses arrive close together. The first pulse raises the membrane potential a little. Before leakage can erase much of that rise, another pulse arrives and raises it again. With enough input arriving over a short enough interval, the potential climbs toward a boundary that determines whether the neuron will communicate an event to the rest of the network.

That boundary is the **firing threshold**. It is a predetermined level of membrane potential that acts as the neuron's decision point. While the membrane potential remains below this level, the neuron continues integrating incoming input and losing some accumulated potential through leakage. When the potential reaches or surpasses the threshold, the neuron emits a spike.

The threshold turns gradual internal accumulation into a discrete output event. The membrane potential may take many values as it rises and falls, but the emitted spike represents a single occurrence at a particular time. Downstream neurons can receive that event as an input and begin integrating its effect into their own membrane potentials. In this way, a changing internal state produces a precisely timed signal that can travel through the network.

Consider a neuron whose threshold is reached after three closely spaced input pulses. The first pulse raises the potential, and a small amount leaks away. The second pulse arrives soon enough to build on what remains. The third pulse adds the final increase that carries the potential across the threshold. The neuron then emits one spike at that crossing time. The sequence can be pictured as filling a container with a small drain: each pulse adds water, the drain continuously removes some, and the threshold is a marked line near the top. Water must be added strongly or rapidly enough for the level to reach the line.

The timing of the inputs therefore affects the outcome. Suppose the same three pulses are spread far apart. After each pulse, leakage has more time to reduce the membrane potential before the following pulse arrives. The neuron may remain below threshold throughout the sequence and emit no spike. Closely spaced inputs can accumulate, while widely separated inputs may fade before they combine. The spike time records the moment when accumulated input finally overcomes the ongoing leakage.

Input strength has a similar effect. A strong pulse produces a larger rise in membrane potential than a weak pulse. One sufficiently strong input may reach the firing threshold quickly, whereas several weaker inputs may need to arrive close together. Leakage sets how long earlier inputs continue to contribute: rapid leakage shortens that window, and slow leakage allows their effects to persist longer. The threshold sets how much accumulated potential is required. Together, input strength, input timing, leakage, and threshold determine whether a spike occurs and when it occurs.

At the instant of threshold crossing, **spike emission** records the neuron's firing event. A useful compact condition is
$$
V(t) \geq V_{\mathrm{th}}
$$
Where $V(t)$ is the membrane potential at time $t$, $V_{\mathrm{th}}$ is the firing threshold, and the symbol $\geq$ means "greater than or equal to." This condition lets you determine whether the neuron fires at a given moment. If the condition is satisfied, a spike is emitted at that time. The crossing time is especially informative because two neurons can emit the same number of spikes while emitting them at different moments.

Emission is immediately followed by **reset behavior**. The neuron lowers its membrane potential to a reset level, giving subsequent inputs a fresh accumulation process. Without this reset, a potential that had already crossed the threshold could remain high and repeatedly satisfy the firing condition. Reset separates one firing event from the buildup toward another, so each new spike requires renewed input accumulation.

For example, imagine that the membrane potential reaches threshold at 12 milliseconds. The neuron emits a spike at 12 milliseconds and returns its internal potential to the reset level. An input arriving at 13 milliseconds then contributes to the new post-reset state. Earlier input still influenced the spike at 12 milliseconds, but the reset prevents that accumulated potential from automatically producing another spike. A later spike occurs only if incoming signals again raise the membrane potential to threshold.

A common beginner confusion is to picture the spike as the high membrane-potential value itself. The membrane potential is the neuron's internal, continuously changing state, while the spike is the discrete event produced when that state crosses the threshold. The reset changes the internal state after emission. Thinking in terms of a trigger clarifies the sequence: the rising potential reaches the trigger level, the neuron sends an event, and the potential returns to its reset level.

**Question.** If several input pulses arrive but the neuron never reaches its firing threshold, what does it emit?

**Answer.** It emits no spike during that interval. The pulses can still raise the membrane potential, but leakage may reduce their combined effect before the threshold is reached. A later input can contribute to whatever potential remains, provided leakage has not already pulled it back down.

**Question.** Why does the neuron reset immediately after emitting a spike?

**Answer.** Reset begins a new accumulation cycle. It removes the threshold-crossing state that produced the completed spike and requires subsequent input to build the membrane potential toward threshold again. This gives each spike a distinct causal sequence: accumulation, threshold crossing, emission, and reset.

The full mechanism can be held as one continuous mental picture. Incoming signals add to the membrane potential, leakage steadily weakens stored influence, and the firing threshold marks the level required for communication. When temporally accumulated input carries the potential across that level, the neuron emits a spike at the crossing time. The reset then closes that event and prepares the neuron to integrate future input. A LIF neuron therefore behaves like a leaky accumulator connected to a trigger: it gathers evidence over time, announces a threshold crossing with a spike, and returns to a starting state for the next decision.
