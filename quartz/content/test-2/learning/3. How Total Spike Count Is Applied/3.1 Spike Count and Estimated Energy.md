## Spike Count and Estimated Energy

Once a spike has been represented as a binary event at a particular neuron and time step, the network's activity can be counted across an entire inference. Imagine following five neurons for four time steps. At each time step, every neuron either emits a spike, recorded as $1$, or remains silent, recorded as $0$. A single row of zeros and ones describes activity at one moment, while the full grid describes activity throughout the inference. To measure how active the network was, we add every entry in that grid.

Let $s_i[t]$ represent the spike event produced by neuron $i$ at time step $t$:
$$
S_i[t] =
\begin{cases}
1, & \text{if neuron } i \text{ emits a spike at time } t,\\
0, & \text{if neuron } i \text{ is silent at time } t.
\end{cases}
$$
Here, $i$ is the **neuron index**, which identifies one neuron among the neurons being measured. The symbol $t$ is the **time-step index**, which identifies one moment in the simulation window. The value $s_i[t]$ is a **spike event**: a binary record of whether that neuron fired at that moment.

The **total spike count** is obtained by summing these binary events over all included neurons and all included time steps:
$$
N_{\text{spike}}
=
\sum_{t=1}^{T}
\sum_{i=1}^{N}
S_i[t]
$$
In this expression, $N_{\text{spike}}$ is the total number of spikes generated during the inference, $N$ is the number of included neurons, and $T$ is the number of included time steps. The inner sum counts how many neurons spike at one time step. The outer sum then combines those per-time-step counts across the complete simulation window. Because every $s_i[t]$ is either $0$ or $1$, each emitted spike adds exactly one to the total.

Suppose three neurons are observed for four time steps, producing the following events:
$$
\begin{array}{c|cccc}
 & t=1 & t=2 & t=3 & t=4\\
\hline
I=1 & 0 & 1 & 0 & 1\\
I=2 & 0 & 0 & 1 & 0\\
I=3 & 1 & 0 & 1 & 0
\end{array}
$$
The first neuron contributes two spikes, the second contributes one, and the third contributes two. Adding all twelve entries gives
$$
N_{\text{spike}} = 2+1+2=5
$$
The count of five compresses the entire activity grid into one measure of event frequency. It says how many spike events occurred, while the grid still carries the information about which neurons fired and when. Two inferences can therefore have the same total spike count even if their spikes occur at different neurons or different times.

A spike also causes computational work as it travels through the network. When a neuron emits an event, that event can be delivered through one or more outgoing synaptic connections. Processing one spike along one such connection is counted as a **synaptic operation**. The **synaptic-operation count**, written $N_{\text{synop}}$, is the number of these connection-level operations performed during the inference.

Consider a neuron that emits one spike and has four active outgoing connections. Its one spike contributes $1$ to $N_{\text{spike}}$, while delivering that spike across the four connections can contribute $4$ to $N_{\text{synop}}$. If another spiking neuron reaches ten connections, its event can contribute ten more synaptic operations. Network connectivity therefore determines how much downstream work follows from the recorded spike activity.

The operation counts become an energy estimate when each kind of operation is assigned a cost. Let $\varepsilon_{\text{spike}}$ be the **spike-event cost**, meaning the estimated energy required to handle one spike event. Let $\varepsilon_{\text{synop}}$ be the **synaptic-operation cost**, meaning the estimated energy required to process one spike through one synaptic connection. The two energy contributions are
$$
E_{\text{spike}}
=
N_{\text{spike}}\varepsilon_{\text{spike}}
$$
And
$$
E_{\text{synop}}
=
N_{\text{synop}}\varepsilon_{\text{synop}}
$$
The estimated energy for the inference is their sum:
$$
E_{\text{total}}
=
N_{\text{spike}}\varepsilon_{\text{spike}}
+
N_{\text{synop}}\varepsilon_{\text{synop}}
$$
Here, $E_{\text{total}}$ is the **estimated inference energy**, $N_{\text{spike}}$ is the total spike count, $\varepsilon_{\text{spike}}$ is the energy cost per spike event, $N_{\text{synop}}$ is the total synaptic-operation count, and $\varepsilon_{\text{synop}}$ is the energy cost per synaptic operation. Both products have units of energy: an operation count multiplied by energy per operation gives the energy attributed to that class of work.

For a concrete calculation, suppose an inference generates $5{,}000$ spikes and performs $40{,}000$ synaptic operations. Assume the cost model assigns $2$ energy units to each spike event and $0.5$ energy units to each synaptic operation. The spike-event contribution is
$$
E_{\text{spike}}
=
5{,}000 \times 2
=
10{,}000
\text{ energy units}
$$
While the synaptic contribution is
$$
E_{\text{synop}}
=
40{,}000 \times 0.5
=
20{,}000
\text{ energy units}
$$
Adding them gives
$$
E_{\text{total}}
=
10{,}000+20{,}000
=
30{,}000
\text{ energy units}
$$
The example shows why spike count and energy must be connected through an explicit cost model. The spikes contribute one part of the estimate, and the synaptic operations triggered by activity contribute another. A network with relatively sparse spiking can still perform substantial synaptic work when each event reaches many connections. Conversely, changes in connectivity, simulation length, or per-operation costs can change the energy estimate even when the spike count remains fixed.

**Question.** Why do we sum over both neurons and time steps when counting spikes?

**Answer.** A spike is identified by both where it occurs and when it occurs. Summing only over neurons at one time step gives an instantaneous activity count. Summing only over time for one neuron gives that neuron's activity count. The double sum includes every neuron-time pair in the inference, so it produces the total number of emitted events.

**Question.** If two networks generate the same number of spikes, will their estimated inference energy be the same?

**Answer.** Their spike-event contributions match when they use the same spike-event cost. Their total estimates can differ because the spikes may produce different numbers of synaptic operations, and the operation costs may also differ. For example, $1{,}000$ spikes reaching two connections each produce a different synaptic-operation count from $1{,}000$ spikes reaching twenty connections each.

The complete mental model begins with a binary event at each neuron and time step. Adding those events gives the total spike count. Following each event through the network gives the synaptic-operation count. Multiplying each count by its corresponding per-operation cost converts activity and computational work into energy contributions, and adding those contributions produces the estimated energy for one inference.
