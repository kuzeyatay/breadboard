## Spike-Timing-Dependent Plasticity

Once a network has produced spike trains, total spike count can help decode its final activity: an output neuron that fires more often during a time window may represent a stronger response. Training the network, however, requires a way to adjust the connections that created those spikes. Imagine an input neuron firing shortly before a connected output neuron. Their spikes occur close together in time, suggesting that the incoming event may have contributed to the output event. If the same timing relationship appears repeatedly, the connection between those neurons can be adjusted so that future input spikes have a different influence on the output neuron.

This timing-based learning process is called **Spike-Timing-Dependent Plasticity**, usually shortened to **STDP**. A network connection has a numerical strength called a **synaptic weight**. A larger weight gives an incoming spike more influence on the connected neuron; a smaller weight gives it less influence. STDP modifies this weight using the relative timing of two events: a spike from the sending neuron and a spike from the receiving neuron.

The sending neuron is called the **presynaptic neuron**, because its spike arrives before crossing the connection. The receiving neuron is called the **postsynaptic neuron**, because its activity occurs on the other side of that connection. Suppose the presynaptic spike occurs at time $t_{\text{pre}}$ and the postsynaptic spike occurs at time $t_{\text{post}}$. The quantity a timing rule needs is their separation:
$$
\Delta t = t_{\text{post}} - t_{\text{pre}}
$$
Here, $\Delta t$ is the relative spike timing, $t_{\text{pre}}$ is the time of the presynaptic spike, and $t_{\text{post}}$ is the time of the postsynaptic spike. This calculation tells the learning rule which neuron spiked earlier and how far apart the two spikes were. A positive value means the presynaptic event came earlier; a negative value means the postsynaptic event came earlier; a value near zero means the events occurred close together.

Consider a presynaptic spike at 12 milliseconds and a postsynaptic spike at 15 milliseconds. Their timing difference is
$$
\Delta t = 15\text{ ms} - 12\text{ ms} = 3\text{ ms}
$$
The positive result records that the incoming spike preceded the receiving neuron's spike by 3 milliseconds. If their times were reversed, with the postsynaptic spike at 12 milliseconds and the presynaptic spike at 15 milliseconds, then $\Delta t=-3$ milliseconds. The two cases contain the same pair of spike times separated by the same interval, yet their temporal order differs. STDP can therefore distinguish patterns that a simple event count would treat as identical.

A timing-dependent update rule maps $\Delta t$ to a connection change. The order of the spikes determines the direction assigned to the change, while their temporal separation can determine its magnitude. Closely spaced events generally represent a stronger temporal relationship than widely separated events within the rule's learning window. Repeated pairings accumulate their effects, gradually shaping which pathways exert greater influence over later network activity.

The interactive timing window makes this relationship easier to inspect. Moving either spike changes $\Delta t$, which changes the update associated with the connection. Crossing the point where both spikes have the same time reverses their order, so the update moves to the other side of the timing rule. Moving the spikes farther apart reduces their temporal closeness. The useful mental picture is a connection listening for a local sequence: "Which side fired first, and how much time passed before the other side fired?"

**Question.** Why does STDP need spike times when the network can already count spikes?

**Answer.** A count records how many events occurred during a chosen interval, while timing records how those events were arranged. Two neurons might each fire three times, giving equal counts, even though one pair fires in a tightly coordinated sequence and the other pair fires at widely separated moments. STDP uses the ordering and spacing of events to adjust the connection. Total spike count can later help decode the network's response, while relative timing provides information for changing the connections that produce that response.

Because every update can be computed from activity near a connection, STDP supports **unsupervised SNN learning**. Unsupervised learning discovers recurring structure in the input spike trains themselves. When certain input events repeatedly occur in a temporal relationship with a neuron's output spikes, the associated connections change according to the timing rule. Over many presentations, different neurons can become responsive to different recurring spike patterns.

Imagine a sensor producing one family of spike sequences whenever an object moves from left to right and another family whenever it moves from right to left. A neuron that repeatedly fires in close temporal relation to the first family will update the participating connections. Another neuron may develop a different pattern of connection strengths because its spikes align with the second family. The learning signal comes from the temporal relationships within the network's own activity. Each connection uses presynaptic timing, postsynaptic timing, and the resulting timing difference to determine its update.

"Unsupervised" can cause a common beginner confusion. The word may sound as though the network receives no organizing signal. Relative spike timing supplies that signal. What disappears is an externally supplied target for every update, such as a class label telling the network which output should win. The timing relationship itself governs the local connection change.

**Question.** Does a single closely timed spike pair teach the network a complete pattern?

**Answer.** A single pair produces one local update for one connection. A learned response emerges through many timing-dependent updates distributed across the network's connections. Repeated spike trains create repeated temporal relationships, allowing their effects to accumulate. The resulting pattern of weights influences which neurons fire, when they fire, and how many spikes appear in the eventual output window.

STDP therefore connects three scales of activity. Individual spikes provide precise event times. Pairs of presynaptic and postsynaptic events provide relative timing for local weight changes. Repeated local changes reshape whole spike trains, whose activity can eventually be decoded through quantities such as total output spike count. A useful mental model is a network whose connections remember temporal coordination: each connection watches the order and spacing of nearby spikes, adjusts its strength through repeated experience, and gradually helps the network organize recurring input patterns into distinguishable spiking responses.
