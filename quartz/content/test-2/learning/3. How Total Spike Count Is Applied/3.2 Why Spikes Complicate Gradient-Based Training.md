## Why Spikes Complicate Gradient-Based Training

Once a neuron has accumulated incoming signals, compared its membrane potential with a firing threshold, and emitted a spike, the network can use the resulting spikes to make a decision. Suppose the desired output is represented by how many times an output neuron fires during a short observation window. If the correct neuron produces too few spikes, its total spike count is too low; if another neuron produces too many, its count is too high. Training must somehow adjust earlier connections so that future membrane potentials cross their thresholds at more useful times and produce better final counts.

Gradient-based training usually performs this adjustment by tracing an error backward through the computations that produced it. A gradient measures how a small change in one quantity would change another quantity. If slightly increasing a connection weight would reduce the final error, the gradient points toward that increase. Repeating these small adjustments allows the network to improve.

This process works smoothly when each intermediate operation changes smoothly. Imagine a unit whose output rises gradually as its input rises. A tiny input change then produces a tiny output change, so the training procedure can estimate how strongly the input influenced the error. A hard spike event behaves differently because the firing threshold creates a sudden jump.

Consider a spiking neuron whose membrane potential is just below its threshold. Its output for that moment is silence. A small increase that still leaves the potential below threshold produces the same silent output. When the potential finally reaches the threshold, the neuron emits a discrete spike. The output therefore jumps directly from no spike to one spike rather than passing through intermediate values.

This jump makes spike generation **non-differentiable** at the firing threshold. Differentiability means that an operation has a well-defined local slope, allowing an arbitrarily small input change to be connected to a correspondingly small output change. For a hard spike, the output stays constant throughout the region below the threshold and throughout the region above it for that time step. Its slope is therefore zero in those regions. At the threshold itself, the abrupt jump has no ordinary finite slope.

Now follow the consequences backward from total spike count. The count is built by adding the spikes emitted across the observation window. If an output neuron emits three spikes, its total count is three. A training objective can compare that count with a desired count and calculate an error. The difficulty appears when the procedure asks how a small change in an earlier weight would alter the count.

A small weight change may slightly raise the membrane potential while leaving every threshold crossing unchanged. The neuron still emits three spikes, so the count remains three and the local change appears to be zero. A slightly larger change may push the membrane potential across the threshold at one moment, causing an additional spike and making the count jump from three to four. The relationship between the weight and the count is therefore organized around discrete threshold-crossing events.

Ordinary backpropagation carries gradients through a chain of intermediate operations. When that chain reaches hard spike generation, the zero slopes provide no useful adjustment signal across most membrane-potential values, while the undefined slope at the threshold prevents an ordinary derivative from being used there. Earlier connections then receive little or no information about how they should move the membrane potential toward a helpful threshold crossing. This is the central direct-training challenge in a spiking neural network.

**Question.** If the final spike-count error can be calculated, why can the network not simply send that error backward?

**Answer.** The error gives the size and direction of the mismatch at the output, such as a count that is too low. Sending it backward also requires local sensitivity information from every operation along the path. Hard spike generation does not provide a usable ordinary derivative: its output is constant away from the threshold and jumps at the threshold. The network can know that the final count should change while lacking a smooth signal that assigns responsibility to earlier weights.

A **surrogate derivative** supplies a practical bridge across this broken gradient path. During the forward computation, the neuron still uses its hard firing threshold and emits discrete spikes. During the backward computation, training substitutes a smooth, usable derivative around the threshold. This substitute gives the learning process a graded indication that membrane potentials near the threshold are especially sensitive to weight changes.

Imagine two membrane potentials. One lies far below the firing threshold, while the other lies just below it. Neither produces a spike during the forward pass. For learning purposes, however, the second potential is much closer to becoming a threshold crossing. A surrogate derivative can assign it a stronger training signal, allowing earlier weights to move it toward or away from firing according to the output error. The distant potential receives less emphasis because a tiny adjustment is less likely to change its spike event.

The surrogate derivative belongs to the training calculation rather than the neuron's emitted message. Forward activity remains event-based: the neuron either emits a spike or remains silent. Backward learning temporarily treats the threshold neighborhood as though it had a manageable slope. In this way, gradient-based adjustment can influence the timing and number of future spikes while preserving hard spike generation during network operation.

**Question.** Does using a surrogate derivative turn each spike into a continuous-valued output?

**Answer.** The spike used in the forward pass remains a discrete event. The continuous quantity appears only as a substitute derivative during the backward pass, where it estimates how changing the membrane potential could affect threshold crossing. The network still computes total spike count from actual emitted spikes.

A useful mental model is a light switch controlled by a gradually rising lever. The switch output changes abruptly when the lever reaches a fixed position, so the switch itself offers no smooth measure of progress. The surrogate derivative acts like a temporary sensitivity gauge near that switching position. It lets training connect a spike-count error to the membrane potentials and weights that shaped each threshold crossing. Hard thresholds create the events, total spike count gathers those events into an output, and surrogate derivatives provide the backward path needed to adjust how those events arise.
