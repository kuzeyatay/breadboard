Imagine two spiking models that classify the same inputs with nearly equal accuracy. One consumes 0.020 joules for each inference, while the other consumes 0.005 joules. Accuracy alone makes them appear similarly capable, and energy alone favors the model consuming fewer joules. Choosing between them requires a measurement that connects successful classification directly to its energy cost.

A ratio provides that connection. Dividing a measure of task success by the energy used to achieve it answers a practical question: how much classification performance is obtained from each joule? This quantity is called **normalized energy efficiency**, or **accuracy per joule**. It turns performance and cost into one measurable relationship while keeping their roles clear: accuracy describes model quality on the task, and energy consumption describes the physical cost of producing the result.

Let $A$ represent classification accuracy and $E$ represent energy consumption in joules. Normalized energy efficiency, written as $\eta_{\mathrm{NE}}$, is
$$
\eta_{\mathrm{NE}}=\frac{A}{E}
$$
Here:

- $\eta_{\mathrm{NE}}$ is the normalized energy efficiency.
- $A$ is the classification accuracy achieved by the model.
- $E$ is the energy consumed to achieve that accuracy, measured in joules.

The formula computes how much accuracy the model delivers per joule of consumed energy. It follows directly from the meaning of a ratio. Begin with the task success $A$, then distribute that success across the energy cost $E$. If the model achieves the same accuracy while consuming less energy, the denominator becomes smaller and the ratio becomes larger. If it consumes the same energy while achieving greater accuracy, the numerator becomes larger and the ratio also becomes larger. A higher value therefore represents a more favorable performance-cost relationship.

The interpretation depends on how accuracy is written. If accuracy is expressed as a percentage, such as $95\%$, the result is measured in percentage points per joule. If accuracy is expressed as a fraction, such as $0.95$, the result uses fractional accuracy per joule. Both conventions describe the same relationship, but every comparison must use one convention consistently.

Consider a model with an accuracy of $95\%$ that consumes $0.005$ joules per inference. Its normalized energy efficiency is
$$
\eta_{\mathrm{NE}}=\frac{95}{0.005}=19{,}000
$$
Percentage points per joule. Using fractional accuracy instead gives
$$
\eta_{\mathrm{NE}}=\frac{0.95}{0.005}=190\ \mathrm{J}^{-1}
$$
These values differ by a factor of 100 because $95\%$ and $0.95$ use different numerical scales. They describe the same model behavior. The reported value should therefore state whether accuracy was entered as a percentage or a fraction.

The ratio becomes especially useful when energy is initially reported in millijoules. A joule contains $1{,}000$ millijoules, so energy must be converted before substitution:
$$
5\ \mathrm{mJ}=\frac{5}{1000}\ \mathrm{J}=0.005\ \mathrm{J}
$$
That conversion prevents a common scale error. Dividing $95$ by $5$ would treat millijoules as though they were joules and produce a value one thousand times smaller than the accuracy-per-joule result.

**Question.** If two models have the same accuracy, which one has greater normalized energy efficiency?

**Answer.** The model that consumes less energy has greater normalized energy efficiency. Suppose both models achieve $96\%$ accuracy, while one uses $0.020$ joules and the other uses $0.010$ joules. Their efficiencies are $96/0.020=4{,}800$ and $96/0.010=9{,}600$ percentage points per joule. Halving the energy while preserving accuracy doubles the ratio.

Accuracy per joule also clarifies cases where neither model wins on both measurements. Suppose Model P achieves $98\%$ accuracy using $0.020$ joules, while Model Q achieves $95\%$ accuracy using $0.005$ joules. Their normalized efficiencies are
$$
\eta_P=\frac{98}{0.020}=4{,}900
$$
And
$$
\eta_Q=\frac{95}{0.005}=19{,}000
$$
Percentage points per joule. Model P has the greater raw accuracy, while Model Q obtains much more accuracy from each joule. The ratio makes this behavior measurable without erasing the original measurements. A careful comparison still reports $A$ and $E$ beside $\eta_{\mathrm{NE}}$, because two different pairs of accuracy and energy can produce similar ratios.

**Question.** Does the model with the highest accuracy per joule always have the highest classification accuracy?

**Answer.** No. Accuracy per joule increases through either greater accuracy or lower energy consumption. A model can therefore have slightly lower classification accuracy and still achieve a larger ratio by using substantially less energy. The ratio measures the relationship between quality and cost; raw accuracy continues to measure task success directly.

Normalized energy efficiency can be pictured as useful classification performance spread across an energy budget. Accuracy supplies the useful outcome, joules supply the cost, and division tells how densely that outcome is packed into the available energy. Read together, the three values form a practical mental model: accuracy shows how well the model performs, energy shows what each inference costs, and accuracy per joule shows how effectively the model converts that cost into task performance.
