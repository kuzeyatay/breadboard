## Convergence Time and Learning Curves

Energy cost per spike tells us how expensive neural activity is, but evaluating a training strategy also requires knowing how long the model must learn before its predictions become useful. Imagine training three spiking neural networks for 20 epochs, where one **epoch** means one complete pass through the training data. After every epoch, we record two values: the model's training loss and its training accuracy. These measurements turn training into a visible process rather than a single final score.

Training loss measures the size of the model's prediction error according to the objective used for learning. Smaller loss generally indicates that the model's internal parameters are producing predictions that fit the training examples more closely. If the loss at epoch 3 is larger than the loss at epoch 10, the learning process has reduced the measured error over those seven additional passes through the data.

A **loss curve** places the epoch number on the horizontal axis and training loss on the vertical axis. Its direction shows whether optimization is progressing, while its slope shows how rapidly the measured error is changing. A steep downward segment represents rapid loss reduction. A shallow downward segment represents slower improvement. When the curve becomes nearly flat, additional epochs produce only small changes in loss, so the model is approaching a stable region of its training process.

The three curves below show how the converted, surrogate-gradient, and STDP-based SNN strategies reduce training loss across the same 20-epoch window.

All three curves should be read from left to right as records of the models' changing error. The surrogate-gradient curve descends more rapidly and stabilizes by the twentieth epoch, indicating that its learning rule reduces the training objective more quickly within the observed interval. The converted SNN also reduces its loss, but its trajectory reflects a different rate of progress. The STDP-based SNN decreases loss more gradually and levels off at a higher loss, showing slower convergence under this measurement. The comparison therefore depends on both direction and rate: downward movement indicates improvement, while the amount of loss removed per epoch indicates how quickly that improvement occurs.

A graph makes the overall shape easy to see, while a table lets us verify the plotted trend one epoch at a time.

![Training loss across 20 epochs for converted, surrogate-gradient, and STDP-based SNN models](/test-2/assets/source-visuals/2510-27379v1-page-10-table-t1-training-loss-across-20-epochs-for-converted-su.png)

Each row pairs an epoch with three exact loss values: one for the converted SNN, one for the surrogate-gradient SNN, and one for the STDP-based SNN. Comparing adjacent rows reveals how much loss each strategy removes during a single epoch. If a model's loss changes from $L_{e-1}$ at the previous epoch to $L_e$ at the current epoch, its one-epoch loss reduction is
$$
\Delta L_e = L_{e-1}-L_e
$$
Here, $e$ is the current epoch, $L_{e-1}$ is the loss recorded one epoch earlier, $L_e$ is the current loss, and $\Delta L_e$ is the amount by which loss decreased. A large positive $\Delta L_e$ corresponds to a steep downward portion of the graph. A small positive value corresponds to a flatter portion. Reading the table across the three model columns verifies the visual pattern: the surrogate-gradient strategy reduces loss more rapidly, the converted strategy follows its own intermediate trajectory, and the STDP-based strategy improves more gradually and retains a higher loss near the end of the reported interval.

Loss describes the optimization objective, while **training accuracy** describes the fraction or percentage of training examples classified correctly. An **accuracy curve** places epoch on the horizontal axis and accuracy on the vertical axis. Its upward movement has an immediate task-level interpretation: the model is producing correct predictions on a larger share of the training set.

![Learning curves comparing training accuracy across 20 epochs for converted, surrogate-gradient, and STDP-based SNNs](/test-2/assets/source-visuals/2510-27379v1-page-11-graph-g1-learning-curves-comparing-training-accuracy-acro.png)

*Learning curves comparing training accuracy across 20 epochs for converted, surrogate-gradient, and STDP-based SNNs* *(p. 11)*

Across the 20 epochs, the rising curves show that training accuracy improves as learning proceeds. Their shapes also reveal when improvement occurs. A curve that rises sharply during early epochs gains useful performance quickly. A curve that rises gradually requires more passes through the data to reach the same accuracy level. The surrogate-gradient SNN's faster improvement is consistent with its more rapid loss reduction, while the slower STDP-based trajectory aligns with its more gradual optimization. Reading loss and accuracy together gives a fuller picture: loss reveals how the learning objective changes, and accuracy reveals how those changes translate into correct classifications.

To measure "fast enough," we choose a **target accuracy**, written as $A_{\text{target}}$. This is a performance threshold selected before comparing the curves. For example, a project might require 90% training accuracy. The relevant question then becomes: at which epoch does each model first reach at least 90%?

Let $A(e)$ represent the training accuracy measured after epoch $e$. The **convergence time** is
$$
T_{\mathrm{conv}}=\min\{e \mid A(e)\geq A_{\mathrm{target}}\}
$$
In this expression, $e$ is an epoch number, $A(e)$ is the accuracy recorded at that epoch, $A_{\mathrm{target}}$ is the chosen target accuracy, and $T_{\mathrm{conv}}$ is the earliest epoch whose accuracy reaches or exceeds the target. The word "minimum" selects the first qualifying epoch. If a curve crosses the target at epoch 8 and remains above it through epoch 20, its measured convergence time is 8 epochs.

This definition separates final performance from speed. Two models can finish the twentieth epoch at similarly high accuracies while having different convergence times. If one first reaches the target at epoch 7 and another reaches it at epoch 13, the first model requires six fewer passes through the training data to satisfy that particular requirement. Raising the target can move both crossing points later, and a sufficiently high target may remain unreached within the 20 observed epochs. Convergence time therefore always belongs with a stated target and observation window.

**Question.** Why use the first epoch that reaches the target rather than the final epoch?

**Answer.** The first crossing measures how soon the required performance becomes available. Using the final epoch would assign every completed 20-epoch run the same time, hiding whether a model became useful early or only near the end. The earliest crossing preserves the timing information carried by the accuracy curve.

**Question.** Can the model with the lowest loss automatically be declared the fastest to converge?

**Answer.** Convergence time is computed from the accuracy threshold, so it must be read from the accuracy curve. Loss can continue decreasing even while accuracy changes very little because the model may become more confident without changing which examples it classifies correctly. Loss and accuracy therefore provide complementary signals: the loss curve explains optimization progress, and the accuracy curve determines when a chosen performance target is first reached.

A useful mental model is to imagine training as a journey observed through two instruments. Loss acts like a distance-to-fit gauge, showing how strongly the measured error is shrinking at each epoch. Accuracy acts like a checkpoint map, showing when the model crosses a required level of correct performance. The target accuracy places the checkpoint, and the earliest crossing supplies the convergence time. Together, the curves turn "this model learns quickly" into a measurable statement tied to a specific threshold, a specific epoch, and the observed 20-epoch training window.
