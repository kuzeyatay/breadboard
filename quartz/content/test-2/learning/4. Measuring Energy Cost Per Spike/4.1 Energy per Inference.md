Imagine that a spiking network has just completed one inference, such as classifying a short stream of sensor data. Earlier, total spike count gave us a measure of how active the network was: every time a neuron crossed its threshold, it emitted a discrete event. Activity alone, however, does not tell us how much energy the inference consumed. To turn event counts into an energy estimate, each kind of computational event needs a cost. The evaluation therefore connects the network's observable activity to energy through two quantities: the energy associated with producing or processing spikes and the energy associated with the synaptic operations triggered as those spikes travel through the network.

An **energy cost per spike** assigns an energy value to one spike event. If one spike has an assumed cost of $e_{\text{spike}}$, then two spikes cost twice that amount, three spikes cost three times that amount, and so on. For $N_{\text{spikes}}$ spikes, the estimated spike-event energy is
$$
E_{\text{spikes}} = N_{\text{spikes}} e_{\text{spike}}
$$
Here, $E_{\text{spikes}}$ is the energy attributed to spike events during one inference, $N_{\text{spikes}}$ is the total number of spikes generated during that inference, and $e_{\text{spike}}$ is the energy cost assigned to one spike. This multiplication converts a count into an energy quantity. For example, if an inference generates $4{,}000$ spikes and each spike is assigned a cost of $0.5$ microjoules, then
$$
E_{\text{spikes}} = 4{,}000 \times 0.5\ \mu\text{J} = 2{,}000\ \mu\text{J} = 2\ \text{mJ}
$$
The number $2\ \text{mJ}$ is the estimated energy contribution of the spike events under the chosen per-spike cost. Energy cost per spike makes spiking behavior measurable in a common unit: a network with fewer spikes will usually receive a smaller spike-energy estimate when the same per-spike cost is used.

A spike can also trigger work at the connections between neurons. Each connection is a **synapse**, and processing a spike through one such connection is a **synaptic operation**. A single spike may reach several downstream neurons, so the number of synaptic operations can be larger than the number of spikes. If one neuron sends a spike to five connected neurons, that spike can produce five synaptic operations. Spike count measures how many events were emitted, while synaptic-operation count measures how much connection-level processing those events caused.

The energy attributed to this connection-level work follows the same count-times-cost pattern:
$$
E_{\text{syn}} = N_{\text{syn}} e_{\text{syn}}
$$
In this expression, $E_{\text{syn}}$ is the energy attributed to synaptic operations, $N_{\text{syn}}$ is the total number of synaptic operations performed during the inference, and $e_{\text{syn}}$ is the energy cost assigned to one synaptic operation. The formula computes the estimated energy required to process all spike-driven interactions between connected neurons.

Suppose the same inference performs $20{,}000$ synaptic operations, with an assigned cost of $0.1$ microjoules per operation. Its synaptic-operation energy is
$$
E_{\text{syn}} = 20{,}000 \times 0.1\ \mu\text{J}
= 2{,}000\ \mu\text{J}
= 2\ \text{mJ}
$$
The spike events and the resulting synaptic operations represent two contributions to the inference cost. Adding them gives the total estimated energy:
$$
E_{\text{total}}
=
N_{\text{spikes}} e_{\text{spike}}
+
N_{\text{syn}} e_{\text{syn}}
$$
Here, $E_{\text{total}}$ is the total estimated energy consumed by one inference; $N_{\text{spikes}}$ is the number of spike events; $e_{\text{spike}}$ is the energy cost per spike; $N_{\text{syn}}$ is the number of synaptic operations; and $e_{\text{syn}}$ is the energy cost per synaptic operation. The expression comes directly from computing each contribution separately and adding the results:
$$
E_{\text{total}} = E_{\text{spikes}} + E_{\text{syn}}
$$
Using the example values, the spike contribution is $2\ \text{mJ}$ and the synaptic contribution is also $2\ \text{mJ}$. The estimated total is therefore
$$
E_{\text{total}} = 2\ \text{mJ} + 2\ \text{mJ} = 4\ \text{mJ}
$$
This estimate behaves like an itemized bill. One line charges for emitted events, and another charges for the connection-level work those events activate. Counting only spikes would miss the fact that spikes can have different numbers of destinations. Counting only synaptic operations would omit the assigned cost of generating or handling the spike events themselves. Combining both terms connects network activity to the two modeled forms of event-driven computation.

**Question.** If two networks produce the same number of spikes, must they have the same estimated energy?

**Answer.** Their spike-event contributions are equal when the same energy cost per spike is used, but their total estimates can differ. A spike that reaches many connected neurons causes more synaptic operations than a spike that reaches only a few. If Network A and Network B each generate $5{,}000$ spikes, yet Network A performs $15{,}000$ synaptic operations while Network B performs $30{,}000$, Network B receives the larger synaptic-energy contribution. Total spike count therefore captures event activity, while synaptic-operation count captures how widely that activity propagates through the connections.

Sparse event activity can reduce both parts of the estimate. When fewer neurons fire, $N_{\text{spikes}}$ decreases directly. Fewer emitted spikes can also lead to fewer synaptic operations, causing $N_{\text{syn}}$ to decrease. Because both counts are multiplied by their respective per-event costs, reductions in either count lower the corresponding energy term. Silence has computational value in this model: when no spike occurs, there is no spike event to charge and no spike-triggered synaptic work along its outgoing connections.

**Question.** Does a low spike count guarantee a low total energy estimate?

**Answer.** A low spike count supports a smaller spike-energy term, but the synaptic term still depends on how many operations each spike triggers. A small number of spikes spreading across many connections can produce substantial synaptic activity. The full estimate therefore requires both counts and both per-event costs. Sparsity is most effective when it reduces emitted spikes and the connection-level operations that follow them.

The complete mental model is a chain from behavior to computation to energy. Neurons emit a countable set of spikes; those spikes activate a countable set of synaptic operations; each kind of event receives a per-event energy cost; multiplication converts each count into an energy contribution; and addition combines the contributions into total estimated energy per inference. This chain makes energy analysis concrete: the network's temporal activity becomes measurable through the work its events create.
