### Spike-Timing Dependent Plasticity

A spiking network has already learned to treat time as part of the message: a neuron sends a spike when its internal state crosses a threshold, and the downstream neuron receives that event through a synaptic connection. Now imagine a simple pathway with two neurons. The upstream neuron fires, its spike travels across the synapse, and a moment later the downstream neuron fires too. The network has seen a tiny cause-and-effect pattern: activity on one side of the connection came just before activity on the other side. Spike-Timing Dependent Plasticity, or STDP, turns that timing relationship into a learning signal.

A synapse in an SNN has a weight, which controls how strongly a pre-synaptic spike influences a post-synaptic neuron. "Pre-synaptic" means the neuron sending the spike into the connection; "post-synaptic" means the neuron receiving that spike. If the weight is larger, the incoming spike has a stronger effect on the receiving neuron's membrane potential. If the weight is smaller, the same incoming spike has a weaker effect. Learning means changing these weights so useful connections become stronger and less useful connections become weaker.

STDP gives the synapse a local rule for changing its own weight. "Local" means the rule can be evaluated from information available at that connection: when the pre-synaptic neuron fired, when the post-synaptic neuron fired, and the current strength of the connection. The synapse does not need a global error signal sent backward through the whole network. It can update itself from the relative timing of the two spikes.

The timing relationship can be written with one small formula:
$$
\Delta t = t_{\text{post}} - t_{\text{pre}}
$$
Here, $\Delta t$ is the timing difference, $t_{\text{post}}$ is the time when the post-synaptic neuron fires, and $t_{\text{pre}}$ is the time when the pre-synaptic neuron fires. This formula lets the synapse compute which spike came earlier and how far apart the two spikes were. If $\Delta t$ is positive, the pre-synaptic spike happened before the post-synaptic spike. If $\Delta t$ is negative, the post-synaptic spike happened before the pre-synaptic spike.

A positive $\Delta t$ is treated as evidence that the pre-synaptic spike helped push the post-synaptic neuron toward firing. The connection becomes stronger. This strengthening is called potentiation. A negative $\Delta t$ is treated as evidence that the pre-synaptic spike arrived after the post-synaptic neuron had already fired. The connection becomes weaker. This weakening is called depression. The learning signal is therefore the order and spacing of events, rather than a separately computed target error.

For a concrete example, suppose neuron A fires at 12 milliseconds and neuron B fires at 15 milliseconds. If A connects to B, then
$$
\Delta t = 15\text{ ms} - 12\text{ ms} = 3\text{ ms}
$$
The positive value says that A fired shortly before B. STDP strengthens the A-to-B synapse, making future spikes from A more capable of helping B fire. Now suppose B fires at 15 milliseconds and A fires at 19 milliseconds. Then
$$
\Delta t = 15\text{ ms} - 19\text{ ms} = -4\text{ ms}
$$
The negative value says that A fired after B. The A-to-B connection weakens, since A's spike arrived too late to explain B's firing.

The size of the timing gap also shapes the weight change. Spikes that occur close together carry a stronger timing relationship than spikes separated by a long delay. If a pre-synaptic spike happens immediately before a post-synaptic spike, the synapse receives a strong signal to strengthen. If the pre-synaptic spike happens much earlier, the connection still has the right order, but the evidence is weaker. In the same way, a spike that arrives just after the post-synaptic firing gives a stronger weakening signal than one that arrives far afterward. STDP therefore behaves like a short memory window around each spike: events close in time are linked strongly, while distant events have smaller influence.

This rule fits naturally with spike-based communication. A dense artificial network often updates many continuous values at every layer. A spiking network can stay quiet until events occur. STDP goes one step further: learning also happens around events. A synapse waits for spikes, compares their timing, and adjusts only when the timing relationship gives useful evidence. Silence consumes little activity, and learning concentrates around the moments when the network actually communicates.

That event-centered style helps explain why STDP is associated with ultra-low-power unsupervised learning. "Unsupervised" means the network can adapt from patterns in its input activity rather than from a labeled answer attached to every example. If certain input neurons repeatedly fire just before a downstream neuron fires, their synapses become stronger. Over time, the downstream neuron becomes more responsive to that recurring pattern. The network has learned a structure in the data through timing regularities.

A simple analogy is a group of people clapping. If one person consistently claps just before the group follows, that person's timing looks predictive of the group's next clap. STDP strengthens the connection from that early clap to the group response. If someone claps after the group has already clapped, that timing gives little reason to treat them as a cause of the group response, so the connection weakens. The synapse is learning from temporal order: who tends to lead, who tends to follow, and how tightly the events are linked.

**Question.** If STDP strengthens a synapse when the pre-synaptic neuron fires before the post-synaptic neuron, does that mean every early spike is useful?

**Answer.** The timing gap matters. A pre-synaptic spike that happens shortly before a post-synaptic spike provides a strong timing signal because it could have contributed to the post-synaptic firing. A spike that happens far earlier gives a much weaker signal because the connection between the two events is less direct. STDP uses relative timing, so both order and closeness shape the weight change.

STDP also creates a clear tradeoff. Because it relies on local spike timing instead of a global backpropagated error signal, it can reduce training cost and spiking activity. Reported comparisons place STDP-based SNNs at very low energy consumption, around 5 millijoules per inference, with sparse activity around 4,000 spikes. That sparsity comes from the same event-driven logic: fewer spikes mean fewer synaptic events to process. The cost is slower convergence and slightly reduced accuracy compared with training approaches that more directly optimize a performance objective. STDP is therefore especially natural when power is tightly constrained and the network can learn from repeated temporal patterns.

A beginner confusion often comes from thinking of STDP as a reward system. The STDP rule does not need to be told that the whole network made a correct or incorrect decision. The synapse only compares two spike times across one connection. If the timing suggests that the pre-synaptic spike helped produce the post-synaptic spike, the weight increases. If the timing suggests the opposite order, the weight decreases. A larger system can combine many such local adjustments, so global behavior emerges from many small timing-based updates.

**Question.** How can a network learn anything useful if each synapse only sees local spike times?

**Answer.** Repeated patterns create repeated timing relationships. If a set of input spikes often arrives just before a neuron fires, STDP strengthens those incoming connections together. The neuron gradually becomes tuned to that pattern. Each synapse only uses local information, but many local changes can shape the network's response to recurring structure in the input.

The mental model is simple: STDP treats a synapse as a tiny timing judge. Each time spikes occur on both sides of the connection, the synapse asks which event came first and how close the two events were. Pre-before-post timing strengthens the pathway; post-before-pre timing weakens it; close timing produces a stronger update than distant timing. Learning becomes a process of wiring together spike patterns that repeatedly lead into later spikes. That is how spike timing itself becomes a learning signal.
