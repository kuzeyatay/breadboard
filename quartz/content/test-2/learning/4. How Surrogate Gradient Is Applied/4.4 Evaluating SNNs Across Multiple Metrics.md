## Evaluating SNNs Across Multiple Metrics

Imagine choosing a spiking neural network for a small edge device that must recognize an event and respond within a few milliseconds. A model with high classification accuracy may still respond too slowly, consume too much energy, or generate so many spikes that its event-driven advantage shrinks. Choosing a training strategy therefore requires several measurements viewed together: classification accuracy, inference latency, energy consumption, spike count, and convergence behavior.

Classification accuracy measures how often a model assigns the correct class to an input. If an SNN correctly classifies 978 out of 1,000 samples, its accuracy is 97.8%. That number tells us how reliably the model performs its prediction task, which makes accuracy an essential starting point. Yet the number says nothing about how long each prediction takes or how much computation supports it. Two models can reach similar accuracy while placing very different demands on time and energy.

Inference latency fills in the time dimension. It measures the delay between presenting an input and obtaining the model's prediction, usually expressed in milliseconds per sample. Surrogate-gradient SNNs can combine competitive accuracy with latency around 10 ms. Converted SNNs may require simulation windows around 20 ms because their spike rates need time to approximate the continuous activations of the original artificial neural network. A longer window gives the converted network more spike events from which to estimate those activation values, but it also postpones the decision.

This timing difference becomes concrete in a responsive system. Suppose an input arrives at time $t=0$. A surrogate-gradient SNN operating at roughly 10 ms latency can produce a decision near $t=10$ ms, while a converted SNN operating at roughly 20 ms produces one near $t=20$ ms. Both delays may appear short in isolation, but repeated predictions or rapidly changing inputs make the accumulated difference significant. A training strategy that directly learns useful temporal spiking patterns can reach a reliable decision with a shorter observation window.

Energy consumption adds the resource dimension. It measures the energy used for each inference and can be expressed in millijoules per prediction. In the reported comparison, a surrogate-gradient SNN uses about 15 mJ per inference, while an STDP-based SNN uses about 5 mJ. The lower-energy STDP result accompanies an accuracy of about 95.5%, whereas the surrogate-gradient model reaches about 97.8% accuracy. The choice between them depends on the cost of an error relative to the cost of energy. A battery-constrained device may value the 5 mJ operating point, while an application requiring stronger classification performance may favor the surrogate-gradient model's balance of accuracy and efficiency.

Spike count helps explain part of the energy difference. A spike count records how many discrete events the network generates while processing one input. Converted SNNs produce roughly 20,000 spikes per inference in the comparison because rate-coded propagation uses many events to represent activation magnitude. Surrogate-gradient SNNs produce roughly 12,000 spikes, while STDP-based SNNs produce roughly 4,000. Sparse activity generally reduces the amount of event processing required, which aligns with the observed energy values of approximately 20 mJ for converted SNNs, 15 mJ for surrogate-gradient SNNs, and 5 mJ for STDP-based SNNs.

**Question.** If the surrogate-gradient SNN has higher accuracy than the STDP-based SNN, why might anyone choose STDP?

**Answer.** The STDP-based SNN occupies a different point in the tradeoff. Its accuracy is lower in the reported comparison, but it produces about one-third as many spikes as the surrogate-gradient SNN and consumes about one-third as much energy per inference. An energy-constrained application can benefit more from this sparse, low-power behavior than from the additional classification accuracy. Model quality therefore depends on the requirements of the system using it.

Convergence behavior brings training time into the evaluation. Convergence describes how a model's performance develops over successive training epochs, where one epoch represents one complete pass through the training data. A model that approaches its final performance by the twentieth epoch reaches a useful solution more quickly than one whose accuracy continues to improve gradually beyond that point. Surrogate-gradient training shows faster convergence by around epoch 20, while STDP-based training converges more slowly. Looking only at final accuracy hides this path: two strategies may eventually reach useful performance while requiring different amounts of training and showing different levels of stability along the way.

Convergence curves also help distinguish a model that has settled from one that happens to report a strong value at a particular epoch. If accuracy rises rapidly and then levels off, additional epochs provide diminishing gains. If it continues to fluctuate or climb slowly, the current result may depend strongly on when training stops. This makes convergence behavior part of a fair comparison rather than a background detail of the training process.

**Question.** Can the five metrics be combined into one universal score?

**Answer.** A combined score requires weights that express how much the application values accuracy, latency, energy, spike count, and convergence. Those weights change with the task. A low-latency recognition system may emphasize accuracy and response time, while an energy-limited sensor may emphasize millijoules per inference and spike count. Keeping the measurements visible preserves the tradeoff that a single score can conceal.

Multi-metric evaluation turns model selection into matching a performance profile to an application. Surrogate-gradient SNNs offer a balanced profile in the reported results: about 97.8% accuracy, roughly 10 ms latency, approximately 15 mJ per inference, moderate spike activity, and relatively fast convergence. STDP-based SNNs shift the profile toward sparse activity and very low energy, accompanied by somewhat lower accuracy and slower convergence. Converted SNNs retain competitive predictive performance while using longer simulation windows and producing more spikes.

A useful mental model is to picture every training strategy as a point in a space with several axes. Accuracy measures correctness, latency measures response time, energy measures operating cost, spike count reveals event activity, and convergence traces the effort required to learn. Moving toward a better value on one axis can change the position on another. The strongest choice is the point whose complete shape fits the system's needs.
