## Choosing an SNN Training Strategy

You have already seen that accuracy, latency, energy, spike activity, and convergence describe different parts of an SNN's behavior. Choosing a training strategy requires bringing those measurements together. Imagine that three trained models are available for the same task. One reaches the highest accuracy, another responds fastest, and another consumes the least energy. A ranking based only on accuracy would favor the first model, while a battery-powered system with a strict energy budget might be unable to use it. Model selection therefore begins with the conditions the model must satisfy.

A **deployment constraint** is a limit or requirement imposed by the setting in which a model will operate. A response-time constraint might require an inference to finish within a certain number of milliseconds. An energy constraint might cap the millijoules available for each inference. An accuracy constraint might specify the minimum acceptable proportion of correct predictions. Spike count can also act as a constraint because every generated spike contributes to activity that must be processed. Convergence behavior adds a training-side consideration: a strategy that improves slowly may require more training time before reaching useful performance.

These constraints turn model selection into a filtering process. Begin by writing each requirement as a threshold or priority. A model either meets a hard threshold or falls outside the feasible set, which is the group of candidates that satisfy every mandatory condition. Preferences can then distinguish among the feasible candidates. For example, suppose a system requires accuracy of at least 97% and latency of at most 12 ms. A strategy reaching 95.5% accuracy would leave the feasible set even if it had excellent energy efficiency. Among the remaining models, energy or spike count could determine the preferred choice.

The comparison becomes a **multi-objective tradeoff** because improving one measurement can accompany a less favorable value on another. The evaluated surrogate-gradient SNN illustrates a balanced point: it reaches about 97.8% accuracy, responds in about 10 ms, consumes roughly 15 mJ per inference, and generates around 12,000 spikes. These values place it close to ANN-level accuracy while retaining the temporal and energy advantages of spike-based computation. Its convergence by around the twentieth epoch also indicates that useful performance develops relatively quickly under the evaluated training conditions.

Consider how those measurements work together. The 10 ms latency is valuable when the response deadline is tight. The 97.8% accuracy supports a comparatively demanding accuracy threshold. Energy use of 15 mJ is far below the approximately 200 mJ reported for the evaluated ANN/CNN, while its spike count is lower than the converted SNN's roughly 20,000 spikes. The surrogate-gradient strategy therefore occupies a useful middle region: strong accuracy and low latency accompany moderate energy consumption and moderate spike activity.

An STDP-based SNN occupies a different region. It consumes about 5 mJ per inference and produces roughly 4,000 spikes, the lowest values among the evaluated models. Its accuracy is about 95.5%, and its convergence is slower. A deployment with a strict 6 mJ energy ceiling would admit the STDP model and reject models requiring 15 or 20 mJ. Within that feasible set, the lower spike count strengthens the same selection because sparse activity aligns with the energy constraint. If the minimum acceptable accuracy were raised above 95.5%, the feasible set would change, and the preferred strategy would change with it.

**Question.** Why can't we simply combine all five measurements into one overall ranking?

**Answer.** A single ranking quietly assumes that every deployment values the measurements in the same way. One setting may treat a 10 ms response limit as mandatory, while another may tolerate a longer simulation window in exchange for lower energy. A third may require the highest available accuracy even when spike activity rises. Constraint-driven selection keeps these priorities visible: mandatory thresholds remove unsuitable candidates, and remaining preferences guide the choice among candidates that can actually operate within the intended conditions.

ANN-to-SNN conversion provides another tradeoff pattern. Converted SNNs preserve competitive accuracy, but the evaluated conversion approach requires a longer simulation window and produces around 20,000 spikes. Its energy use is approximately 20 mJ per inference. A longer window can increase latency because the model needs more time steps to represent and accumulate rate-coded spike activity. The high spike count also means more events must be processed. Conversion can remain competitive when preserving accuracy carries high priority and the deployment can accommodate its timing and activity demands.

These comparisons show why each metric should be interpreted as part of a chain rather than as an isolated score. A training strategy shapes how the model learns. That learning behavior affects convergence, meaning how quickly performance stabilizes during training. The learned spike patterns influence the number of events generated during inference. Spike activity contributes to the processing work associated with an inference, which is reflected in energy consumption. The time window and event dynamics contribute to latency. Accuracy then tells whether the resulting behavior solves the task well enough. Selection connects the entire chain to the limits of the intended deployment.

Hard constraints should be applied before softer preferences. Suppose a candidate set contains the evaluated surrogate-gradient, converted, and STDP-based SNNs. A deployment requires at least 95% accuracy, permits at most 20 mJ per inference, and strongly prefers low spike activity. All three may satisfy the stated accuracy and energy ceilings at their reported values, although a value exactly at the energy limit leaves little margin. Spike count then favors STDP with roughly 4,000 spikes, followed by the surrogate-gradient model with roughly 12,000 and the converted model with roughly 20,000. If the deployment also imposes a tight latency requirement near 10 ms, the surrogate-gradient strategy becomes more attractive because it combines that latency with about 97.8% accuracy.

A useful concept for comparing feasible candidates is **dominance**. One model dominates another when it performs at least as well on every relevant objective and better on at least one. Candidates that remain after such comparisons form a tradeoff boundary: moving from one candidate to another gains something while giving up something else. The reported surrogate-gradient and STDP results illustrate this boundary. Surrogate training offers higher accuracy, lower latency, and faster convergence, while STDP offers lower energy and fewer spikes. Each becomes preferable under a different constraint profile.

**Question.** How should convergence influence a choice if inference performance is the main concern?

**Answer.** Convergence determines how readily the training process reaches the performance used in the deployment comparison. Faster convergence can reduce the time needed to obtain a useful model and makes the observed accuracy available earlier in training. Slower convergence may still be acceptable when the resulting model satisfies a stronger operational constraint, such as very low energy or sparse spike activity. Treat convergence as a training constraint alongside the inference constraints rather than allowing it to replace them.

The practical mental model is a sequence of gates. Accuracy establishes whether the model performs the task adequately. Latency establishes whether it responds within the available time. Energy establishes whether each inference fits the power budget. Spike count reveals the level of event activity associated with that operation. Convergence establishes whether training reaches the desired behavior within acceptable effort. The strategy that passes every required gate enters the feasible set, and the deployment's remaining priorities select among those survivors. Under this view, surrogate-gradient training, conversion, and STDP each represent a different balance, and the preferred balance emerges from explicit constraints rather than from a single global ranking.
