## Energy Use and Spike Activity Across Models

Once total spike count and estimated energy consumption are understood separately, they can be read together to answer a practical question: how much activity does each model generate during one inference, and how much energy is associated with that computation? Imagine four models processing one input. Each reaches an output, but each follows a different computational path. An artificial neural network, or ANN, repeatedly processes continuous-valued activations. A spiking neural network, or SNN, communicates through discrete spike events distributed over time. Counting those events gives a measure of **spike activity**, while energy per inference estimates the energy required to process one input from start to finish.

The comparison includes four models: an ANN convolutional neural network, a converted SNN, a surrogate-gradient SNN, and an STDP-based SNN. Their training strategies shape how activity moves through the network. A converted SNN begins from a trained ANN and represents its activations through spike rates. A surrogate-gradient SNN learns spiking patterns directly with a gradient-based training method. An STDP-based SNN adapts through spike-timing-dependent plasticity, where learning depends on the relative timing of spikes. These approaches produce different amounts of spike activity, and the reported energy estimates fall alongside those differences.

The table places the model name, energy per inference, and spike count in the same row so that each model can be read as a paired result rather than as two isolated measurements.

Begin with the ANN row. Its energy use is approximately 200 millijoules per inference. A millijoule, abbreviated mJ, is one thousandth of a joule, so this value describes the estimated energy cost of processing a single input. The ANN has no SNN-style spike count because its activity is represented by continuous-valued operations rather than discrete spike events. Its 200 mJ result therefore provides the high-energy reference point for comparing the three spiking approaches.

The converted SNN lowers energy per inference to about 20 mJ. That is one tenth of the ANN value, corresponding to an energy reduction of about 90%. It generates approximately 20,000 spikes per inference, the largest spike count among the evaluated SNNs. Rate coding helps explain this dense activity: activation strength is represented through the frequency of spikes, so many events may be needed to carry the values inherited from the original ANN. Conversion therefore produces a substantial energy reduction while retaining relatively high spike traffic.

The surrogate-gradient SNN reaches approximately 15 mJ per inference and generates about 12,000 spikes. Compared with the converted SNN, it uses 5 mJ less energy and produces 8,000 fewer spikes. The spike reduction is 40% because 8,000 is 40% of the converted model's 20,000 spikes. Direct training allows the model to learn spiking patterns suited to the task instead of reproducing ANN activations through a large number of rate-coded events. In this comparison, that learned activity pattern sits between the converted and STDP-based models in both energy use and spike count.

The STDP-based SNN has the sparsest activity: approximately 4,000 spikes per inference. Its estimated energy use is also the lowest, at about 5 mJ per inference. Relative to the converted SNN, it produces 16,000 fewer spikes, an 80% reduction, while using one quarter as much energy. Relative to the ANN's 200 mJ reference, 5 mJ represents roughly a 97% reduction. This model therefore occupies the low-activity, low-energy end of the comparison, though its energy advantage accompanies a reported slight reduction in accuracy.

Across the three SNNs, the bars or plotted values move in the same broad direction: converted SNN activity is highest at about 20,000 spikes with 20 mJ of energy, surrogate-gradient activity falls to about 12,000 spikes with 15 mJ, and STDP activity falls further to about 4,000 spikes with 5 mJ. Fewer spike events mean fewer event-driven updates must be handled during inference, so spike count provides a useful indicator of computational activity. The graph's descending pattern shows an association between sparser activity and lower reported energy among these models.

Treat spike count as one contributor to the energy comparison. Each spike can trigger computation, yet the total energy estimate also reflects the kinds of operations used to process activity and the way information is represented. The ANN makes this broader picture visible: it has no spike count to compare with the SNN values, yet it has the largest energy estimate because it relies on continuous-valued computation. Even within the SNN group, energy does not decrease in a perfectly fixed amount per spike. Moving from 20,000 to 12,000 spikes removes 8,000 spikes while energy falls by 5 mJ; moving from 12,000 to 4,000 removes the same number of spikes while energy falls by 10 mJ. The paired results support a relationship between sparse activity and low energy, while the unequal changes show that a spike count by itself does not fully calculate energy per inference.

**Question.** Why does the converted SNN use much less energy than the ANN while still generating about 20,000 spikes?

**Answer.** The two models represent and process activity differently. The ANN uses continuous-valued operations throughout inference, producing an estimate of about 200 mJ. The converted SNN communicates through spike events and reaches about 20 mJ, even though its rate-coded representation generates many spikes. Its high spike count should therefore be compared with other SNN spike counts, while its energy should also be interpreted in light of its event-based computation.

**Question.** Does the STDP-based SNN's low spike count prove that every model with 4,000 spikes will consume 5 mJ?

**Answer.** The values describe this reported model comparison. They show that the evaluated STDP-based SNN combines about 4,000 spikes with about 5 mJ per inference. Energy also depends on how those events are processed, so the same spike count in a different model or evaluation setting does not guarantee the same energy value.

A useful mental model is to picture each inference as a journey with two linked records. Spike count records how many discrete events travel through an SNN, while energy per inference records the estimated cost of completing the whole journey. Converted training produces the busiest SNN journey, surrogate-gradient training reduces both traffic and cost, and STDP produces the sparsest and lowest-energy result. Reading both records together reveals the central pattern: sparser spiking accompanies lower energy across the evaluated SNNs, while representation and computation complete the explanation of each model's energy result.
