## Training Loss and Convergence Behavior

Having established training loss as a measure of prediction error during learning, we can use its movement across epochs to study how efficiently each spiking neural network strategy learns. Imagine checking three learners after every round of practice. A score recorded after only one round tells you where each learner started, while a sequence of scores reveals who improves quickly, who improves steadily, and who settles into a stable level early. A training-loss curve provides the same kind of history for a model.

An **epoch** is one complete pass through the training data. After each epoch, the model's loss records how large its training error is under the chosen loss measure. Lower loss represents a closer fit to the training objective. When the loss falls over successive epochs, the model is making progress. When the reductions become very small, training is approaching **convergence**: a state in which additional epochs produce little further improvement.

Convergence therefore has several dimensions. The **convergence rate** describes how quickly loss decreases. **Training stability** describes how smoothly and consistently that decrease occurs. The later part of the curve shows whether learning continues, slows gradually, or reaches an early plateau. Reading all three dimensions requires following the complete trajectory rather than selecting one convenient point.

The twenty-epoch curves below compare converted, surrogate-gradient, and STDP-based SNNs. Begin at the left edge, where the curves show their initial losses. Then follow their slopes through the early and middle epochs before examining how they behave near epoch 20.

![Training loss across 20 epochs for converted, surrogate-gradient, and STDP-based SNN models](/test-2/assets/source-visuals/2510-27379v1-page-10-graph-g1-training-loss-across-20-epochs-for-converted-su.png)

*Training loss across 20 epochs for converted, surrogate-gradient, and STDP-based SNN models* *(p. 10)*

The surrogate-gradient curve descends most steeply and most consistently. Its early slope shows that substantial loss reduction occurs within relatively few epochs. The continued downward progression then shows that improvement persists as training proceeds. By the later epochs, the curve has reached a low-loss region more quickly than the other two strategies. Together, the steep decline and smooth progression indicate the fastest convergence among the three strategies.

The converted SNN follows a more moderate path. Its loss decreases across the training run, so the model continues moving toward the training objective. Its curve falls less rapidly than the surrogate-gradient curve, however, which means it requires more epochs to achieve comparable reductions. The trajectory still represents convergence, but at an intermediate rate. Thinking of the curves as downhill paths, the surrogate-gradient SNN takes the steeper route, while the converted SNN follows a gentler descent toward a lower-loss region.

The STDP-based SNN shows the slowest progression. Its loss decreases more gradually during the early epochs and then changes less as training continues. This flattening is an **early plateau**: the curve still has a low or nearly level slope, but each additional epoch yields only a small reduction in loss. The plateau reveals limited later-epoch progress within the observed twenty-epoch window. It supports a description of slower convergence and earlier stabilization for this training run.

A curve's slope gives a practical way to interpret these patterns. Between two epochs, a large downward change means rapid improvement over that interval. A small downward change means slower improvement. A nearly horizontal segment means that the loss has stabilized over those epochs. The exact steepness can vary from one interval to another, so a sound interpretation examines the early, middle, and late portions separately.

For example, suppose two models finish close together at epoch 20. One may have reached that region after only a few epochs and then remained stable, while the other may have approached it gradually throughout training. Their endpoints would look similar, yet their learning behavior would differ substantially. The first has a faster convergence rate; the second needs more training rounds to reach the same neighborhood. The trajectory supplies information that the endpoint alone cannot provide.

The table preserves the exact epoch-by-epoch measurements, allowing two nearby points to be distinguished and a perceived pattern to be verified. A useful reading method is to identify a shape in the graph, select representative epochs from each part of the run, and confirm the pattern using the corresponding table entries.

**Question.** If the STDP-based loss decreases during training, why describe its convergence as slow?

**Answer.** Convergence rate depends on how much loss falls and how quickly that reduction occurs. The STDP-based curve moves downward, so learning is occurring, but its decline is more gradual and becomes flatter earlier. Smaller changes across later epochs indicate that progress is slowing sooner than it does for the other strategies. "Slow convergence" therefore describes the trajectory across time rather than claiming that the loss never improves.

A beginner may also wonder whether the smoothest curve must always be the best curve. Smoothness describes stability, while loss level and convergence rate describe different properties. A smooth curve can decline slowly, and a steep curve can reach a lower region quickly. Here, the surrogate-gradient trajectory combines a steep decline with consistent progress, so its convergence judgment draws on both rate and stability. The converted trajectory remains moderate, while the STDP-based trajectory stabilizes earlier at a more slowly reached level.

**Question.** Can epoch 20 alone determine which strategy converged fastest?

**Answer.** Epoch 20 shows where each strategy finished within the observed run, but convergence speed depends on the path taken to get there. Early and middle epochs reveal when the major reductions occurred, and later epochs reveal whether improvement continued or plateaued. Judging the full twenty-epoch sequence supports the conclusion that the surrogate-gradient SNN converges fastest, the converted SNN converges at a moderate rate, and the STDP-based SNN converges more slowly with an earlier plateau.

The complete mental model is a journey down an error landscape recorded one epoch at a time. Initial loss marks the starting height, the curve's downward slope represents the pace of learning, smooth progression reflects stable improvement, and a flattening tail signals that further epochs are producing smaller gains. Across twenty epochs, surrogate-gradient training follows the fastest and most consistent descent, conversion produces a steadier moderate descent, and STDP-based training moves more gradually before leveling earlier.
