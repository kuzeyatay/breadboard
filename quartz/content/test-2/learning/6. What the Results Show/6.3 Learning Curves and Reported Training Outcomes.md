## Learning Curves and Reported Training Outcomes

With the earlier view of optimization in place, training accuracy gives us a complementary way to follow learning: it tracks how often a model classifies its training examples correctly as training progresses. A final accuracy value tells us where a run ended, while a **training-accuracy curve** reveals the route taken to reach that value. That route helps answer two separate questions: How high did accuracy rise within the available training period, and how quickly did it approach that level?

Imagine checking three learners after twenty practice sessions. Their final scores provide a useful comparison, yet those scores conceal whether one learner improved rapidly, another advanced steadily, and a third stopped improving halfway through. Plotting the score after every session exposes those differences. In a learning curve, the horizontal axis plays the role of practice time, measured here in **epochs**. One epoch represents one complete pass through the training data. The vertical axis shows training accuracy, the proportion of training examples classified correctly.

If a model correctly classifies $C$ examples out of $N$ training examples, its training accuracy is
$$
A=\frac{C}{N}\times 100\%
$$
Where $A$ is training accuracy as a percentage, $C$ is the number of correctly classified training examples, and $N$ is the total number of evaluated training examples. This formula lets us compute one point on the curve. Repeating the calculation after each epoch creates an **accuracy trajectory**: the sequence of accuracy values showing how learning unfolds over time.

The shape of that trajectory carries information beyond its endpoint. A steep upward segment means that accuracy is increasing substantially over a small number of epochs. A gentler rise means that improvement is arriving more gradually. When the curve begins to flatten, each additional epoch produces a smaller change in training accuracy. That flattening region is often described as stabilization or a plateau. The epoch at which a trajectory approaches this region provides a practical view of its convergence time within the observed training window.

The three trajectories below compare converted, surrogate-gradient, and STDP-based spiking neural networks across twenty epochs. Reading them from left to right shows how quickly each approach gains training accuracy; reading their vertical positions near the right edge shows the outcomes reported at the end of the twenty-epoch window.

The surrogate-gradient trajectory rises most steeply. Its shape indicates that this model gains accuracy faster during the observed epochs than the other two SNN variants. By epoch twenty, it stabilizes near 98% training accuracy. The reported outcome therefore contains two linked observations: the model reaches a high endpoint, and its trajectory approaches that endpoint comparatively quickly. Describing only the final value would omit the speed visible in the earlier portion of the curve.

The converted SNN follows a slower trajectory. Its accuracy continues to develop across the training window, but the rise is less rapid than the surrogate-gradient curve. That shape qualifies the claim of competitive performance. A converted model can approach a strong final result while requiring more of the twenty-epoch window to get there. In the practice-session analogy, this learner eventually performs well but needs more sessions before its score settles near its later level.

The STDP-based SNN improves more gradually and reaches a plateau earlier. Once its curve flattens, later epochs add relatively little training accuracy. Its trajectory therefore supports a more limited reported accuracy outcome within this comparison: learning occurs, but improvement slows at an earlier and lower region than for the surrogate-gradient approach. The early plateau is consistent with the reported limits of gradual unsupervised adaptation. The curve turns that broad description into an observable pattern-steady initial progress followed by diminishing changes.

The shaded bands around the curves add another layer of interpretation. Each central curve represents mean accuracy across five independent runs, while its surrounding band shows plus or minus one standard deviation. A narrow band means that the runs remained relatively close to their mean trajectory at that epoch. A wider band means that the runs varied more. These bands encourage us to read each line as an average pattern supported by repeated trials rather than as the exact path followed by every run.

**Question.** If the surrogate-gradient SNN reaches about 98% by epoch twenty, can we describe 98% as its accuracy in every setting?

**Answer.** The careful claim is that it stabilizes near 98% training accuracy within this twenty-epoch comparison. The value belongs to the reported evaluation context: the particular model setup, training procedure, data, measurement, and run aggregation used to produce the curve. The graph supports comparison among the displayed trajectories under those conditions. It does not establish the same percentage for other datasets, architectures, or evaluation settings.

This careful phrasing creates a **context-bounded reported outcome**. A result is context-bounded when the claim preserves the conditions represented by the evidence. "The surrogate-gradient SNN stabilizes near 98% training accuracy by epoch twenty in the reported comparison" retains both the metric and the observation window. "The surrogate-gradient SNN achieves 98% accuracy" drops those boundaries and makes the claim broader than the curve supports.

Training accuracy also answers a focused question: how well the model fits the examples used during training. Deployment decisions require additional evidence, because a system may face unseen examples and other practical constraints. Here, the curve contributes evidence about learning behavior. Its slope describes the pace of improvement, its plateau describes where improvement slows during the observed epochs, its endpoint records the reported training outcome, and its shaded band describes variation across repeated runs.

**Question.** Does a steeper curve always identify the preferable SNN?

**Answer.** A steeper curve identifies faster training-accuracy improvement within the plotted interval. Preference depends on the goal of the comparison. When rapid progress toward high training accuracy is the priority, the surrogate-gradient trajectory is favorable. Other evaluation goals can require additional metrics, so the curve should support a specific claim about learning speed and reported training performance rather than serve as a complete model-selection rule.

A useful mental model is to treat the graph as a record of motion rather than a scoreboard frozen at epoch twenty. The surrogate-gradient SNN climbs quickly and stabilizes near a high level, the converted SNN advances more slowly, and the STDP-based SNN improves gradually before reaching an earlier plateau. The endpoints tell us where the trajectories finish within twenty epochs; the slopes and flattening regions explain how they arrive there; and the shaded bands show how consistently that story appears across five runs. Together, these features turn a reported accuracy number into a bounded account of both final training performance and speed of learning.
