### Comparative Results Across Models and Metrics

Imagine a small wearable device trying to recognize a gesture from a stream of sensor readings. It has a tiny battery, limited memory, and a strict time budget: if the model waits too long, the response feels sluggish; if it computes too much, the battery drains quickly. A conventional artificial neural network can often give very high accuracy, but it usually processes dense numerical activations layer after layer. A spiking neural network changes the accounting. It asks the model to communicate through brief events called spikes, so computation happens mainly when activity occurs. Once several models are placed beside one another, the central question becomes practical: how much accuracy, speed, and energy does each design buy for the amount of spiking activity it uses?

Accuracy is the easiest metric to recognize because it answers the most familiar question: out of all test examples, what fraction did the model classify correctly? If a model sees 10,000 images and classifies 9,800 correctly, its accuracy is 98%. In comparative results, conventional ANN or CNN models usually set the strongest reference point for raw accuracy because they use continuous-valued activations and mature gradient-based training. Those continuous activations are like adjustable volume levels: each neuron can pass along a finely graded number. Spiking models use events over time, so they must express information through when spikes occur, how often they occur, and which neurons spike.

A converted SNN begins with a trained ANN and translates its activations into spiking behavior. This often preserves much of the ANN's accuracy because the starting point already learned a useful input-output mapping. The cost appears in time and spike count: if a converted model uses rate coding, a larger activation may be represented by many spikes over a window of time. A bright pixel or strong feature may need repeated spikes before the downstream layer receives a stable signal. The model can perform well, but it may need many events to say what the ANN said with one continuous number.

A surrogate-gradient SNN trains the spiking model more directly. The difficulty is that a spike is a sharp all-or-nothing event, while gradient learning prefers smooth changes. A surrogate gradient supplies a smooth stand-in during training so the model can adjust its weights even though the forward pass still uses spikes. This method often gives a strong accuracy-energy compromise because the network learns when spikes are useful rather than simply inheriting a dense firing pattern from an ANN. In the reported learning curves over 20 epochs, the surrogate-gradient SNN rises fastest and stabilizes near 98% accuracy by the final epoch. The shaded variation around the curve represents plus or minus one standard deviation across five runs, so a narrow band means repeated training runs behaved similarly, while a wider band would mean the outcome depended more heavily on the particular run.

**Question.** Why can a surrogate-gradient SNN learn efficiently if spikes are all-or-nothing events?

**Answer.** During the forward computation, the neuron still emits discrete spikes. During training, the sharp spike threshold is paired with a smooth mathematical approximation so gradient descent can estimate how a small weight change would affect future performance. The approximation guides learning, while the deployed model still communicates through spikes.

Latency asks how long the model needs before it can make a decision. In a dense ANN, latency is usually tied to the number of layers, operations, and hardware speed. In an SNN, latency also depends on the time window over which spikes are collected. A rate-coded converted SNN may need a longer window because the model estimates signal strength by counting spikes. For example, if one class becomes clear only after thousands of spikes have accumulated across layers, the model may wait longer before committing. A surrogate-trained SNN can sometimes reduce this delay because training can encourage informative spikes earlier in the window. STDP-based models, which adapt through local spike timing, may be very sparse, but their accuracy can plateau earlier, so reducing latency has to be balanced against how much evidence the model has gathered.

Energy consumption measures how much power the model spends per inference. The comparison across four model types gives a concrete scale. A conventional ANN/CNN uses about 200 millijoules per inference in the reported comparison. A converted SNN lowers this to about 20 millijoules. A surrogate-gradient SNN reaches about 15 millijoules. An STDP-based SNN reaches about 5 millijoules. The reason follows from the way computation is triggered. Dense ANN computation performs many multiply-and-accumulate operations whether the input is highly informative or mostly quiet. Spiking computation can skip work during silence, and fewer spikes usually mean fewer synaptic events to process.

Spike count makes that energy story visible. In the same comparison, the converted SNN produces about 20,000 spikes per inference, the surrogate-gradient SNN about 12,000 spikes, and the STDP-based SNN about 4,000 spikes. A spike count is simply the number of discrete events generated while processing one input. If two models have similar accuracy but one uses far fewer spikes, the sparser model is usually more attractive for edge devices because each spike can trigger communication and synaptic computation. The STDP-based model's roughly 4,000 spikes align with its low energy use of about 5 millijoules, showing a very sparse operating style. The tradeoff is that this sparse, local-learning approach may sit below the strongest accuracy levels reached by ANN/CNN or surrogate-gradient models.

A useful way to compare energy savings is with a percent reduction:
$$
\text{Energy reduction}=\frac{E_{\text{baseline}}-E_{\text{model}}}{E_{\text{baseline}}}\times 100\%
$$
Here, $E_{\text{baseline}}$ is the energy used by the reference model, usually the ANN/CNN, and $E_{\text{model}}$ is the energy used by the spiking model being compared. Using 200 millijoules for the ANN/CNN and 5 millijoules for the STDP-based SNN gives
$$
\frac{200-5}{200}\times 100\% = 97.5\%
$$
That means the STDP-based model uses only a small fraction of the ANN/CNN energy in this comparison. Using 15 millijoules for the surrogate-gradient SNN gives
$$
\frac{200-15}{200}\times 100\% = 92.5\%
$$
This places surrogate-gradient training in a compelling middle region: much lower energy than the ANN/CNN reference while maintaining high reported accuracy.

**Question.** If STDP-based SNNs use the least energy, why choose a surrogate-gradient SNN?

**Answer.** The best choice depends on the constraint. STDP-based models can be excellent when battery life and sparse activity dominate the design goal. Surrogate-gradient SNNs are often attractive when the task still needs very high accuracy and reliable convergence, because their training process can shape spike patterns using a supervised objective.

Convergence describes how the model improves during training and whether it settles into a stable performance range. The learning curves over 20 epochs show three different behaviors. The surrogate-gradient SNN climbs quickly and approaches about 98% by epoch 20, suggesting that supervised gradient-based adjustment gives a strong training signal. The converted SNN improves more slowly because its spiking behavior depends on how well the ANN-to-SNN translation preserves useful activations across time. The STDP-based model improves gradually and plateaus earlier, which fits its local adaptation rule: each synapse changes using nearby spike timing rather than a global supervised error signal.

The shaded bands around the learning curves help temper the interpretation. A mean curve shows the average across five independent runs, while the band shows how much the runs varied. When comparing models, the final accuracy number alone gives an incomplete picture. A model that reaches high accuracy with little variation is easier to trust than a model that sometimes reaches the same value but sometimes falls short. The reported curves support a cautious reading: surrogate-gradient training appears strongest for fast, stable accuracy gains in this benchmark setting, while STDP appears strongest for sparse, low-energy inference, and converted SNNs occupy a bridge position between dense ANN training and event-based execution.

The mental model is a three-way budget. Accuracy measures how correct the model is, latency measures how long it waits before deciding, and energy plus spike count measure how much event-driven work it spends to get there. ANN/CNN models spend dense computation to achieve strong accuracy. Converted SNNs preserve much of that learned behavior while reducing energy, though rate-coded spikes can remain numerous. Surrogate-gradient SNNs learn spiking patterns more directly and can reach high accuracy with fewer spikes and much lower energy. STDP-based SNNs push sparsity and energy efficiency furthest, making them appealing when every millijoule counts. Comparing these models means reading accuracy, latency, energy, spike count, and convergence together as one design surface rather than treating any single number as the whole story.
