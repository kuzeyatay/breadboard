### Comparative Results Across Models and Metrics

Imagine a tiny wearable sensor trying to recognize a gesture while running on a small battery. Each inference is one complete attempt to classify an input, such as deciding whether the current motion pattern means "walking," "running," or "still." A conventional artificial neural network can often produce strong accuracy, but it keeps moving dense numerical activations through many layers every time it processes an input. That style of computation is powerful, yet it spends energy even when the useful signal is sparse. A spiking neural network changes the accounting: information travels as spikes, brief events that occur at particular times, so the system can spend computation mainly when activity happens.

Comparing these models begins with accuracy because accuracy tells us whether the system is solving the task at all. Accuracy is the fraction of predictions that are correct. If a model classifies 980 examples correctly out of 1,000, its accuracy is
$$
\text{accuracy}=\frac{\text{number of correct predictions}}{\text{total number of predictions}}=\frac{980}{1000}=0.98
$$
Here, the value $0.98$ means $98\%$. The "number of correct predictions" counts successful classifications, and the "total number of predictions" counts every test example the model attempted. In the reported comparisons, artificial neural networks tend to dominate raw accuracy, while spiking systems can come close depending on how they are trained. Surrogate-gradient spiking networks can stabilize near $98\%$ accuracy over training, converted spiking networks improve more gradually, and STDP-based spiking networks improve more slowly and plateau earlier. The practical reading is that the highest-accuracy option may be the dense ANN or a carefully trained surrogate-gradient SNN, while the most energy-frugal option may accept a smaller accuracy margin.

Latency enters as soon as the learner asks how long the system must wait before it can answer. In a dense network, the main delay comes from passing numerical activations through the computation graph. In a spiking network, time itself can be part of the representation: the model may need to observe spikes over a window before its output becomes reliable. A rate-coded converted SNN often needs many spikes across time because a stronger activation is represented by more spike events. That can preserve behavior from an ANN conversion, but it can also increase the number of events needed before a confident decision appears. A surrogate-gradient SNN can learn spiking patterns directly, so it can often use fewer spikes than a simple converted model while maintaining strong accuracy. Latency therefore sits between accuracy and event count: waiting longer can allow more spikes to accumulate, but a well-trained spiking model aims to reach a useful decision with fewer events.

Energy gives the comparison its sharpest shape. Energy per inference measures how much energy the system spends to produce one prediction. In the reported energy comparison, an ANN/CNN uses about $200$ millijoules per inference. A converted SNN uses about $20$ millijoules, a surrogate-gradient SNN about $15$ millijoules, and an STDP-based SNN about $5$ millijoules. A millijoule, written $mJ$, is one thousandth of a joule, so smaller values mean less battery drain for each prediction.

The percent energy reduction from one model to another can be computed by comparing the saved energy with the original energy:
$$
\text{energy reduction}=\frac{E_{\text{baseline}}-E_{\text{new}}}{E_{\text{baseline}}}\times 100\%
$$
In this formula, $E_{\text{baseline}}$ is the energy used by the comparison model, and $E_{\text{new}}$ is the energy used by the lower-energy model. If the ANN baseline uses $200\,mJ$ and the STDP-based SNN uses $5\,mJ$, then
$$
\frac{200-5}{200}\times 100\%=97.5\%
$$
So the STDP-based model uses about $97.5\%$ less energy per inference than the ANN baseline in this comparison. A surrogate-gradient SNN at $15\,mJ$ gives
$$
\frac{200-15}{200}\times 100\%=92.5\%
$$
Which places it in the same broad low-energy range while preserving stronger trained accuracy than the STDP case.

**Question.** If the ANN is more accurate, why would anyone choose an SNN for a real device?

**Answer.** A real device often has more constraints than accuracy alone. A wearable or edge sensor may need to run continuously, stay cool, and preserve battery life. If an SNN reduces energy from about $200\,mJ$ to $15\,mJ$ or $5\,mJ$ per inference while keeping performance acceptable for the task, the energy savings can be worth the accuracy tradeoff.

Spike count explains where much of the energy saving comes from. A spike count is the number of spike events generated during one inference. The ANN comparison has dense continuous-valued operations rather than spike events, and its energy is highest. The converted SNN uses around $20{,}000$ spikes per inference. The surrogate-gradient SNN uses around $12{,}000$ spikes. The STDP-based SNN uses around $4{,}000$ spikes. The downward movement from $20{,}000$ to $12{,}000$ to $4{,}000$ shows increasing sparsity: the model sends fewer events to complete the same general kind of task. Sparsity is useful because neuromorphic and event-driven systems can often avoid work when no spike is present. A helpful analogy is a room with motion-triggered lights. A dense system behaves like lights that remain on everywhere during every check. A sparse spiking system behaves like lights that turn on only where motion occurs.

STDP-based learning sits at the extreme energy-saving end because updates are local and activity is sparse. STDP stands for spike-timing-dependent plasticity. It adjusts a connection using the relative timing of spikes: when one neuron's spike consistently helps precede another neuron's spike, the connection can strengthen; when timing suggests a weaker relationship, the connection can weaken. This local timing rule can operate with very low energy, which fits ultra-low-power settings. Its learning behavior also explains the earlier plateau in accuracy curves: local adaptation can discover useful structure, but it may stop improving sooner than a globally optimized surrogate-gradient method.

Surrogate-gradient training takes a different route. Spikes are discrete events, and ordinary gradient learning expects smooth changes, so surrogate gradients provide a smooth training signal that approximates how changing internal parameters should affect performance. During training, this lets the model improve spiking behavior through gradient-based optimization. The reported learning curves over 20 epochs show surrogate-gradient SNNs gaining accuracy fastest and stabilizing near $98\%$ by the final epoch. An epoch is one full pass through the training data. The shaded variation across five independent runs, described as $\pm 1$ standard deviation, shows that repeated training runs can vary even when the same method is used. A standard deviation is a measure of spread around the mean; narrower bands mean runs behave more consistently, while wider bands mean training outcomes vary more.

**Question.** Does "converges faster" mean the model is always better?

**Answer.** Faster convergence means the model reaches its training performance level in fewer epochs. A surrogate-gradient SNN can climb quickly toward high accuracy, but the final choice still depends on energy, spike count, latency, hardware target, and stability across runs. A slower or lower-accuracy model may still fit a device that has strict power limits.

Comparative results for spiking models need careful reading because the field uses many combinations of encoding schemes, neuron models, training rules, datasets, and hardware platforms. A rate-coded converted SNN, a surrogate-gradient SNN, and an STDP-based SNN can all be called spiking neural networks, yet they spend spikes differently and learn by different mechanisms. Accuracy, latency, spike count, energy per inference, and energy-delay behavior describe different sides of the same design decision. The most useful mental model is a set of connected tradeoffs: dense ANNs spend more energy to achieve strong raw accuracy; converted SNNs reduce energy while often keeping many rate-coded spikes; surrogate-gradient SNNs learn efficient spiking patterns with strong accuracy; STDP-based SNNs push sparsity and energy use lowest while accepting earlier accuracy plateaus. A good comparison follows the whole chain from prediction quality, to time needed for a decision, to events generated, to energy spent, because the best model is the one whose tradeoffs match the device and task.
