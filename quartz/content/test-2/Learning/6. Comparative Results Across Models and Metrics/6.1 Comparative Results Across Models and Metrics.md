## Comparative Results Across Models and Metrics

Imagine choosing a neural network for a small edge device, such as a hand-gesture sensor, a wearable monitor, or a robot that must react quickly while running on limited battery power. A familiar artificial neural network can often classify patterns accurately, but it tends to keep many numerical activations moving through the network at every layer. That style of computation is powerful, yet it can spend energy even when only a small part of the input has changed. A spiking neural network approaches the same problem differently: it sends information as brief events called spikes, and the timing and number of those spikes become part of the computation. Comparing these systems requires more than asking which one is most accurate. A practical model also has to answer quickly, use little energy, and avoid producing unnecessary spike traffic.

Accuracy gives the cleanest starting point because it tells us whether the model is learning the task at all. On simple image-recognition benchmarks such as MNIST, spiking systems can come close to conventional artificial networks. Directly trained surrogate-gradient SNNs are especially strong because they use gradient-based learning while still respecting the spike-based behavior of the network. The word "surrogate" means that training temporarily uses a smooth approximation to handle the spike's sharp on/off threshold, allowing the model to adjust its weights with familiar optimization tools. In comparative results, surrogate-gradient SNNs can approach ANN accuracy within about 1-2%, reaching roughly 97.8% in the reported integrated comparison. Converted SNNs also perform competitively because they begin from a trained ANN and translate continuous activations into spike rates. STDP-based SNNs reach slightly lower accuracy, around 95.5% in the same comparison, because their learning rule is local and timing-driven rather than globally optimized for a labeled objective.

Read the table as a tradeoff map. Higher accuracy values show how well each model handles the classification task, while normalized energy shows how costly that performance is. The useful pattern is that the most ANN-like systems tend to preserve accuracy most directly, while the most spike-sparse systems reduce energy most aggressively. A beginner might expect the best model to be the one with the highest accuracy column, but an edge device often cares about the ratio between correct decisions and energy spent per decision. A model that is slightly less accurate can be more suitable when it extends battery life, reduces heat, or enables always-on sensing.

Latency is the time between receiving an input and producing an answer. If a camera-based gesture recognizer waits too long before classifying a motion, the response feels delayed even if the final label is correct. Latency is usually measured in milliseconds, abbreviated ms. One millisecond is one-thousandth of a second, so a 10 ms inference is fast enough for many interactive sensing tasks. SNN latency depends on how many time steps the network needs before enough spikes have arrived to support a decision. Converted SNNs often need a longer simulation window because they approximate ANN activations through spike rates: a stronger activation is represented by more spikes over time. Surrogate-gradient SNNs can respond faster because training directly shapes when spikes occur. STDP-based SNNs may adapt more gradually during early inference, but optimized versions can settle into sub-15 ms behavior.

In the graph, shorter bars mean faster inference. The surrogate-gradient SNN bar is low because it reaches decisions around 10 ms. Energy and spike count explain why SNNs are attractive for hardware-aware computing. Energy per inference means the amount of energy used to process one input and produce one output. Here it is measured in millijoules, abbreviated mJ. A millijoule is one-thousandth of a joule. Spike count means the number of spikes generated during one inference. These two quantities are related because each spike can trigger communication and computation. Fewer spikes often mean less movement of data and less energy use, especially on neuromorphic hardware designed for event-based operation.

The reported comparison gives approximate values across four model types. An ANN/CNN uses about 200 mJ per inference because dense continuous-valued operations remain active throughout the computation. A converted SNN lowers energy to about 20 mJ, a tenfold reduction, but it still produces about 20,000 spikes because rate coding needs many events to approximate continuous activations. A surrogate-gradient SNN uses about 15 mJ and about 12,000 spikes because training can shape more efficient spike patterns. An STDP-based SNN reaches about 5 mJ and roughly 4,000 spikes, making it the sparsest and lowest-energy option among the compared models. The phrase "90-97% lower energy" describes the scale of reduction when SNNs are compared with the ANN/CNN baseline: moving from 200 mJ to 20 mJ is a 90% reduction, and moving from 200 mJ to 5 mJ is a 97.5% reduction.

A simple way to compute an energy reduction percentage is

\[
\text{Energy reduction}=\frac{E_{\text{baseline}}-E_{\text{model}}}{E_{\text{baseline}}}\times 100\%.
\]

Here, \(E_{\text{baseline}}\) is the energy used by the reference model, such as the 200 mJ ANN/CNN, and \(E_{\text{model}}\) is the energy used by the model being compared. For an STDP-based SNN using 5 mJ, the calculation is \((200-5)/200 \times 100\% = 97.5\%\). This formula lets you translate raw energy numbers into a percentage savings that is easier to compare across systems.

**Question.** If STDP-based SNNs use the least energy, should they always be chosen for edge devices?

**Answer.** They are a strong choice when energy is the main constraint, especially for ultra-low-power or unsupervised settings, but model choice depends on the whole task. A surrogate-gradient SNN may be better when the device needs both high accuracy and low latency, because it keeps accuracy close to ANN performance while still using much less energy.

Convergence describes how quickly a model improves during training. In the reported comparison, surrogate-gradient SNNs converge quickly, with strong behavior by around the 20th epoch. An epoch means one full pass through the training data. Converted SNNs inherit much of their capability from the ANN training stage, then depend on how well the conversion preserves activation behavior through spikes. STDP-based SNNs converge more slowly because each synapse changes according to local spike timing: if a presynaptic spike regularly arrives just before a postsynaptic spike, the connection can strengthen; if the timing pattern differs, it can weaken. This local timing rule is biologically inspired and energy-efficient, but it does not push every weight using a global task error in the same direct way as gradient descent.

**Question.** Why can a model with more spikes sometimes have higher accuracy?

**Answer.** More spikes can give the network a richer signal over time. In a converted SNN, many spikes may be needed to approximate the continuous activation values of the original ANN. That can preserve accuracy, but it also increases latency and energy. Sparse spiking is powerful when the network learns to send the right spikes at the right times rather than relying on large spike counts.

The mental model is a set of linked tradeoffs. Accuracy asks whether the answer is correct. Latency asks how quickly the answer arrives. Energy asks how costly the answer is. Spike count helps explain that cost by showing how much event traffic the network creates. Convergence asks how easily the model learns its behavior during training. ANN/CNN models sit at the accuracy-heavy, energy-heavy end of the comparison. Converted SNNs preserve much of that accuracy while reducing energy, though they often pay with longer spike windows and higher spike counts. Surrogate-gradient SNNs occupy a balanced region: high accuracy, low latency, and moderate energy. STDP-based SNNs occupy the sparse, low-energy region, with a tradeoff in accuracy and training speed. Choosing among them means matching the model's shape to the application's constraints, the same way an engineer chooses a vehicle by considering speed, fuel use, load, and terrain together.
