### Comparative Results Across Models and Metrics

Imagine choosing a neural model for a tiny wearable device that must recognize a pattern from sensor data while running on a small battery. Accuracy is the first thing you would ask about, because a model that saves power but makes poor decisions will fail at its job. Yet accuracy alone cannot decide the choice. A wearable also has to respond quickly, avoid wasting energy, and keep its internal activity sparse enough that the hardware does little work when the input is quiet. Comparative evaluation of spiking neural networks begins with this practical tension: each model type offers a different balance between correct predictions, response time, energy use, and number of spikes.

Start with the familiar artificial neural network, such as a convolutional neural network. It represents information with continuous numerical activations. Every layer computes arrays of values, and those values are updated whether the input is changing dramatically or only slightly. This style often gives strong raw accuracy because training methods for conventional networks are mature and stable. When accuracy is the main metric, conventional ANNs remain a strong reference point. They set a high-performance baseline that spiking models try to approach while using event-based computation.

A converted spiking neural network begins from a trained conventional network and translates its activations into spike activity. The reason this approach exists is simple: training conventional networks is reliable, so conversion tries to keep much of that learned behavior while moving computation into spikes. A common way to do this is rate coding, where a larger activation is represented by more spikes over a time window. For example, if one neuron fires many times during the window and another fires only a few times, the first neuron is carrying a stronger signal. This makes conversion intuitive, but it also explains why converted SNNs can still generate many spikes. The network has inherited a dense style of representation and then expresses it through spike counts.

Surrogate-gradient SNNs take a more direct path. They train spiking neurons while using a mathematical workaround that makes gradient-based learning possible. A spike is a sharp event: below threshold the neuron stays quiet, and at threshold it emits a spike. That sharp jump is difficult for ordinary gradient descent, because gradients depend on smooth changes. A surrogate gradient replaces the hard spike step during learning with a smooth approximation, so the model can adjust its weights in a useful direction. The actual computation still uses spikes, but the training signal becomes easier to optimize. This helps explain why surrogate-gradient SNNs can reach high accuracy while also reducing energy and spike count compared with converted SNNs.

STDP-based SNNs use a more local learning rule. STDP means spike-timing-dependent plasticity. A synapse changes according to the relative timing of spikes from the sending neuron and the receiving neuron. If the sending neuron tends to fire just before the receiving neuron, the connection can strengthen; if the timing relationship is weaker or reversed, the connection can weaken. The learning rule is local because each synapse mainly uses nearby spike timing information instead of a global error signal propagated through the whole network. This locality makes STDP attractive for low-power systems, especially when the goal is sparse activity and on-device adaptation. Its accuracy can sit below the strongest supervised methods because local timing rules have less direct access to the final classification error.

Accuracy curves across training make these differences easier to interpret. In a 20-epoch comparison of three SNN variants, the surrogate-gradient SNN rises fastest and stabilizes near 98% accuracy by the end of training. Converted SNNs improve more slowly, reflecting the extra difficulty of translating a continuous trained model into a spike-based computation over time. STDP models improve gradually and then level off earlier, which fits their local unsupervised adaptation style. The shaded variation around the curves, measured as plus or minus one standard deviation across five independent runs, is part of the result: a narrow band suggests more consistent training behavior, while a wider band means different runs can land at meaningfully different performance levels.

Latency enters once accuracy depends on spike timing. Latency means the time needed to produce a usable answer. In a spiking system, a decision may require waiting for enough spikes to arrive. A rate-coded converted SNN may need a longer observation window because the signal is represented by spike counts: the model must collect enough events before the count reliably reflects the intended activation. A surrogate-gradient SNN can learn more efficient spike patterns, so it may reach a confident decision with fewer events. STDP systems can be very sparse, which helps energy, but the timing and stability of their learned representations can affect how quickly a reliable decision emerges. Latency therefore belongs beside accuracy: a model that eventually becomes correct may still be unsuitable for a fast-control application if it needs too many time steps.

Energy and spike count make the tradeoff concrete. Energy per inference means the amount of energy used to process one input and produce one output. Spike count means the number of spikes generated during that inference. These two quantities are linked because each spike can trigger communication and synaptic updates. Fewer spikes usually mean less activity for the hardware to perform, especially on neuromorphic systems designed around event-driven computation.

The comparison across four model types shows a clear energy pattern. A conventional ANN uses about 200 millijoules per inference, reflecting continuous-valued operations across the network. A converted SNN drops to about 20 millijoules, a large improvement, but still produces roughly 20,000 spikes per inference because rate-coded propagation remains active. A surrogate-gradient SNN reduces energy further to about 15 millijoules and uses about 12,000 spikes, showing that learning spike patterns directly can improve both power use and event efficiency. An STDP-based SNN reaches about 5 millijoules and about 4,000 spikes, giving the sparsest and lowest-energy profile among the compared models.

A useful way to read these numbers is through percentage energy reduction. If $E_{\text{ANN}}$ is the energy used by the ANN and $E_{\text{SNN}}$ is the energy used by a spiking model, then
$$
\text{energy reduction} = \frac{E_{\text{ANN}} - E_{\text{SNN}}}{E_{\text{ANN}}} \times 100\%
$$
Here, $E_{\text{ANN}}$ is the baseline energy per inference, $E_{\text{SNN}}$ is the energy per inference of the chosen spiking model, and the result tells you how much energy is saved compared with the ANN baseline. For the STDP-based SNN, the calculation is $(200 - 5) / 200 \times 100\% = 97.5\%$. For the surrogate-gradient SNN, it is $(200 - 15) / 200 \times 100\% = 92.5\%$. For the converted SNN, it is $(200 - 20) / 200 \times 100\% = 90\%$. These values explain why SNNs are attractive for energy-constrained settings: even when accuracy tradeoffs exist, the energy savings can be very large.

**Question.** If the STDP-based SNN uses the least energy, why would anyone choose a surrogate-gradient SNN instead?

**Answer.** The surrogate-gradient SNN can offer a stronger balance when accuracy matters more. It uses more energy than the STDP-based model, about 15 millijoules instead of 5 millijoules per inference, but it can train toward high supervised accuracy and reaches strong performance in the reported learning curves. The choice depends on the application: an ultra-low-power sensor may favor STDP, while a task needing higher predictive performance may favor surrogate-gradient training.

Spike count also helps separate models that look similar by energy alone. The converted SNN and surrogate-gradient SNN both save large amounts of energy compared with the ANN, but their spike counts differ substantially: about 20,000 spikes versus about 12,000 spikes per inference. That difference means the surrogate-gradient model is doing less event traffic for each decision. In hardware terms, fewer events can reduce communication, memory access, and synaptic activity. In learning terms, it suggests the model has found a more economical spike representation rather than simply translating dense activations into frequent firing.

A common beginner confusion is to treat spikes as ordinary numbers with a different name. A spike is an event at a time, and silence between spikes can carry meaning because no computation needs to happen there. In a dense ANN, a small activation is still a value that must be processed. In a sparse SNN, a quiet neuron may simply emit no event. This is why spike count is a performance metric rather than a decorative detail. It measures how much event-based work the network actually asks the hardware to perform.

**Question.** Does fewer spikes always mean a better spiking neural network?

**Answer.** Fewer spikes are useful when they preserve the information needed for the task. A model that fires rarely and loses accuracy has become too sparse for its job. A good comparison reads spike count together with accuracy, latency, and energy. The strongest model for a particular use case is the one whose spike activity is sparse enough to save power while still producing reliable decisions on time.

Convergence results should be read with care. A learning curve that rises quickly and stabilizes near a high value shows that the training method can reliably improve over epochs under the tested conditions. The surrogate-gradient curve reaching about 98% by epoch 20 is a strong sign of effective optimization in that setting. The converted SNN's slower progress suggests that conversion and temporal spike dynamics introduce extra adjustment costs. The STDP curve's earlier plateau fits a local learning mechanism that adapts from spike timing but has a more limited path toward task-level accuracy. These curves do not guarantee the same behavior on every dataset, hardware platform, or noisy real-world deployment. They show how the models behave under the reported benchmark-style comparison.

The mental model is a four-way tradeoff. ANNs are strong accuracy baselines but spend energy on continuous dense computation. Converted SNNs borrow ANN performance and gain large energy savings, while rate-coded activity can keep spike counts high. Surrogate-gradient SNNs learn spike patterns more directly, often giving a strong middle ground: high accuracy, fewer spikes, and lower energy. STDP-based SNNs push hardest toward sparse, local, low-energy operation, with accuracy that can be lower because learning is driven by timing relationships rather than a full supervised error signal. A careful comparison therefore reads each model through several lenses at once: accuracy tells whether the answer is useful, latency tells whether it arrives soon enough, energy tells whether the system can afford the computation, spike count tells how much event-based work was required, and convergence tells how reliably the model learns its behavior over training.
