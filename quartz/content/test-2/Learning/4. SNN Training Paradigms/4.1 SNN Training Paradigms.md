Imagine a small camera on a battery-powered drone watching a quiet hallway. Most pixels stay almost unchanged from one moment to the next, but the drone still has to recognize a hand wave quickly and spend as little energy as possible. A conventional artificial neural network usually processes dense numerical activations layer after layer, even when the scene is mostly still. A spiking neural network takes a different starting point: information travels as brief events called **spikes**. A neuron stays quiet while its input is weak, then emits a spike when its internal state crosses a threshold. Silence saves work, and the timing of spikes becomes part of the signal.

Training such a system means deciding how the network should adjust its connections so useful spikes happen at useful times. Each connection has a **weight**, a number that controls how strongly one neuron affects another. A large positive weight makes the receiving neuron's internal state rise more when a spike arrives; a smaller weight has a gentler effect. The training problem is to choose weights that turn input spike patterns into correct output decisions, such as recognizing a gesture or classifying a sound.

The difficulty comes from the spike itself. A spike is a sudden all-or-nothing event. Before threshold, the neuron stays silent; after threshold, it fires. That jump is excellent for sparse event-based computing, but it makes ordinary gradient training harder. Gradient descent works by asking, "If I change this weight a tiny amount, how does the error change?" In a smooth network, tiny weight changes cause tiny output changes, so the gradient gives a useful direction. In a spiking neuron, a tiny change may do nothing, then suddenly shift a spike time or create a spike. Training paradigms for SNNs are different ways of handling that central tension between useful discrete events and learnable weights.

One direct approach is **surrogate gradient descent**. The network still uses real spikes during the forward pass: neurons accumulate input, fire when threshold is reached, and pass events onward. During learning, however, the sharp spike threshold is temporarily treated as if it had a smooth slope nearby. This smooth substitute is called a **surrogate gradient** because it stands in for the missing ordinary gradient at the spike. The learner can then compute how the output error should push each weight up or down.

A simple way to picture this is a doorbell that rings only after enough pressure is applied. The actual ring is sudden, but during practice you might pretend there is a narrow "almost ringing" zone where extra pressure matters gradually. That pretend zone gives feedback about which direction to adjust. In an SNN, the surrogate gradient plays the same role: it makes the threshold trainable while preserving spike-based computation. This is why surrogate-trained SNNs often provide a strong balance between accuracy, latency, and energy. They can learn spiking patterns directly rather than inheriting them from another model, and they can reach competitive performance with relatively low response times, around 10 ms in the benchmark setting described here.

A second approach is **ANN-to-SNN conversion**. Here, training begins with a conventional artificial neural network. The ANN learns using continuous activations, where each neuron passes along a numerical value. After training, those activations are translated into spike rates. A high activation becomes many spikes over a simulation window; a low activation becomes fewer spikes. The converted SNN therefore tries to reproduce the behavior of a trained ANN by using spike counts as an approximation of continuous values.

This method is attractive because mature ANN training methods already work well. If a convolutional network has learned a visual task accurately, conversion offers a route to a spiking version without redesigning the whole learning process. The cost appears in time and activity. To estimate a continuous activation reliably, the converted SNN may need to observe spikes over a longer window. If one neuron represents the value 0.8 by firing more often than another neuron representing 0.2, the network needs enough time to distinguish those rates. That longer simulation window can raise latency, around 20 ms in the comparison here, and it can also produce many spikes, roughly 20,000 per inference in the energy-and-spike-count comparison. The converted model can be accurate, but its spike traffic remains relatively heavy because it is using spikes to imitate dense numerical activity.

The third approach is **spike-timing-dependent plasticity**, usually shortened to **STDP**. STDP learns from the relative timing of spikes between connected neurons. If a presynaptic neuron fires just before a postsynaptic neuron, the connection between them is strengthened, because the earlier spike may have helped cause the later one. If the timing goes the other way, the connection may be weakened. The learning rule is local: a synapse adjusts using nearby spike timing rather than a global error signal sent backward through the whole network.

A compact form of this idea is:
$$
\Delta w =
\begin{cases}
A_+ e^{-\Delta t / \tau_+}, & \Delta t > 0 \\
-A_- e^{\Delta t / \tau_-}, & \Delta t < 0
\end{cases}
$$
Here, $\Delta w$ is the change in the synaptic weight. The value $\Delta t$ is the time difference between the postsynaptic spike and the presynaptic spike, so $\Delta t > 0$ means the input spike came before the output spike. The constants $A_+$ and $A_-$ set how much strengthening or weakening can happen, and $\tau_+$ and $\tau_-$ set how quickly the timing effect fades as the spikes move farther apart in time. The formula lets you compute how strongly a synapse should change from a pair of spike times. Nearby cause-like timing produces a larger update; distant timing produces a smaller update.

STDP fits naturally with ultra-low-power and unsupervised settings because it can learn from spike timing without labeled answers at every step. Its activity can be extremely sparse, with energy near 5 mJ per inference and around 4,000 spikes in the comparison. The tradeoff is learning speed and task accuracy. Because STDP does not use the same direct error-correction signal as gradient descent, it can converge more slowly and may plateau earlier. It is well suited to situations where energy and local adaptation matter strongly, especially when the system must learn from streams of events rather than large labeled datasets.

The surrogate-gradient curve rises steeply and consistently because the network receives a direct training signal that tells weights how to reduce error. The converted SNN improves more moderately because its spiking behavior is constrained by the ANN behavior it is trying to approximate; its accuracy depends on how well spike rates reproduce continuous activations. The STDP curve climbs more slowly and levels off earlier because local timing rules discover useful structure gradually. Read the graph as a picture of how each learning rule "finds" good weights: surrogate gradients search with guided correction, conversion transfers a solution into spikes, and STDP shapes connections through repeated timing relationships.

**Question.** Why can surrogate gradient training use gradients if spikes are sudden events?

**Answer.** The network still communicates with sudden spikes, but the learning calculation uses a smooth stand-in near the firing threshold. That stand-in gives a usable slope for weight updates. The spike mechanism remains event-based during activity, while the training step borrows a smooth approximation only to decide how weights should change.

**Question.** Why does a converted SNN often need more spikes than a directly trained SNN?

**Answer.** A converted SNN often represents an ANN's continuous activation values through firing rates. To express a larger value, a neuron fires many times over a window; to express a smaller value, it fires fewer times. That rate code can require many events before the network has a reliable estimate. A directly trained spiking network can learn more task-specific spike patterns, so it can often use fewer events.

These three paradigms give SNN design its basic map. Surrogate gradient descent treats spikes as trainable events and usually offers the strongest balance when accuracy and low latency both matter. ANN-to-SNN conversion starts from the strength of conventional neural networks and trades some spike efficiency for an easier path to competitive performance. STDP lets synapses adapt from timing itself, giving very low energy and sparse activity when local, event-driven learning is the priority. The mental model is a triangle: one corner is guided optimization, one corner is converted dense computation, and one corner is timing-based adaptation. Every later tradeoff among accuracy, latency, energy, spike count, and convergence follows from where a training method sits inside that triangle.
