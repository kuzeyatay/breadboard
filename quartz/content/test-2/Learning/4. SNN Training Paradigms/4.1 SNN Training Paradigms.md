### SNN Training Paradigms

Now that event-driven sparsity is established, efficiency only pays off if the network can be trained to fire useful spikes at useful times. Consider a network whose connection weights must be adjusted so that input spike patterns lead to correct decisions.

The challenge comes from the spike itself. A spike is a sharp event: a neuron either fires at a moment or stays silent. In ordinary gradient descent, learning depends on asking a smooth question: "If this weight changes a tiny amount, how does the output error change?" Smooth activations make that question easy because tiny input changes cause tiny output changes. Spikes make the question harder because crossing a firing threshold changes the output suddenly. SNN training paradigms are different answers to that difficulty. The three main approaches are surrogate gradient descent, ANN-to-SNN conversion, and spike-timing dependent plasticity, usually called STDP.

Surrogate gradient descent trains the SNN directly while using a smooth helper curve during learning. During the forward pass, the neuron still behaves like a spiking neuron: it accumulates input, fires when its membrane potential crosses a threshold, and sends discrete spikes onward. During the backward pass, where gradients are computed, the sharp spike function is replaced by a smooth approximation called a surrogate gradient. "Surrogate" means substitute. The substitute does not change the idea that the network communicates through spikes; it gives the learning algorithm a usable slope near the firing threshold, so the weights can be nudged in the direction that improves the task.

A simple way to picture this is a door with a stiff latch. In actual use, the door is either closed or open. For learning, the network temporarily imagines a springy transition near the latch point, so it can estimate whether pushing slightly harder would have helped the door open at the desired time. That estimate lets the network learn spike timing and spike frequency patterns with gradient-based optimization. The practical result is a strong balance: surrogate-trained SNNs can closely approach ANN accuracy, often within about 1-2%, while keeping inference latency low, around 10 ms in the reported benchmark setting. They also converge quickly, with learning behavior that improves strongly by about the 20th epoch.

An epoch means one full pass through the training data. A training curve shows how performance changes as epochs pass. When accuracy rises quickly and then stabilizes at a high level, the learning rule is finding useful weights efficiently. The learning curves below compare the three SNN training paradigms over epochs. The surrogate-gradient curve climbs steeply and consistently, which means direct gradient-based training is quickly organizing spikes into useful task patterns. The converted SNN improves more moderately because much of its structure comes from a pre-trained ANN rather than from spike-native timing optimization. The STDP-based curve rises more slowly and tends to flatten earlier, showing a learning process that adapts locally and gradually.

ANN-to-SNN conversion starts from a trained artificial neural network and turns it into a spiking version. The motivation is practical: ANNs already have powerful training methods, mature architectures, and strong accuracy. Instead of training spikes from the beginning, conversion first trains a conventional network with continuous activations, then maps those activations into spike rates. A high activation becomes a high firing rate; a low activation becomes a low firing rate. The SNN then approximates the ANN's computations by counting spikes over a simulation window.

A rate code represents information through how many spikes occur in a time interval. If one neuron emits 80 spikes in a window and another emits 10, the first represents a stronger activation. This makes conversion intuitive because ANN activation magnitudes can be translated into spike counts. The cost is time and activity. To estimate a continuous value reliably from spikes, the network often needs a longer simulation window and many spikes. That is why converted SNNs can achieve competitive performance while producing relatively high spike counts, around 20,000 spikes per inference in the reported comparison, and latency around 20 ms when longer windows are needed.

A concrete interpretation helps. Suppose an ANN layer produces an activation value of 0.8 for an edge detector. A converted SNN may represent that value by making the corresponding spiking neuron fire frequently during the inference window. If the window is too short, the spike count may give a noisy estimate of 0.8. If the window is longer, the estimate improves, but latency and spike count increase. Conversion therefore borrows the accuracy advantages of ANN training while paying for them with extra temporal sampling.

STDP takes a more local and biologically inspired route. Spike-timing dependent plasticity changes a connection according to the relative timing of spikes between two neurons. If a presynaptic neuron fires shortly before a postsynaptic neuron, the connection can strengthen, because the earlier spike may have helped cause the later spike. If the timing relationship is less useful, the connection can weaken. The learning signal is local: each synapse adjusts from nearby spike timing rather than from a global error gradient.

The phrase "plasticity" means the connection can change. In STDP, timing carries the teaching signal. A useful mental example is a motion sensor learning repeated event order. If neuron A often fires just before neuron B when a hand moves across a sensor, the A-to-B connection becomes stronger. Over time, the network becomes sensitive to that temporal pattern. This makes STDP attractive for unsupervised and ultra-low-power settings, because learning can proceed from event timing without dense error computations. It also helps explain the energy profile: STDP-based SNNs can reach very sparse activity, around 4,000 spikes per inference, and low energy use, around 5 mJ per inference.

The tradeoff is learning speed and accuracy. Because STDP uses local timing rules, it tends to converge more slowly and may plateau earlier than supervised gradient training. "Convergence" means the training process is settling: the loss stops dropping quickly, or accuracy stops rising quickly. A training loss value measures error, so a lower loss usually means better fit to the training task. Over 20 epochs, surrogate-gradient training shows the steepest and most consistent loss decline, converted SNNs show moderate convergence, and STDP-based SNNs converge more slowly with an earlier plateau. That pattern matches the underlying mechanisms: global gradient information can coordinate many layers toward the task objective, conversion inherits an ANN solution and then approximates it with spikes, and STDP lets local spike timing sculpt the network more gradually.

**Question.** Why does surrogate gradient descent use a smooth substitute if the network is supposed to communicate with spikes?

**Answer.** The smooth substitute is used during learning so the training algorithm can estimate how changing a weight would affect the error near a firing threshold. The network's forward behavior still uses discrete spike events. The substitute supplies a usable gradient for weight updates, which helps the SNN learn accurate spike patterns directly.

Energy, latency, spike count, accuracy, and convergence are tied together because they all reflect how information moves through time. An ANN uses continuous-valued operations and can reach strong raw accuracy, but it consumes more energy in the reported comparison, around 200 mJ per inference. A converted SNN reduces energy substantially, around 20 mJ, by using spikes, yet its rate-coded representation can require many spikes. A surrogate-gradient SNN pushes the balance further, around 15 mJ and about 12,000 spikes, because training shapes spike patterns more directly. An STDP-based SNN uses the sparsest activity and lowest energy, while accepting weaker accuracy or slower adaptation in demanding supervised settings.

**Question.** If STDP uses the fewest spikes and lowest energy, why use surrogate gradients or conversion at all?

**Answer.** A training paradigm must match the goal. STDP is well suited to ultra-low-power and unsupervised settings where sparse local adaptation is valuable. Surrogate gradients are better when the task needs high accuracy and low latency together. Conversion is useful when a strong ANN solution already exists and the goal is to obtain a spiking version with competitive performance.

The mental model is to see the three paradigms as three ways of placing the burden of learning. Surrogate gradient descent learns spike behavior directly by giving the sharp firing event a trainable slope during optimization. ANN-to-SNN conversion learns first in the continuous world, then translates activation strength into spike rates over time. STDP learns from the timing relationship between neighboring spikes, allowing sparse local adaptation. Once those mechanisms are clear, the later tradeoffs become predictable: direct gradient training tends to converge quickly and preserve accuracy, conversion tends to inherit ANN strengths while needing more spikes and time, and STDP tends to minimize activity and energy by letting timing itself shape the network.
