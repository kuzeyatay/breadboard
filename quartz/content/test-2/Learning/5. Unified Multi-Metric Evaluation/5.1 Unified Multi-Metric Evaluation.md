## Unified Multi-Metric Evaluation

Imagine choosing a spiking neural network for a small robot that must recognize hand gestures while running on a battery. If you look only at classification accuracy, the choice seems simple: pick the model with the highest percentage of correct answers. But the robot also needs to react quickly, avoid draining its battery, and keep its neural activity sparse enough for neuromorphic hardware to use efficiently. A model that is slightly more accurate but takes too long to respond may fail in a real-time setting. A model that reaches good accuracy by producing too many spikes may lose the energy advantage that made spiking computation attractive in the first place.

A unified multi-metric evaluation exists to make that choice fair. It treats a spiking neural network as a system with several connected costs and benefits, rather than as a classifier with a single score. The same comparison lens is applied across different SNN training approaches, including surrogate-trained SNNs, converted SNNs, and STDP-based SNNs. A surrogate-trained SNN learns with a differentiable approximation that helps gradient-based optimization work despite the discrete spike events. A converted SNN begins from an artificial neural network and translates its activations into spiking behavior. An STDP SNN uses spike-timing-dependent plasticity, a learning rule where the timing between pre-synaptic and post-synaptic spikes changes connection strength. These methods can look strong or weak depending on which metric is chosen, so a shared protocol keeps the comparison from favoring one method by accident.

The evaluation can be pictured as a five-part measurement vector:
$$
M = (A, L, E, S, C)
$$
Here, $M$ is the full performance profile of a model. $A$ is accuracy, the fraction or percentage of examples classified correctly. $L$ is latency, the time needed to produce an inference. $E$ is energy consumption, the computational energy required to run the model. $S$ is spike count, the amount of spiking activity generated during processing. $C$ is convergence behavior, meaning how the model improves during training over time, such as across 20 epochs. This formula does not combine the metrics into one magic number. It gives a compact way to remember that a deployment decision needs the whole profile.

Accuracy answers the most familiar question: "Does the model get the right answer?" If an SNN recognizes handwritten digits or image categories, accuracy measures how often its prediction matches the label. Accuracy becomes especially revealing when the task grows in scale. On simpler MNIST-scale tasks, different approaches can appear close enough that accuracy alone gives a reassuring impression. On larger CIFAR-10-scale tasks, the gap can widen: artificial neural networks may reach about 92% accuracy while direct SNN approaches can drop closer to 85%. That difference affects model selection, because a system meant for image-rich environments may require stronger recognition performance than a system handling simpler sensory patterns.

Latency answers a different question: "How long does the model need before it can act?" In a spiking system, information unfolds through events over time, so the time window used for inference becomes part of the cost. A gesture-recognition system on event-based vision data, such as DVS128, shows why latency belongs beside accuracy. Surrogate-trained SNNs can achieve inference latencies around 5-10 ms, while ANN counterparts can take around 20-50 ms. For a robot arm, drone, or edge device responding to motion, that difference changes the feel of the system. A 5-10 ms response supports real-time interaction; a 20-50 ms response can make the same intelligence feel delayed.

Energy consumption answers: "How expensive is each inference for the hardware?" Spiking networks are attractive because silence can save work. When neurons communicate through sparse spike events, hardware can avoid repeated dense numerical operations during quiet periods. In the reported comparisons, surrogate-trained SNNs can operate at less than 10% of ANN energy costs. That order-of-magnitude reduction is central for edge AI, where devices may run far from large power supplies and cooling systems. Energy also becomes more valuable as model scale increases, because every repeated computation is multiplied across more neurons, layers, examples, and time steps.

Spike count gives a more internal view of that energy story. A spike is a discrete event sent by a neuron, and each event can trigger communication and computation. A low spike count means the model is using fewer events to reach its decision. A high spike count means more activity is moving through the system. Since neuromorphic hardware is designed to benefit from sparse event-driven processing, spike count helps explain why one SNN may be more hardware-friendly than another even when their accuracies are similar. Two models may both classify well, but the one that reaches its answer with fewer spikes better matches the promise of efficient spiking computation.

Convergence completes the picture by asking: "How does the model learn over training?" A model that eventually performs well after long or unstable training has a different practical cost from one that improves quickly and reliably. Tracking convergence across 20 epochs shows how different learning rules behave during optimization. Surrogate gradient training can converge faster when its parameters are tuned well, often reaching useful performance with fewer spikes. STDP-based learning, by contrast, is valuable in ultra-low-power unsupervised settings, where the learning process follows local timing relationships rather than relying on full supervised gradient pipelines.

**Question.** Why can accuracy-only reporting make a spiking neural network look better than it will be in practice?

**Answer.** Accuracy-only reporting hides the time and energy costs of reaching that accuracy. A model may classify many examples correctly while requiring high latency, high energy, or excessive spiking activity. In a battery-powered or real-time device, those hidden costs can make the model a poor deployment choice even if its accuracy is strong.

The need for unified evaluation grows from fragmentation in SNN research. Different studies may use different spike encodings, such as rate coding or temporal coding. Rate coding represents information through how often spikes occur over a time window. Temporal coding represents information through when spikes occur. Different studies may also use different neuron models, such as leaky integrate-and-fire neurons or Izhikevich neurons. When encoding choices, neuron dynamics, hardware platforms, and reported metrics vary together, results become hard to compare. A unified protocol reduces that confusion by asking each model to stand under the same measurement lights.

A useful way to think about the five metrics is as a set of tradeoff dials. Turning one dial can move another. Increasing the inference window may improve accuracy because the network has more time to accumulate evidence, but it can also increase latency and spike count. Reducing spikes can lower energy, but if the model becomes too quiet, accuracy may suffer. Optimizing surrogate training can improve convergence and reduce spike activity, which helps both learning speed and efficiency. The evaluation is "unified" because these effects are read together instead of being separated into isolated claims.

Energy-delay product makes this connection even more explicit:
$$
EDP = E \times L
$$
Here, $EDP$ is the energy-delay product, $E$ is the energy consumed by an inference, and $L$ is the latency of that inference. The product rewards systems that are both energy-efficient and fast. A model with very low energy but slow response can still have a large $EDP$. A model with fast response but high energy can also have a large $EDP$. This single combined quantity cannot replace the full five-metric profile, but it helps explain why energy and latency are often considered together in neuromorphic and edge settings.

**Question.** If an STDP model uses very little energy, why would anyone choose a surrogate-trained SNN instead?

**Answer.** The choice depends on the deployment goal. STDP is attractive for ultra-low-power unsupervised settings, especially when local timing-based learning is desirable. Surrogate-trained SNNs are often better suited when the target is low-latency accuracy, because their training can be optimized to converge faster and perform strongly while still using far less energy than comparable ANNs.

The mental model is a decision table rather than a race with one winner. Accuracy tells whether the model understands the task. Latency tells whether it can act in time. Energy tells whether it can run sustainably. Spike count tells whether it uses the event-driven advantage of spiking computation. Convergence tells whether the learning process is practical. A unified multi-metric evaluation turns SNN comparison into principled model selection: the best network is the one whose full profile matches the job it must perform.
