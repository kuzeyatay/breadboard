### Unified Multi-Metric Evaluation

Imagine a small robot moving through a hallway on battery power. Its camera sees walls, doors, and people; its controller must decide quickly whether to turn, stop, or keep moving. A conventional neural network can process each frame as a full grid of numbers, but that means the system keeps spending computation even when most of the scene barely changes. A spiking neural network uses a different assumption: information can arrive as timed events. When activity crosses a threshold, a neuron emits a spike, and the timing and number of those spikes become part of the computation.

That event-based style creates a new evaluation problem. If two spiking models both recognize an object correctly, the better choice for the robot may still depend on which one responds sooner, which one uses less energy, which one produces fewer spikes, and which one learns reliably during training. Accuracy alone tells whether the final answer is right. It does not tell whether the answer arrived in time for a moving robot, whether the battery can support thousands of inferences, or whether the training method settles into useful behavior within a practical number of epochs.

A unified multi-metric evaluation treats model quality as a set of linked measurements rather than a single score. The five measurements are accuracy, latency, energy per inference, spike count, and convergence. Accuracy is the fraction of predictions that match the correct label. Latency is the time needed to produce an inference, usually measured in milliseconds. Energy per inference is the amount of energy spent to make one prediction, often measured in millijoules. Spike count is the number of spike events produced during an inference. Convergence describes how quickly training reaches a target level of performance.

Each metric sees a different part of the same system. Accuracy measures decision quality. Latency measures speed. Energy measures deployment cost. Spike count measures event activity, which is closely tied to hardware workload because neuromorphic systems can often save power when neurons stay silent. Convergence measures training behavior, showing whether a learning rule reaches stable performance quickly or slowly. Looking at all five together gives a practical profile of a spiking model, much like evaluating a vehicle by range, speed, fuel use, cargo capacity, and reliability rather than by top speed alone.

The comparison becomes meaningful because the same evaluation lens can be applied to different ways of building spiking neural networks. A surrogate-gradient SNN is trained by using a smooth approximation to the spike function during learning. Spikes are discrete events, so their exact threshold behavior is difficult to optimize with ordinary gradient descent. The surrogate gradient gives the training algorithm a usable slope while preserving spike-based computation during operation. This approach tends to balance strong accuracy with low latency: results around 97.8% accuracy, about 10 ms latency, moderate energy near 15 mJ per inference, and moderate activity around 12,000 spikes per inference show a model that is fast and accurate while still using event-based computation.

A converted SNN begins from an artificial neural network and translates its learned activations into spiking behavior. This path can preserve competitive accuracy because the original network has already learned useful features. The cost appears in time and activity: converted models often need longer simulation windows and higher spike counts so their spike rates can represent the information that continuous activations carried directly. A useful analogy is replacing a steady water level with a sequence of droplets. The same amount of information can be conveyed, but the receiver may need to observe enough droplets over time before the message becomes clear.

An STDP-based SNN learns through spike-timing dependent plasticity, a local rule based on the relative timing of spikes. If one neuron repeatedly fires shortly before another, the connection between them can strengthen; if the timing pattern differs, the connection can weaken. This makes STDP attractive for unsupervised adaptation, where the system adjusts from local activity rather than relying on labeled examples and global error signals. Its profile leans toward efficiency: energy can be as low as about 5 mJ per inference, and spike activity can be very low. The tradeoff is lower classification accuracy, around 95.5% in the integrated comparison, and slower training behavior, with loss plateauing at higher values and needing more epochs to stabilize.

Convergence gives the evaluation a time axis during learning. Instead of asking only where training ends, convergence asks how quickly useful performance appears. A compact way to express this is to define convergence time as the earliest epoch when the model reaches a chosen target accuracy.

*Convergence time as minimum epoch reaching target accuracy* *(p. 6)*

The shape of this idea is simple: imagine plotting accuracy after each training epoch. Let `e` be an epoch number, let `A(e)` be the accuracy measured at that epoch, and let `A_target` be the target accuracy the model must reach. The convergence time is the smallest `e` for which `A(e)` is at least `A_target`. This lets you compare learning rules by asking, "How many training passes are needed before the model becomes good enough?" Surrogate-gradient training tends to reduce loss more rapidly and stabilize by around the twentieth epoch, which fits applications where fast learning and real-time performance are both valuable. STDP tends to stabilize more slowly, which fits situations where ultra-low power and unsupervised adaptation are more valuable than maximum supervised classification accuracy.

**Question.** If a model has high accuracy, why should latency and energy still be measured?

**Answer.** High accuracy means the model often chooses the correct label, but a deployed system also needs the label at the right time and within its power budget. A robot, wearable sensor, or edge device can fail practically even with accurate predictions if each inference arrives too late or drains too much energy. Latency and energy show whether the accuracy can be used in the environment where the model will actually run.

Spike count connects the computational story to the hardware story. In a spiking system, each spike is an event that may trigger communication and computation. Fewer spikes generally mean less activity, which can reduce energy use on event-driven hardware. Yet fewer spikes also mean fewer opportunities to transmit information, so a model must preserve enough spike activity to make reliable decisions. Surrogate-gradient SNNs sit in the middle with moderate spike counts and strong accuracy. STDP-based SNNs push toward minimal activity and low energy. Converted SNNs often spend more spikes and longer windows to preserve the accuracy of their artificial-network origin.

**Question.** Does the lowest-energy spiking model automatically become the best model?

**Answer.** The best model depends on the job. An STDP-based model with about 5 mJ per inference is attractive for ultra-low-power unsupervised settings, especially when adaptation and battery life dominate the design. A surrogate-gradient model is often better for low-latency accuracy targets because it can reach competitive accuracy with about 10 ms latency and faster convergence. Model choice follows the constraints of the application.

A unified evaluation turns spiking neural network design into a clear matching problem. Surrogate-gradient training offers a strong balance for real-time learning: high accuracy, short latency, moderate energy, moderate spikes, and fast convergence. ANN-to-SNN conversion offers competitive accuracy but often asks for more spike activity and longer simulation time. STDP offers the leanest energy profile and local adaptation while accepting slower convergence and lower supervised classification accuracy. The mental model is a control panel with five connected dials: turning one dial toward accuracy, speed, energy savings, sparse activity, or training stability shifts the others. A good spiking system is chosen by setting those dials for the world it must operate in.
