### Unified Multi-Metric Evaluation

Imagine choosing a small vision system for a drone that must recognize obstacles while flying through a narrow hallway. If the system is accurate but waits too long before responding, the drone may hit the wall. If it responds quickly but burns too much power, the battery drains before the mission ends. If it saves energy by producing very few signals but learns too slowly to become useful, the design still fails. A spiking neural network is meant for situations like this because it processes information through brief events called spikes, so silence can save computation and time can carry meaning. To judge whether that promise is real, the network has to be evaluated through several connected measurements at once.

A single accuracy number gives only one slice of the behavior. Accuracy tells us how often the model gives the correct answer, but a spiking system is also shaped by when it answers, how many spikes it uses, how much energy those spikes imply, and how steadily it improves during training. These measurements are linked. A model can often gain accuracy by waiting through more simulation steps, because it has more time to collect spikes before deciding. That extra time can increase latency, which is the delay between receiving an input and producing a usable output. More time can also allow more spikes, and more spikes usually mean more switching activity in neuromorphic hardware, which raises energy use. A fair evaluation therefore treats accuracy, latency, energy, spike count, and convergence as parts of one design space rather than separate scoreboards.

The need for a unified evaluation becomes clear once different kinds of spiking systems are placed side by side. A surrogate-trained SNN learns with an approximate gradient. The difficulty is that a spike is a sharp, all-or-nothing event, so ordinary gradient methods cannot pass smoothly through it. Surrogate training solves this by using a smooth substitute during learning while keeping spiking behavior during operation. This tends to support strong accuracy and fast convergence because the model receives useful training signals even though its neurons communicate with spikes. An ANN-to-SNN converted model starts from a conventional artificial neural network and maps its continuous activations into spiking activity. This can preserve some of the accuracy learned by the original network, but it may need enough time steps to represent those activations through spike rates. An STDP-based SNN learns through spike-timing-dependent plasticity, where the timing between a presynaptic spike and a postsynaptic spike changes the strength of the connection. If one neuron repeatedly fires just before another, that connection can be strengthened; if the timing goes the other way, it can be weakened. This gives STDP a biologically inspired and often energy-frugal learning rule, especially for unsupervised settings.

These methods answer different practical needs, so comparing them through only one measurement can produce a misleading choice. Suppose a surrogate-trained SNN reaches high accuracy in a small number of epochs. An epoch means one full pass through the training data. If its accuracy rises quickly during the first 20 epochs, its convergence behavior suggests that the training rule is efficient: the network is not merely good at the end, it becomes useful sooner. Now suppose an STDP model produces far fewer spikes and uses much less energy, but reaches lower accuracy on a supervised classification task. That result does not make the STDP design useless; it points toward a different deployment setting, such as an ultra-low-power sensor that must discover patterns without labels. The comparison becomes meaningful only when each model is viewed through the same set of metrics.

A simple way to think about energy in spiking systems is to connect it to events. In a dense conventional network, many numerical activations may be computed at every layer whether the input changed much or not. In a spiking network, computation is concentrated around spike events. A rough evaluation can treat total spiking activity as a proxy for energy demand:

\[
S_{\text{total}}=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_{i=1}^{N} s_i(t)
\]

Here, \(S_{\text{total}}\) is the total spike count over an inference window, \(T\) is the number of time steps observed, \(N\) is the number of neurons being counted, and \(s_i(t)\) is 1 if neuron \(i\) spikes at time \(t\) and 0 if it stays silent. This formula lets you compute how many spike events the network produced while processing an input. If two models reach similar accuracy but one has a much smaller \(S_{\text{total}}\), the lower-spike model is usually more attractive for energy-constrained neuromorphic or edge hardware.

A beginner may wonder why latency and spike count are both needed, since more time often means more spikes. They measure related but different things. Latency asks how long the system takes before it can decide. Spike count asks how much event activity the system spends while reaching that decision. A model could be fast but burst many spikes in a short window, or slower but sparse. For a robot avoiding an obstacle, the fast bursting model may be acceptable. For a battery-powered environmental sensor that wakes rarely and runs for months, the slower sparse model may be better.

**Question.** If a spiking model is less accurate than an ANN, why would anyone choose it?

**Answer.** A spiking model can still be the better choice when the deployment setting values energy efficiency, low latency, or event-driven sensing more than the last few percentage points of accuracy. On larger tasks, conventional ANNs may retain higher accuracy, such as around 92% on CIFAR-10 while direct SNNs can drop nearer to 85%. At the same time, surrogate-trained SNNs can operate at less than 10% of ANN energy costs. The practical decision depends on the whole tradeoff: a small loss in accuracy may be worth a large reduction in energy, especially on edge devices.

The same logic applies to real-time tasks. Event-based gesture recognition on neuromorphic platforms can use surrogate SNNs with inference latencies around 5-10 ms, while ANN counterparts may take around 20-50 ms. The numbers mean that the spiking system can turn sensory change into action sooner. In a live control loop, milliseconds are not decorative; they decide whether perception arrives in time to guide movement. A unified comparison makes that advantage visible because it reports latency beside accuracy instead of hiding response time behind a final score.

Convergence adds another layer because it describes how learning unfolds. If accuracy after training is the destination, convergence is the path. Tracking performance over 20 epochs shows whether a method improves quickly, slowly, or unstably. A training method that reaches a useful level early may reduce training cost and simplify model selection. Surrogate gradient methods often benefit here because their approximate gradients guide the network toward better spike-based behavior with fewer wasted updates. Spike count during this process also matters, because faster convergence with fewer spikes means the model is not only learning well but learning in a way that aligns with the efficiency goal of spiking computation.

**Question.** Why should all methods be tested under the same evaluation protocol?

**Answer.** Without a shared protocol, differences may come from inconsistent measurement rather than genuine model behavior. One study might report accuracy after many time steps, another might report energy on different hardware assumptions, and another might use a different spike encoding or neuron model. A unified protocol keeps the comparison focused: surrogate-trained, converted, and STDP-based SNNs can be judged across the same accuracy, latency, energy, spike count, and convergence measurements, so the tradeoffs become visible rather than accidental.

The mental model is a five-dial control panel. Accuracy says how often the answer is right. Latency says how soon the answer arrives. Energy says how costly the computation is. Spike count reveals how much event traffic the network creates. Convergence shows how efficiently the model learns over training. Turning one dial often moves another, so the goal is not to crown one method in isolation. The goal is to match a spiking design to a real constraint: surrogate-trained SNNs when low-latency accuracy is central, converted SNNs when ANN performance needs to be carried into a spiking format, and STDP-style learning when local, sparse, ultra-low-power adaptation is the main requirement.
