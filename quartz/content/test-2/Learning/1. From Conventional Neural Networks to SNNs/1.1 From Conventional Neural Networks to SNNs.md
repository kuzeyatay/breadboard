Imagine a small camera on a battery-powered drone watching a hallway. For long stretches, the hallway barely changes: the same walls, the same floor, the same lighting. A conventional artificial neural network still processes the image as a dense grid of numbers each time it makes a decision. Each layer multiplies many input values by many learned weights, adds the results, and passes continuous activation values onward. That style of computation is powerful, but it asks the hardware to keep moving and updating large arrays of numbers even when most of the scene carries no new information.

A spiking neural network begins from a different assumption: silence can carry meaning. Instead of sending a continuous value at every step, a neuron sends a brief event called a spike only when its internal state reaches a firing threshold. The spike is discrete, like a click, and its timing becomes part of the message. If the drone's camera sees nothing change, many neurons can remain quiet. If an edge moves, a few neurons can fire at precise moments. Computation then becomes event-driven: activity happens when information arrives, rather than on every full synchronous pass through the network.

This shift matters most when energy is scarce. A conventional ANN or CNN usually depends on continuous-valued operations across many layers, especially matrix multiplications. A matrix multiplication is the repeated pattern of multiplying inputs by weights and summing them to produce activations. When this happens densely, many operations occur for every inference, where an inference means one completed prediction or decision. In an SNN, inference can be carried by spike events accumulated over time. Fewer spikes usually mean fewer operations, and fewer operations usually mean less energy per inference.

A helpful way to compare these systems is to ask how much accuracy is obtained for each unit of energy spent. The normalized energy efficiency metric expresses that idea directly:

*Normalized energy efficiency as accuracy over energy consumption* *(p. 6)*

The formula reads as a ratio: normalized energy efficiency equals accuracy divided by energy consumption. Here, accuracy is the fraction or percentage of correct predictions, and energy consumption is the energy used per inference, commonly measured in millijoules. The ratio lets you compare models that may have similar accuracy but very different power costs. For example, a model with 98% accuracy that uses 200 mJ per inference is accurate, but each correct decision is expensive. A model with slightly lower accuracy that uses only 15 mJ may deliver far more useful prediction per millijoule, especially on a robot, sensor, wearable device, or edge processor.

The energy numbers make the intuition concrete. A conventional ANN/CNN can consume around 200 mJ per inference because it relies on continuous-valued dense computation. A converted SNN can reduce that to about 20 mJ, roughly a tenfold drop, while still preserving much of the original ANN behavior. A surrogate-gradient-trained SNN can use about 15 mJ, and an STDP-based SNN can reach about 5 mJ per inference. The pattern is easy to interpret: as computation becomes more sparse and more event-driven, energy falls sharply. The tradeoff is that different SNN training methods pay for this efficiency in different ways.

A converted SNN starts with a trained conventional ANN and translates its activations into spike-based behavior. This makes conversion attractive because it can inherit the strong accuracy of the original model. The cost is spike activity: converted SNNs may require around 20,000 spikes per inference because they often use rate coding. Rate coding represents a value by how frequently spikes occur over a time window. A high activation becomes many spikes; a low activation becomes fewer spikes. This is intuitive, like representing loudness by the number of claps per second, but it can require longer simulation windows and more total spikes.

A surrogate-gradient SNN is trained more directly as a spiking system. The difficulty is that a spike is a sharp event: below threshold there is no spike, and above threshold there is one. Ordinary gradient descent prefers smooth changes, so surrogate gradient training uses a smooth approximation during learning while keeping spike-like behavior for the network. This lets the model learn efficient spiking patterns and often reach accuracy close to ANN performance, within about 1-2%, while keeping spike counts lower than converted SNNs. Around 12,000 spikes per inference and about 15 mJ can be enough for strong performance, making this approach useful when low latency and high accuracy both matter.

Latency is the time a model needs to produce a usable answer. In SNNs, latency is tied to how long the network must observe and accumulate spikes before the output is reliable. A model that needs a long spike window may save energy compared with an ANN but respond more slowly. A model that learns precise spike timing can answer faster. Some surrogate-gradient SNNs can reach latencies as low as about 10 milliseconds, which is fast enough for real-time settings such as robotics or responsive edge intelligence.

Training speed is another part of the tradeoff. A model may eventually become accurate, but the number of training epochs it needs still affects practical usefulness. An epoch is one full pass through the training data. Convergence time names the point at which the model first reaches a chosen target accuracy:

*Convergence time as epoch reaching target accuracy* *(p. 6)*

This expression defines convergence time as the epoch at which accuracy reaches the target accuracy. The symbol for the epoch identifies a training pass, and the target accuracy is the performance level chosen as the goal. The formula helps separate two models that end near the same final accuracy: one may become useful quickly, while another may need many more passes through the data. Surrogate-gradient SNNs can converge quickly, reaching strong performance by around the 20th epoch in the reported comparison. STDP-based SNNs often converge more slowly, but they can produce the sparsest activity and the lowest energy use.

STDP, or spike-timing dependent plasticity, learns from the relative timing of spikes. If one neuron tends to fire just before another, the connection between them can strengthen; if the timing relationship is weaker or reversed, the connection can weaken. This resembles a local learning rule: each connection changes based on nearby spike timing rather than a global error signal alone. The result can be extremely sparse activity, around 4,000 spikes per inference, and very low energy, around 5 mJ. That makes STDP especially appealing for ultra-low-power or unsupervised settings, where a system must adapt with limited supervision and limited energy.

**Question.** If SNNs use fewer operations, why do they sometimes have slightly lower accuracy than ANNs?

**Answer.** Accuracy can drop because an SNN represents information through discrete spike events over time rather than continuously updated numerical activations. A conventional ANN can carry fine-grained values through every layer at every step. An SNN compresses activity into spike timing and spike counts, so the model must learn when to fire, how often to fire, and how long to observe spikes before deciding. Surrogate-gradient training can reduce the gap to about 1-2%, while STDP may trade more accuracy for much lower energy.

A beginner confusion often appears around "spikes" sounding like binary on/off signals that are too simple for learning. A single spike is indeed a discrete event, but a population of spiking neurons can represent rich information through which neurons fire, how many spikes occur, and when those spikes arrive. Think of a drum ensemble: one hit is simple, but many players hitting at different times can create complex rhythm. SNNs use that temporal structure to carry information while avoiding unnecessary continuous updates.

**Question.** Why compare accuracy, energy, latency, spike count, and convergence together instead of choosing the most accurate model?

**Answer.** A model used on a large plugged-in server and a model used on a tiny edge device face different constraints. Accuracy tells how often predictions are correct, energy tells how costly each prediction is, latency tells how quickly the answer arrives, spike count reflects how much event activity the network produces, and convergence shows how quickly training becomes useful. A model with the highest accuracy may be a poor fit for a battery-powered real-time system, while a slightly less accurate SNN may deliver enough correctness with far lower energy and faster event-driven response.

The mental model is a shift from dense numerical broadcasting to sparse temporal signaling. Conventional neural networks push continuous values through many synchronized operations, which supports high accuracy but can consume substantial energy. Spiking neural networks let neurons stay quiet until their activity is needed, then communicate through precisely timed events. Converted SNNs preserve much of ANN behavior, surrogate-gradient SNNs learn efficient spike patterns with strong accuracy and low latency, and STDP-based SNNs push sparsity and energy savings even further. The guiding question becomes: how much useful prediction can the system produce for each spike, each millijoule, and each millisecond?
