## Sparse Surrogate-Gradient Computation

Once a spike function has been given a surrogate derivative, learning can pass an approximate gradient through each neuron's threshold. A dense backward pass would evaluate that surrogate for every neuron at every simulated time step. Spiking activity is usually sparse, so many of those evaluations concern neurons whose membrane voltages lie far from the firing threshold and whose local surrogate derivatives contribute little or nothing. Sparse surrogate-gradient descent uses this structure to concentrate backward computation on the neurons that can affect the learning signal.

Consider neuron $i$ at time step $t$. Let $u_i[t]$ be its membrane voltage and let $\vartheta$ be its firing threshold. The forward spike can be written as
$$
S_i[t] = H\!\left(u_i[t]-\vartheta\right)
$$
Where $s_i[t]$ is the emitted spike and $H$ is a step function. The neuron emits a spike when its voltage reaches the threshold. During the backward pass, the surrogate-gradient method replaces the unavailable ordinary derivative of $H$ with a chosen approximation. Write that approximation as
$$
\frac{\partial s_i[t]}{\partial u_i[t]}
\approx
\widetilde{\sigma}'\!\left(u_i[t]-\vartheta\right)
$$
Where $\widetilde{\sigma}'$ is the surrogate derivative and $u_i[t]-\vartheta$ is the neuron's signed distance from threshold. A positive distance means the voltage has reached or crossed the threshold; a negative distance means it remains below threshold.

A sparse backward pass adds an activity gate to this derivative. Let $a_i[t]$ indicate whether neuron $i$ is active for gradient computation at time $t$. The gated surrogate derivative is
$$
\widehat{\frac{\partial s_i[t]}{\partial u_i[t]}}
=
\begin{cases}
\widetilde{\sigma}'\!\left(u_i[t]-\vartheta\right), & a_i[t]=1,\\[4pt]
0, & a_i[t]=0.
\end{cases}
$$
Here, $\widehat{\partial s_i[t]/\partial u_i[t]}$ is the derivative actually used during learning. An active neuron receives the usual surrogate derivative. An inactive neuron receives zero, so gradient propagation through that local spike operation can be skipped. This piecewise rule turns neuronal activity into a computational decision: retain the backward calculation where it can matter and omit it elsewhere.

Activity can include neurons that emitted spikes, but spike emission alone gives too narrow a view of relevance. A neuron sitting just below threshold may have produced no spike at the current step, yet a small change in its input or parameters could move it across the threshold. Its membrane voltage therefore places it in a sensitive region. Threshold proximity captures this sensitivity by measuring the magnitude of the distance
$$
D_i[t]=\left|u_i[t]-\vartheta\right|
$$
Where $d_i[t]$ is the absolute distance between the membrane voltage and the firing threshold. Choose a proximity width $\Delta>0$. Neuronal activity for the backward pass can then be defined by
$$
A_i[t]
=
\begin{cases}
1, & \left|u_i[t]-\vartheta\right|\leq \Delta,\\[4pt]
0, & \left|u_i[t]-\vartheta\right|>\Delta.
\end{cases}
$$
The parameter $\Delta$ sets the size of the voltage region treated as relevant. A larger $\Delta$ admits more neurons into the backward pass. A smaller $\Delta$ retains only neurons very close to firing. Combining this condition with the gated derivative gives
$$
\widehat{\frac{\partial s_i[t]}{\partial u_i[t]}}
=
\begin{cases}
\widetilde{\sigma}'\!\left(u_i[t]-\vartheta\right),
& \left|u_i[t]-\vartheta\right|\leq \Delta,\\[4pt]
0,
& \left|u_i[t]-\vartheta\right|>\Delta.
\end{cases}
$$
This formula computes a sparse surrogate gradient directly from threshold proximity. The same band around the threshold identifies the neurons included in backward propagation and sets every other local spike derivative to zero.

For a concrete interpretation, suppose the threshold is $\vartheta=1.0$ and the proximity width is $\Delta=0.1$. Three neurons have voltages $0.94$, $0.40$, and $1.03$. Their threshold distances are $0.06$, $0.60$, and $0.03$, respectively. The neurons at $0.94$ and $1.03$ lie inside the interval from $0.9$ to $1.1$, so both use their surrogate derivatives. The neuron at $0.40$ lies far outside that interval, so its gated derivative is zero. One included neuron is below threshold and the other is above it; proximity, rather than the present spike value by itself, determines their relevance.

The resulting backward-pass sparsity can be expressed as a count. For $N$ neurons observed over $T$ time steps, a dense calculation considers $NT$ neuron-time pairs. The sparse calculation considers
$$
K=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_{i=1}^{N} a_i[t]
$$
Where $K$ is the number of active neuron-time pairs. The active fraction is
$$
\rho_{\mathrm{back}}=\frac{K}{NT}
$$
If $100$ neurons are simulated for $20$ steps, there are $2{,}000$ possible local surrogate-gradient evaluations. If $160$ neuron-time pairs satisfy the activity condition, then $\rho_{\mathrm{back}}=160/2000=0.08$. The backward pass retains local spike derivatives for $8\%$ of the possible pairs. This fraction describes sparsity in the gradient computation itself; the total cost of training also includes other operations and data movement.

**Question.** Why include a neuron that stayed below threshold?

**Answer.** A voltage just below $\vartheta$ lies near the spike boundary. A small parameter update could change whether that neuron fires, so its surrogate derivative can carry a useful learning signal. The threshold-proximity condition preserves such neurons even when their current spike value is zero.

**Question.** How should the proximity width $\Delta$ be understood?

**Answer.** It is a computational selectivity parameter. Increasing $\Delta$ keeps a wider voltage band and produces a denser backward pass. Decreasing $\Delta$ keeps fewer neuron-time pairs and produces a sparser approximation. Its role is to decide how close to the threshold a neuron must be before its local surrogate gradient is retained.

The full mental model is a narrow learning corridor around the firing threshold. Forward dynamics still determine each membrane voltage and spike. During backward propagation, the activity condition asks whether each voltage lies inside that corridor. Neurons inside it pass their surrogate gradients onward; neurons outside it contribute zeros at the local spike derivative. Sparse surrogate-gradient descent therefore links the geometry of threshold proximity to the computational structure of learning: sensitivity is concentrated near the spike boundary, and the backward pass follows that concentration.
