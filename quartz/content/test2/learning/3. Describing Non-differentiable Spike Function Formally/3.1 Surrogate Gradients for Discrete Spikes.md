## Surrogate Gradients for Discrete Spikes

You already know how backpropagation through time carries information backward across the time steps of a spiking neural network. Now imagine that this backward-moving signal reaches a neuron whose membrane potential sits just below its firing threshold. A tiny increase in an earlier weight could push the potential across the threshold and create a spike, while a tiny decrease would leave the neuron silent. The neuron therefore makes a discrete decision: it emits either a spike or no spike. That decision is useful during forward computation, yet its abruptness blocks the ordinary derivative needed to update the weight.

<!-- learning-unit:U9:after-introduction -->

Let $u_t$ denote the neuron's membrane potential at time $t$, and let $\vartheta$ denote its firing threshold. The spike $s_t$ can be written using a step function:
$$
S_t = H(u_t-\vartheta)
=
\begin{cases}
1, & u_t \geq \vartheta,\\
0, & u_t < \vartheta.
\end{cases}
$$
Here, $s_t$ is the spike produced at time $t$, $u_t$ is the membrane potential, $\vartheta$ is the firing threshold, and $H$ is the threshold or Heaviside step function. This formula lets us compute whether the neuron fires: subtract the threshold from the membrane potential, then check which side of zero the result lies on.

Suppose $\vartheta=1.0$. A potential of $u_t=0.99$ produces $s_t=0$, while $u_t=1.01$ produces $s_t=1$. The potential changes by only $0.02$, but the spike changes by a full unit. Between such threshold crossings, small changes in $u_t$ leave $s_t$ unchanged. The derivative is therefore
$$
\frac{\partial s_t}{\partial u_t}=0
\qquad \text{for } u_t\neq\vartheta
$$
While the derivative at $u_t=\vartheta$ is undefined because the spike function jumps there. This is spike nondifferentiability: the forward rule contains an abrupt threshold, so ordinary differentiation supplies zero almost everywhere and no finite derivative at the jump.

A weight earlier in the network influences the loss through this spike. If $w$ is such a weight and $L$ is the training loss, the chain rule needs a factor of the form
$$
\frac{\partial L}{\partial w}
=
\frac{\partial L}{\partial s_t}
\frac{\partial s_t}{\partial u_t}
\frac{\partial u_t}{\partial w}
$$
The symbol $L$ denotes the quantity training seeks to reduce, $w$ is the weight being adjusted, and each derivative measures how a small change passes through one link in the computational chain. The middle factor, $\partial s_t/\partial u_t$, creates the difficulty. When it equals zero, the product becomes zero even if changing the membrane potential enough to cross the threshold would alter the loss substantially.

A surrogate gradient supplies a useful backward signal in place of that unavailable or unhelpful threshold derivative. The neuron continues to use the discrete step function during its forward pass. During the backward pass, training substitutes a simple derivative that is active near the threshold. A piecewise Differential Spike surrogate can be written as
$$
\frac{\partial s_t}{\partial u_t}
\;\approx\;
G(u_t)
=
\begin{cases}
\alpha, & |u_t-\vartheta|\leq \delta,\\
0, & |u_t-\vartheta|>\delta.
\end{cases}
$$
Here, $g(u_t)$ is the surrogate derivative, $\alpha$ is the gradient magnitude inside the active region, and $\delta$ is the distance on either side of the threshold over which the surrogate remains active. The expression $|u_t-\vartheta|$ measures how far the membrane potential lies from the firing threshold. This formula lets training assign credit to potentials close enough to the threshold that a modest parameter change could alter the spike decision.

For example, choose $\vartheta=1.0$, $\delta=0.1$, and $\alpha=1$. A neuron with $u_t=0.96$ remains silent in the forward pass because its potential is below threshold, yet its surrogate derivative equals $1$ because it lies within the interval from $0.9$ to $1.1$. A neuron with $u_t=0.40$ has surrogate derivative $0$, reflecting that it is far from firing. The backward pass therefore concentrates learning around neurons whose spike decisions are sensitive to feasible changes in their inputs or weights.

The stepped curve in the figure represents what happens when discrete spike patterns determine the loss. As a parameter moves through a region without changing any spike, the loss stays on the same plateau. Crossing a boundary changes one or more spikes, producing a sudden jump to another level. A derivative measured along a flat plateau gives no direction toward a better step. The surrogate supplies the sloped optimization landscape shown alongside it, turning proximity to spike boundaries into a usable descent direction. Training can then move parameters toward lower-loss regions even while every forward spike remains a discrete event.

The smoothing belongs to optimization rather than to the neuron's forward behavior. Forward computation evaluates $H(u_t-\vartheta)$ and produces $s_t\in\{0,1\}$. Backward computation evaluates $g(u_t)$ and uses that value inside the chain rule. Keeping these two roles separate gives a precise mental picture: the threshold defines the model's events, while the surrogate derivative guides adjustments to the parameters that produce those events.

**Question.** If the true derivative is zero for almost every membrane potential, why can the surrogate use a positive value near the threshold?

**Answer.** The surrogate represents local sensitivity useful for learning. A potential near the threshold can change the neuron's spike after a small weight adjustment, so assigning it a positive backward signal helps the optimizer discover that adjustment. The value is an optimization approximation chosen for credit assignment; the forward spike still comes from the threshold rule.

The width $\delta$ controls how broadly training treats neurons as sensitive to the threshold. A narrow region sends gradients through only those membrane potentials extremely close to firing. A wider region sends gradients through a larger neighborhood, creating a smoother optimization landscape but giving similar backward treatment to potentials at different distances from the threshold. The scale $\alpha$ controls the strength of the signal passed backward. Together, these parameters shape how easily gradient information travels through threshold events.

**Question.** Does surrogate-gradient training turn a spiking neuron into a continuously valued neuron?

**Answer.** Forward computation remains binary: each time step produces either $0$ or $1$. Continuous values appear in the backward signal, where they express how strongly a near-threshold state should influence learning. The discrete event representation and the smooth optimization signal serve different stages of training.

The complete mental model is a two-path view of the same neuron. Along the forward path, membrane potential accumulates until the threshold converts it into a discrete spike. Along the backward path, the loss reaches that threshold through backpropagation through time and encounters a surrogate derivative that is strongest near the decision boundary. The resulting gradient tells earlier weights how to move membrane potentials toward spike patterns that reduce the loss. Discrete spikes define what the network does; surrogate gradients create a navigable landscape for learning how to do it.
