## Alternative Gradient and Spike Approximations

Once a spiking network has a loss function, training needs a way to answer a practical question: if a weight changes slightly, does the loss improve or worsen? A hard spike makes this question difficult near the membrane threshold. The neuron emits a spike when its membrane potential crosses the threshold, so a tiny weight change may leave the spike unchanged or suddenly flip it. A fixed surrogate derivative handles that jump by supplying a smooth local slope. Other approaches can instead estimate the loss change directly, reformulate the learning objective around information, or make the spike activation itself evolve from smooth to sharp during training.

Begin with the most literal strategy: try a small weight change and observe what happens to the loss. Suppose every trainable weight in the network is flattened into one vector
$$
\mathbf{w}=(w_1,w_2,\ldots,w_n)
$$
Where $n$ is the total number of weights. Let $L(\mathbf{w})$ denote the loss produced by running the network with those weights. To estimate how strongly the loss depends on weight $w_i$, perturb that coordinate in both directions:
$$
\frac{\partial L}{\partial w_i}
\approx
\frac{L(\mathbf{w}+\varepsilon \mathbf{e}_i)-L(\mathbf{w}-\varepsilon \mathbf{e}_i)}
{2\varepsilon}
$$
Here, $\varepsilon$ is a small positive perturbation size, and $\mathbf{e}_i$ is a vector containing a $1$ in position $i$ and $0$ everywhere else. The expression $L(\mathbf{w}+\varepsilon\mathbf{e}_i)$ is the loss after increasing only $w_i$, while $L(\mathbf{w}-\varepsilon\mathbf{e}_i)$ is the loss after decreasing it. Their difference measures how the loss responds across a small interval, and division by $2\varepsilon$ converts that response into an estimated slope.

For example, imagine that $\varepsilon=0.01$. Increasing one weight gives a loss of $0.42$, while decreasing it gives a loss of $0.46$. The estimate is
$$
\frac{0.42-0.46}{2(0.01)}=-2
$$
The negative value means that moving the weight upward tends to reduce the loss. A gradient-based update can therefore increase that weight. The spike function never had to provide a derivative: the estimate came from complete evaluations of the network on either side of the current weight.

Applying this coordinate by coordinate produces an estimated gradient vector,
$$
\widehat{\nabla L}(\mathbf{w})
=
\left(
\widehat{\frac{\partial L}{\partial w_1}},
\ldots,
\widehat{\frac{\partial L}{\partial w_n}}
\right)
$$
The estimate becomes more local as $\varepsilon$ becomes smaller, although an extremely small perturbation can make the measured loss difference difficult to resolve. The computational burden also grows with $n$, because many loss evaluations may be needed. Finite differences therefore provide a direct formal route around the spike discontinuity, with an explicit trade-off between the number of perturbations, the perturbation scale, and the quality of the estimated direction.

**Question.** If finite differences avoid differentiating the spike function, where does the learning signal come from?

**Answer.** It comes from changes in the final loss. A perturbation can alter membrane potentials, spike times, spike counts, and downstream activity. The two perturbed network runs compress all of those effects into two loss values. Comparing those values reveals whether the tested weight direction helps or harms the objective.

A different route begins by asking what useful spike activity should preserve. Let $S$ represent a neuron's spike activity and $U$ its membrane potential. Their statistical dependence can be expressed through mutual information:
$$
I(S;U)=H(S)-H(S\mid U)
$$
Here, $I(S;U)$ is the mutual information between spikes and membrane potentials, $H(S)$ is the entropy of the spike activity, and $H(S\mid U)$ is the uncertainty remaining about the spikes after the membrane potential is known. Maximizing $I(S;U)$ encourages membrane potentials to be informative about whether spikes occur. Equivalently, training may minimize
$$
L_{\mathrm{info}}=-I(S;U)
$$
Where $L_{\mathrm{info}}$ is an information-based loss. Lower values correspond to stronger statistical dependence between internal membrane states and emitted events.

The membrane threshold gives this objective a concrete geometric interpretation. For neuron $j$ at time $t$, define the threshold deviation
$$
D_{j,t}=u_{j,t}-\vartheta
$$
Where $u_{j,t}$ is the membrane potential and $\vartheta$ is the firing threshold. Positive deviations lie on the spiking side of the threshold, while negative deviations lie on the silent side. A smooth probability of spiking can be written as
$$
P_{j,t}=\sigma(\beta d_{j,t})
=\frac{1}{1+\exp(-\beta d_{j,t})}
$$
Where $p_{j,t}$ is the modeled spike probability, $\sigma$ is the logistic function, and $\beta>0$ controls sensitivity to threshold distance. When $u_{j,t}$ sits far above $\vartheta$, $p_{j,t}$ approaches $1$; far below it, $p_{j,t}$ approaches $0$. Near the threshold, the probability changes smoothly, allowing membrane-potential deviations to support an information-oriented loss.

For an observed binary spike $s_{j,t}\in\{0,1\}$, a corresponding loss is
$$
L_{\mathrm{spike}}
=
-\sum_{j,t}
\left[
S_{j,t}\log p_{j,t}
+
(1-s_{j,t})\log(1-p_{j,t})
\right]
$$
Every term rewards agreement between spike activity and the probability implied by the membrane potential. A spike paired with a strongly positive threshold deviation receives a small penalty. Silence paired with a strongly negative deviation also receives a small penalty. Ambiguous or inconsistent pairings receive larger penalties. The optimization signal therefore arises from how membrane potentials are arranged around the threshold.

The same smooth activation can become an evolving approximation to a discrete spike:
$$
\widetilde{s}_{j,t}^{(e)}
=
\sigma\!\left(\alpha_e\left(u_{j,t}-\vartheta\right)\right)
$$
Where $\widetilde{s}_{j,t}^{(e)}$ is the differentiable spike value at training epoch $e$, and $\alpha_e$ is its sharpness. A schedule such as
$$
\alpha_e=\alpha_0+\frac{e}{E}\left(\alpha_{\max}-\alpha_0\right)
$$
Moves the activation gradually from smooth to sharp. Here, $\alpha_0$ is the initial sharpness, $\alpha_{\max}$ is the final sharpness, and $E$ is the number of epochs over which the change occurs. Early training spreads useful gradients across a wider band of membrane potentials. Later training concentrates the transition more tightly around $\vartheta$, bringing the activation closer to a hard spike.

**Question.** Why let sharpness change during training?

**Answer.** A gentle transition gives many near-threshold membrane potentials a meaningful slope, which supports early weight adjustment. As the network organizes its membrane states, increasing sharpness makes spike values increasingly decisive. Training follows a path from an easy-to-optimize smooth system toward the discrete event rule the network ultimately needs.

These three approaches create three distinct mental models for learning through spikes. Finite differences treat the whole network as something that can be probed by controlled weight perturbations. Information maximization treats spikes as events that should remain meaningfully related to membrane states, especially their deviations from threshold. Evolving activation sharpness treats discreteness as a destination reached gradually during optimization. Each approach supplies a learning signal while respecting the threshold-centered structure that gives a spiking neuron its event-driven behavior.
