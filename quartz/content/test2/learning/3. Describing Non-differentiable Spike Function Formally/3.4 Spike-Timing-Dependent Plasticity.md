### Spike-Timing-Dependent Plasticity

Once spikes have precise times and synapses have adjustable weights, learning can connect the two. Imagine a presynaptic neuron sending a spike into a synapse, followed a few milliseconds later by a spike from the postsynaptic neuron. The sequence suggests that the incoming event helped drive the receiving neuron toward firing. The synapse can preserve that useful relationship by increasing its weight. If the order is reversed-if the postsynaptic neuron fires before the presynaptic spike arrives-the incoming event provides weaker evidence of having contributed to that output, so the synapse can decrease its weight. **Spike-timing-dependent plasticity**, usually shortened to **STDP**, turns this temporal ordering into a local learning rule.

A synaptic weight controls how strongly a presynaptic spike affects the postsynaptic neuron. Let $w$ denote that weight. Learning changes it by an amount $\Delta w$, producing an updated weight
$$
W_{\text{new}} = w_{\text{old}} + \Delta w
$$
Here, $w_{\text{old}}$ is the weight before the learning event, $\Delta w$ is the timing-dependent adjustment, and $w_{\text{new}}$ is the resulting weight. A positive $\Delta w$ strengthens the synapse, a process called **synaptic potentiation**. A negative $\Delta w$ weakens the synapse, a process called **synaptic depression**.

The direction of the update comes from the **relative spike time**. Let $t_{\text{pre}}$ be the time of the presynaptic spike and $t_{\text{post}}$ be the time of the postsynaptic spike. Define their difference as
$$
\Delta t = t_{\text{post}} - t_{\text{pre}}
$$
This sign convention gives the timing difference an immediate interpretation. When $\Delta t>0$, the presynaptic spike occurred first because $t_{\text{post}}$ is later than $t_{\text{pre}}$. When $\Delta t<0$, the postsynaptic spike occurred first. When the two spikes occur at nearly the same time, $|\Delta t|$ is small; when they are widely separated, $|\Delta t|$ is large.

A useful STDP rule should account for both ordering and temporal proximity. Ordering determines whether the synapse is strengthened or weakened. Proximity determines how large that change should be. Spikes separated by a short interval produce a large update because their temporal relationship is strong. Spikes separated by a long interval produce a small update because their relationship is weaker. Exponential decay expresses this fading influence:
$$
\Delta w =
\begin{cases}
A_{+}\exp\left(-\dfrac{\Delta t}{\tau_{+}}\right), & \Delta t>0,\\[8pt]
-A_{-}\exp\left(\dfrac{\Delta t}{\tau_{-}}\right), & \Delta t<0.
\end{cases}
$$
This piecewise formula selects one expression for each possible spike order. The quantity $\Delta w$ is the weight update, and $\Delta t=t_{\text{post}}-t_{\text{pre}}$ is the relative spike time. The constants $A_{+}>0$ and $A_{-}>0$ set the largest potentiation and depression magnitudes. The constants $\tau_{+}>0$ and $\tau_{-}>0$ are time scales that determine how quickly each effect fades as the spikes move apart. The exponential function $\exp(x)$, also written $e^x$, converts temporal distance into a smooth decay.

Consider the positive branch. If the presynaptic spike arrives shortly before the postsynaptic spike, then $\Delta t$ is a small positive number. The exponent $-\Delta t/\tau_{+}$ is close to zero, so the exponential is close to one and $\Delta w$ is close to $A_{+}$. As the postsynaptic spike occurs later and later, $\Delta t$ increases, the exponent becomes more negative, and the potentiation shrinks toward zero. The synapse therefore receives its strongest strengthening when the presynaptic event closely precedes the postsynaptic event.

For example, suppose $A_{+}=0.05$, $\tau_{+}=20\text{ ms}$, and the presynaptic spike occurs $10\text{ ms}$ before the postsynaptic spike. Then $\Delta t=10\text{ ms}$, giving
$$
\Delta w
=0.05\exp\left(-\frac{10}{20}\right)
=0.05e^{-0.5}
\approx 0.030
$$
A weight of $0.40$ would become approximately $0.43$. If the delay grew to $40\text{ ms}$, the update would be $0.05e^{-2}\approx0.0068$, so the same ordering would still cause potentiation, but much less of it.

Now consider the negative branch. If the postsynaptic spike occurs first, $\Delta t$ is negative. Dividing it by the positive time scale $\tau_{-}$ leaves a negative exponent, so the exponential remains between zero and one. The minus sign in front of $A_{-}$ makes the entire update negative. A small negative timing difference produces depression close to $-A_{-}$, while a large negative timing difference produces depression with a magnitude close to zero.

Suppose $A_{-}=0.04$, $\tau_{-}=20\text{ ms}$, and the postsynaptic neuron fires $10\text{ ms}$ before the presynaptic neuron. Then $\Delta t=-10\text{ ms}$, so
$$
\Delta w
=-0.04\exp\left(\frac{-10}{20}\right)
=-0.04e^{-0.5}
\approx -0.024
$$
A weight of $0.40$ would become approximately $0.376$. Reversing the spike order therefore reverses the direction of learning, while changing their separation adjusts its magnitude.

**Question.** Why does the depression branch use $\exp(\Delta t/\tau_{-})$ rather than $\exp(-\Delta t/\tau_{-})$?

**Answer.** On that branch, $\Delta t<0$. Therefore $\Delta t/\tau_{-}$ is already negative and the exponential decays as the separation grows. Using $-\Delta t/\tau_{-}$ would create a positive exponent, causing the magnitude to grow for spikes that are farther apart. The outer minus sign supplies the direction of depression; the negative exponent supplies the decay with temporal distance.

A common confusion comes from memorizing "positive means potentiation" without first checking how $\Delta t$ was defined. The sign follows the chosen subtraction order. With $\Delta t=t_{\text{post}}-t_{\text{pre}}$, a positive value means pre-before-post and selects potentiation. A definition using the reverse subtraction would require the branches to be reversed as well. Writing the definition beside the formula keeps the biological ordering and mathematical sign connected.

**Question.** What update occurs when the two spikes have exactly the same recorded time?

**Answer.** The two displayed branches cover $\Delta t>0$ and $\Delta t<0$, leaving $\Delta t=0$ to the model's chosen convention. A system may assign zero change, choose one limiting branch, or resolve the order using finer timing information. The convention should be stated explicitly because the update near zero has its largest magnitude.

STDP can therefore be pictured as a timing window centered on coincident spikes. Just to one side of the center, pre-before-post timing produces strong positive updates; just to the other side, post-before-pre timing produces strong negative updates. Both effects fade as temporal separation increases. Relative timing chooses the direction, the time constants shape the fading window, and the amplitude constants set the update scale. Together, these pieces let a synapse transform the temporal relationship between two spikes into a precise local change in connection strength.
