Building on membrane potential, firing threshold, and loss functions, imagine a neuron receiving many input patterns while producing nearly the same spike response every time. Its membrane potential may vary richly with the input, yet its output communicates little if it always spikes or always remains silent. Learning therefore needs a way to favor spike activity that changes meaningfully with the neuron's internal state. Information maximization supplies that signal by asking how strongly the observed spikes depend on the membrane potentials that produced them.

Let $U$ denote a neuron's membrane potential and $S$ denote its spike activity. For a single time step, $S$ can be represented as a binary variable: $S=1$ when the neuron emits a spike and $S=0$ when it remains silent. Both quantities vary across inputs or time, so they can be treated as random variables with probability distributions. The mutual information between them is
$$
I(S;U)
=
\sum_{s}\int p(s,u)
\log\!\left(
\frac{p(s,u)}{p(s)p(u)}
\right)\,du
$$
Here, $I(S;U)$ is the mutual information; $s$ is a possible spike state, usually $0$ or $1$; $u$ is a possible membrane-potential value; $p(s,u)$ is the joint probability that spike state $s$ and potential $u$ occur together; and $p(s)$ and $p(u)$ are their separate marginal probabilities. The ratio inside the logarithm compares the observed joint behavior with the behavior expected if spikes and potentials varied independently. The formula therefore computes how much knowing the membrane potential reduces uncertainty about spike activity, or equivalently how much observing the spike reveals about the potential.

Consider a neuron whose high membrane potentials are usually accompanied by spikes and whose low potentials are usually accompanied by silence. Particular values of $u$ then predict particular values of $s$, so $p(s,u)$ differs from the independent product $p(s)p(u)$, producing positive mutual information. If the spike output varies without any dependable relationship to membrane potential, the joint distribution approaches that product and the mutual information approaches zero.

An information-maximization objective seeks parameters that increase this dependence:
$$
\max_{\boldsymbol{\theta}} I(S;U)
$$
Where $\boldsymbol{\theta}$ denotes the trainable parameters that influence membrane potential and spike activity, such as synaptic weights. Optimization code is commonly organized around minimizing a loss, so the same goal can be written as
$$
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{info}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
=
- I(S;U)
$$
The negative sign converts maximization into minimization. A gradient-descent update then has the formal shape
$$
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{k+1}
=
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{k}
-
\eta
\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{info}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{k})
$$
Where $k$ is the optimization step, $\eta>0$ is the learning rate, and $\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{info}}$ is the gradient of the loss with respect to the parameters. This update changes the parameters in the direction that reduces the negative mutual information and therefore increases the statistical dependence between membrane potential and spike activity.

A concrete interpretation helps. Suppose two groups of input patterns produce potentials concentrated near $0.2$ and $1.2$, while the firing threshold is $1.0$. If low-potential trials are mostly silent and high-potential trials mostly spike, the output distinguishes the two groups. If every trial remains silent, observing $S=0$ reveals very little about whether the potential was near $0.2$ or $1.2$. The information objective favors parameters that preserve a dependable relationship between internal state and emitted events.

**Question.** Does high mutual information require the neuron to spike as often as possible?

**Answer.** High mutual information requires informative dependence, rather than a large spike count. A neuron that spikes on every input produces a nearly constant $S$, so its spikes reveal little about changing values of $U$. Useful activity generally includes both spikes and silence arranged in a way that reflects the membrane potential.

Computing or differentiating a full joint probability distribution can be difficult. A more direct learning signal can be built from the neuron's distance from its firing threshold. Let $u_t$ be the membrane potential at time $t$, let $\vartheta$ be the firing threshold, and define the threshold deviation
$$
D_t = u_t-\vartheta
$$
The value $d_t$ measures the signed distance between the potential and threshold. A positive deviation places the neuron above threshold, while a negative deviation places it below threshold. Its magnitude, $|d_t|$, measures how far the neuron lies from the decision boundary between spiking and silence.

Spike activity can be written as $s_t\in\{0,1\}$. A threshold-based neuron associates $s_t=1$ with the above-threshold side and $s_t=0$ with the below-threshold side. To express both cases through one signed target, define
$$
Y_t = 2s_t-1
$$
When $s_t=1$, $y_t=1$; when $s_t=0$, $y_t=-1$. The product $y_t d_t$ then measures whether the threshold deviation agrees with the observed spike state. It is positive when a spike accompanies an above-threshold potential or silence accompanies a below-threshold potential. It becomes small near the threshold, where spike status is most sensitive to a slight change in potential.

A threshold-deviation loss uses this relationship to turn membrane-potential distance into an optimization signal. In general form, it can be written as
$$
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{th}}
=
\frac{1}{T}
\sum_{t=1}^{T}
\ell\!\left(s_t,\,u_t-\vartheta\right)
$$
Where $T$ is the number of observed time steps and $\ell$ is a penalty that depends jointly on spike activity $s_t$ and threshold deviation $u_t-\vartheta$. The loss evaluates each spike in the context of the internal state that produced it. Its gradient can then adjust parameters that move membrane potentials toward deviations associated with more informative spike behavior.

For example, a spiking trial with $u_t$ barely above $\vartheta$ has a small positive deviation. A small parameter change could move that trial below threshold and change its output. A spiking trial far above threshold has a larger positive deviation and a more stable relationship between potential and spike state. Threshold deviation therefore provides a local measure of how firmly each event is connected to the neuron's membrane dynamics.

**Question.** Why use threshold deviation when spike activity already records whether the neuron fired?

**Answer.** The binary spike records the outcome, while threshold deviation records how the neuron reached that outcome. Two trials can both produce $s_t=1$, yet one potential may sit barely above threshold and the other far above it. Their deviations provide different learning signals even though their observed spike values match.

The resulting mental model connects probability, dynamics, and optimization. Membrane potentials carry continuously varying internal evidence, the threshold converts that evidence into discrete spike events, mutual information measures how dependably those events reflect the internal state, and threshold deviation supplies a practical state-sensitive quantity from which a loss can be constructed. Gradient descent then changes the parameters so that spikes and silence become increasingly informative consequences of the membrane potentials driving them.
