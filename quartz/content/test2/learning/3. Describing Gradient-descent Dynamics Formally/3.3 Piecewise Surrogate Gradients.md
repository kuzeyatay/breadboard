## Piecewise Surrogate Gradients

A firing threshold turns the membrane voltage from the previous unit into a spike: voltages below the threshold produce $0$, while voltages that reach or cross it produce $1$. Gradient-based training must carry information backward through this decision. The hard threshold creates the difficulty already established: its output stays constant across each side of the threshold and changes abruptly at the boundary. A useful training signal therefore has to express a simple intuition-voltages close to firing are sensitive to small changes, while voltages far from firing are comparatively insensitive.

Let $u$ be a neuron's membrane voltage and let $\vartheta$ be its firing threshold. The forward spike can be written as
$$
S = H(u-\vartheta)
$$
Where $s$ is the spike output and $H$ is the hard threshold function. The difference $u-\vartheta$ measures the neuron's distance from threshold. A negative value means the voltage lies below threshold, and a nonnegative value produces a spike.

During the forward pass, this hard rule preserves the discrete behavior of the spiking neuron. During the backward pass, training needs a derivative that can transmit an error signal to the parameters that influenced $u$. A **surrogate gradient** supplies that signal by replacing the hard threshold's derivative during gradient computation with a simple, deliberately chosen approximation.

One such approximation is the **Piecewise Differential Spike** function. Choose a half-width $\delta>0$ around the threshold and a gradient height $\gamma>0$. The approximate derivative is
$$
\frac{\widetilde{\partial s}}{\partial u}
=
\begin{cases}
\gamma, & \text{if } |u-\vartheta|\leq \delta,\\
0, & \text{if } |u-\vartheta|>\delta.
\end{cases}
$$
Here, $\widetilde{\partial s}/\partial u$ is the surrogate derivative used in the backward pass, $u$ is the membrane voltage, $\vartheta$ is the firing threshold, $\delta$ defines how far the useful-gradient interval extends on either side of the threshold, and $\gamma$ sets the size of the derivative inside that interval. The tilde marks the derivative as an approximation chosen for training.

The interval from $\vartheta-\delta$ to $\vartheta+\delta$ is the **surrogate support region**. Inside it, the neuron receives a nonzero approximate derivative. Outside it, the derivative is zero. The Piecewise Differential Spike therefore treats a neuron near threshold as trainable through small voltage changes. A neuron far below threshold receives no local spike derivative because a small change would still leave it well below firing. A neuron far above threshold also lies outside the selected region once its voltage is sufficiently distant from the switching boundary.

Consider a neuron with threshold $\vartheta=1.0$, support half-width $\delta=0.2$, and gradient height $\gamma=0.5$. Its support region runs from $0.8$ to $1.2$. If its voltage is $u=0.9$, then
$$
|u-\vartheta|=|0.9-1.0|=0.1
$$
Which lies within the support region, so the backward pass uses a surrogate derivative of $0.5$. If the voltage is $u=0.4$, its threshold distance is $0.6$, so the surrogate derivative is $0$. The value at $0.9$ says that this nearly firing neuron can receive credit or blame through its voltage, even though its forward output is still determined by the hard spike rule.

To see how that derivative participates in learning, suppose the loss is $L$, and a weight $w$ affects the membrane voltage $u$. The chain rule gives the approximate weight gradient
$$
\frac{\widetilde{\partial L}}{\partial w}
=
\frac{\partial L}{\partial s}
\frac{\widetilde{\partial s}}{\partial u}
\frac{\partial u}{\partial w}
$$
The term $\partial L/\partial s$ measures how a change in the spike output would affect the loss. The surrogate term $\widetilde{\partial s}/\partial u$ carries that influence through the threshold. The term $\partial u/\partial w$ measures how the weight changes the membrane voltage. Their product estimates how adjusting $w$ could improve the objective.

Suppose these three factors are $2$, $0.5$, and $0.3$, respectively. Then
$$
\frac{\widetilde{\partial L}}{\partial w}
=
2\times 0.5\times 0.3
=
0.3
$$
The optimizer can use this value to adjust the weight. If the same neuron's voltage moves outside the support region, the middle factor becomes zero, and this particular path contributes no weight update through the spike threshold.

The choice of $\delta$ controls how many voltage states participate in learning. A narrow support region concentrates the gradient around neurons that are very close to changing their spike decision. A wider region allows more neurons to receive a signal, including those farther from threshold. The parameter $\gamma$ controls the strength of that signal. Increasing $\gamma$ scales the gradient passed through the threshold, while decreasing it makes the backward influence gentler. Together, $\delta$ and $\gamma$ determine where the approximation acts and how strongly it acts there.

**Question.** Why keep the hard threshold in the forward pass if training uses a piecewise derivative?

**Answer.** The two passes serve different roles. The forward pass computes the neuron's actual discrete spike output, preserving the intended threshold dynamics. The backward pass estimates how parameters should change to improve the loss. Using the hard spike for computation and the Piecewise Differential Spike for gradient propagation gives each pass the information suited to its role.

**Question.** Does a nonzero surrogate derivative mean the neuron produced a partial spike?

**Answer.** The spike remains a discrete value of $0$ or $1$. The nonzero derivative expresses sensitivity for learning: a voltage near threshold is close to changing its future spike decision, so the parameters that produced that voltage receive an update signal.

The resulting mental model has two coordinated layers. Forward computation asks whether the membrane voltage crossed the firing threshold and emits a discrete event. Backward computation draws a small training window around that threshold, passes gradients through neurons whose voltages fall inside the window, and carries those gradients onward to the responsible parameters. The Piecewise Differential Spike makes this window explicit: $\delta$ sets its reach, $\gamma$ sets its strength, and the chain rule turns that local approximation into a usable direction for gradient-based learning.
