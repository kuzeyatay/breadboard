## Temporal Credit Assignment with BPTT

The voltage dynamics developed earlier give each neuron a memory: its voltage at one time step depends on its voltage at the preceding time step. Imagine that an SNN receives an input sequence over four steps and produces an undesirable spike at the fourth. Changing a synaptic weight may alter the neuron's voltage immediately, yet it may also alter the voltage carried into every later step. The final spike can therefore trace back to activity several steps earlier. Learning must follow that chain backward through time to determine how much responsibility belongs to each earlier event.

Let the network state at time step $t$ be $s[t]$. The state can include membrane voltages and any other quantities carried from one step to the next. Let $x[t]$ be the input at that step, $W$ a synaptic weight or collection of weights, and $F$ the state-update rule. The recurrent dynamics can be written as
$$
S[t] = F\bigl(s[t-1],x[t],W\bigr)
$$
This equation describes recurrence: the new state depends on the preceding state. The same weights $W$ are used as the sequence advances, so a weight can influence many states even though it appears only once in the model's parameter set.

Suppose the network incurs a loss $\mathcal{L}[t]$ at each time step. A loss is a numerical measure of how far the network's behavior is from the desired behavior. The loss over a sequence of $T$ steps is accumulated as
$$
\mathcal{L} = \sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathcal{L}[t]
$$
Here, $\mathcal{L}$ is the total sequence loss, $\mathcal{L}[t]$ is the contribution associated with time step $t$, $t$ indexes time, and $T$ is the number of steps in the sequence. The formula lets us compute one training objective from errors distributed across the whole sequence.

Because every timestep loss may depend on $W$, differentiating the total loss gives
$$
\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial W}
=
\sum_{t=1}^{T}
\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}[t]}{\partial W}
$$
The derivative $\partial \mathcal{L}/\partial W$ measures how a small change in $W$ changes the total loss. Each term $\partial \mathcal{L}[t]/\partial W$ measures the contribution associated with the loss at step $t$. This accumulation across timesteps is represented by S1.P5.E6: training must gather gradient information from every relevant point in the sequence rather than treating one selected step as the complete learning signal.

Consider a three-step sequence with losses $0.2$, $0.1$, and $0.7$. Their numerical sum is $1.0$, but the gradient is more informative than that total alone. A weight might slightly increase the first loss, reduce the second, and strongly increase the third. Its update must combine all three effects. The largest loss also need not produce the largest weight gradient, because sensitivity depends on the paths connecting the weight to that loss.

To expose those paths, focus on the loss at time $t$. It depends on the current state $s[t]$, while the current state depends both on $W$ directly and on the earlier state $s[t-1]$. Differentiating through these dependencies gives
$$
\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}[t]}{\partial W}
=
\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}[t]}{\partial s[t]}
\frac{\partial s[t]}{\partial W}
$$
With
$$
\frac{\partial s[t]}{\partial W}
=
\left.\frac{\partial s[t]}{\partial W}\right|_{\mathrm{direct}}
+
\frac{\partial s[t]}{\partial s[t-1]}
\frac{\partial s[t-1]}{\partial W}
$$
In these expressions, $\partial \mathcal{L}[t]/\partial s[t]$ measures how sensitive the current loss is to the current state. The direct derivative $\left.\partial s[t]/\partial W\right|_{\mathrm{direct}}$ measures how the weight affects the current update while the preceding state is held fixed. The factor $\partial s[t]/\partial s[t-1]$ measures how changes in the preceding state pass into the current state. Finally, $\partial s[t-1]/\partial W$ carries the weight's earlier influence forward.

This expansion, captured by S1.P5.E7, separates two routes by which a weight receives credit or blame. One route reaches the current state through the current update. The other reaches it through the recurrent dependency on previous states. For example, a synaptic input at step $1$ may raise the voltage slightly. Some of that voltage remains at step $2$, affects the state at step $3$, and changes the output at step $4$. Assigning part of the step-$4$ error to the step-$1$ influence is called **temporal credit assignment**.

**Question.** Why can't the gradient at time $t$ use only the weight's direct effect on $s[t]$?

**Answer.** The state $s[t-1]$ already contains effects of the same weight from earlier steps. Since $s[t]$ is computed from $s[t-1]$, the current loss inherits those earlier effects. Keeping only the direct term would discard every route that passes through the network's temporal memory.

Repeatedly expanding the earlier-state derivative reveals the full structure:
$$
\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial W}
=
\sum_{t=1}^{T}
\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}[t]}{\partial s[t]}
\sum_{\tau=1}^{t}
\left(
\prod_{k=\tau+1}^{t}
\frac{\partial s[k]}{\partial s[k-1]}
\right)
\left.\frac{\partial s[\tau]}{\partial W}\right|_{\mathrm{direct}}
$$
Here, $\tau$ marks the earlier time at which $W$ directly changes a state. The index $k$ walks from that earlier time toward the loss at time $t$. The product multiplies the temporal Jacobians $\partial s[k]/\partial s[k-1]$, where a Jacobian records how one state responds to a small change in the preceding state. When $\tau=t$, the product contains no intervening recurrence and is treated as $1$. The formula computes the total weight gradient by summing every direct influence at time $\tau$, transported through all later states that lead to each loss at time $t$.

S1.P5.E8 condenses backpropagation through time into this repeated pattern. The recurrence is conceptually **unrolled** by drawing a copy of the state update for each timestep:
$$
S[1]\rightarrow s[2]\rightarrow \cdots \rightarrow s[T]
$$
The copies share $W$, and the backward calculation traverses the arrows in reverse. A contribution originating far in the past contains more temporal Jacobian factors than a nearby contribution. Multiplying many such factors can greatly shrink or enlarge a signal, depending on their values. The same repeated products also explain the computational and memory burden of BPTT: learning may need intermediate states from many timesteps so that each backward dependency can be evaluated.

**Question.** Does unrolling create a different set of weights for every timestep?

**Answer.** Unrolling creates separate views of the network state at different times while preserving the same parameters across those views. Each appearance of $W$ refers to the same weight. Its gradient accumulates contributions from all the timesteps at which that shared weight influenced the sequence.

The resulting mental model is a chain of stored states connected by recurrent updates. A weight touches the chain at many times, each touch can travel forward through later states, and losses can arise anywhere along the chain. BPTT reverses those routes: it begins with timestep losses, follows their dependencies backward through the unrolled recurrence, and adds every contribution that reaches the shared weight. Temporal credit assignment is therefore the act of measuring how present error grew from both present influence and remembered influence across time.
