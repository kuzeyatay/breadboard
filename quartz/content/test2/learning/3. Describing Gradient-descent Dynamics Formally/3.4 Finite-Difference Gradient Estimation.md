## Finite-Difference Gradient Estimation

The loss function from the previous discussion gives the network a single score for its current behavior, and gradient descent needs to turn that score into a direction for changing the weights. In a spiking neural network, this step becomes difficult when a small weight change shifts a neuron across its firing threshold. The output can jump from silence to a spike, so the ordinary derivative through that event may be unavailable. Weight perturbation offers another route: change a weight slightly, run the network again, and observe how the loss responds.

Begin with one adjustable weight $w$ and a loss $L(w)$. Suppose increasing the weight by a small amount lowers the loss. That observation tells us that moving $w$ upward is locally helpful. If the loss rises instead, the weight should move downward. The gradient records this local sensitivity as a number: its sign gives the uphill direction, and its magnitude describes how rapidly the loss changes near the current weight.

To estimate that sensitivity, choose a small positive perturbation scale $\varepsilon$. Evaluate the loss once at $w+\varepsilon$ and once at $w-\varepsilon$. The two perturbed weights lie on opposite sides of the current value. Their loss difference measures how much the objective changes across a total weight interval of $2\varepsilon$, giving the central finite-difference estimator
$$
\widehat{\frac{\partial L}{\partial w}}
=
\frac{L(w+\varepsilon)-L(w-\varepsilon)}{2\varepsilon}
$$
Here, $L$ is the loss function, $w$ is the weight being examined, $\varepsilon>0$ is the perturbation scale, and the hat indicates an estimated derivative. The numerator compares losses on the two sides of $w$, while the denominator converts that change into loss change per unit of weight. The formula therefore computes an approximate local slope without differentiating through the spike-generation mechanism.

Imagine that the current weight is $w=0.60$ and the perturbation scale is $\varepsilon=0.01$. Running the network with $w+\varepsilon=0.61$ produces a loss of $2.18$, while running it with $w-\varepsilon=0.59$ produces a loss of $2.22$. The estimate is
$$
\widehat{\frac{\partial L}{\partial w}}
=
\frac{2.18-2.22}{2(0.01)}
=
\frac{-0.04}{0.02}
=
-2
$$
The negative value means that the loss decreases as the weight increases near $0.60$. Gradient descent moves opposite the gradient, so it will increase this weight. The value $-2$ describes the estimated local sensitivity: around the current setting, a small positive change in the weight is associated with a decrease in loss.

The same idea extends to an entire network by arranging all adjustable weights into one flattened weight vector. Flattening simply means listing the weights in a fixed order, regardless of whether they originally belonged to matrices, layers, or groups of synapses:
$$
\mathbf{w}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
W_1 & w_2 & \cdots & w_d
\end{bmatrix}^{\mathsf T}
$$
In this expression, $\mathbf{w}$ is the complete weight vector, $w_i$ is its $i$-th component, $d$ is the total number of adjustable weights, and ${\mathsf T}$ indicates that the list is written as a column vector. Flattening changes only the bookkeeping. Each component still refers to its original connection in the network.

To estimate the gradient component associated with $w_i$, perturb only that component. Let $\mathbf{e}_i$ be a vector whose $i$-th entry is $1$ and whose other entries are $0$. Then $\mathbf{w}+\varepsilon\mathbf{e}_i$ increases $w_i$ by $\varepsilon$, while $\mathbf{w}-\varepsilon\mathbf{e}_i$ decreases it by the same amount. The resulting estimate is
$$
\widehat{g}_i
=
\frac{
L(\mathbf{w}+\varepsilon\mathbf{e}_i)
-
L(\mathbf{w}-\varepsilon\mathbf{e}_i)
}{
2\varepsilon
}
$$
Here, $\widehat{g}_i$ estimates the partial derivative $\partial L/\partial w_i$, and the perturbed loss evaluations compare network behavior on opposite sides of the current $i$-th weight. Repeating this construction for the components produces the estimated gradient vector
$$
\widehat{\nabla L(\mathbf{w})}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
\widehat{g}_1 &
\widehat{g}_2 &
\cdots &
\widehat{g}_d
\end{bmatrix}^{\mathsf T}
$$
The paired positive and negative perturbations reveal direction through their ordering. If the positive perturbation gives the larger loss, then $\widehat{g}_i>0$, and reducing $w_i$ is locally favorable. If the negative perturbation gives the larger loss, then $\widehat{g}_i<0$, and increasing $w_i$ is locally favorable. Similar losses on both sides produce a component near zero, indicating little observed sensitivity at that perturbation scale.

Once the estimated gradient is available, the weight dynamics can be written as
$$
\mathbf{w}^{(k+1)}
=
\mathbf{w}^{(k)}
-
\eta\,\widehat{\nabla L\!\left(\mathbf{w}^{(k)}\right)}
$$
The superscript $k$ labels the current optimization step, $\mathbf{w}^{(k+1)}$ is the updated weight vector, and $\eta>0$ is the learning rate controlling the update size. The minus sign sends the weights toward decreasing loss according to the estimated slope.

**Question.** Why evaluate both $+\varepsilon$ and $-\varepsilon$ perturbations?

**Answer.** The two evaluations inspect the loss on both sides of the current weight. Their difference supplies a balanced estimate of the local slope: one side shows what happens when the weight rises, and the other shows what happens when it falls. A spike may appear in one perturbed run and disappear in the other, yet the resulting losses can still be compared directly.

The perturbation scale determines how local this comparison is. A large $\varepsilon$ measures change across a broad interval and can blur the behavior near the current weights. A very small $\varepsilon$ makes the two loss values extremely close, so their difference may become difficult to resolve reliably. The chosen scale should therefore be small enough to represent nearby behavior while still producing an observable loss difference.

**Question.** Does the finite-difference estimate remove the spike discontinuity?

**Answer.** The spike mechanism remains discrete. Finite differences work around the missing derivative by treating the whole network as a system that maps weights to a loss. The estimator asks how that final loss changes under controlled perturbations, so it can provide a usable descent direction even when the internal spike event has no ordinary derivative.

The resulting mental model is a local experiment repeated in weight space. Flatten the network's weights into coordinates, nudge one coordinate upward and downward, compare the two losses, and convert that comparison into a slope estimate. Assemble those estimated slopes into a gradient vector, then move the weights in the opposite direction. In this way, discrete spike responses still lead to formally described gradient-descent dynamics through measured changes in the loss.
