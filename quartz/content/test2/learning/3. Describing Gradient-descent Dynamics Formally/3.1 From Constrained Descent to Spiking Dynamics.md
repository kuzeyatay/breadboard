## From Constrained Descent to Spiking Dynamics

The feasible region from the previous section tells us which states satisfy the constraints. Now imagine the current state sitting inside that region while the objective function pulls it toward a lower-cost location. Ordinary gradient descent follows this pull directly. Near a boundary, however, the same motion may point outside the allowed region. A constrained optimizer therefore needs two coordinated motions: descent toward lower objective values and correction back toward feasibility. When the strength of that correction is expressed through a voltage-like variable, the resulting equations begin to resemble the dynamics of a spiking neural network.

Let the state of the optimizer be a vector $\mathbf{x}(t)$, whose entries change continuously with time $t$. Consider the quadratic objective
$$
F(\mathbf{x})=\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{x}^{\mathsf T}Q\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{b}^{\mathsf T}\mathbf{x}
$$
Here, $F(\mathbf{x})$ is the cost assigned to state $\mathbf{x}$, $Q$ is a matrix that determines the curvature and interactions of the quadratic cost, $\mathbf{b}$ is a vector that pulls the state in preferred directions, and ${\mathsf T}$ denotes transpose. The factor $1/2$ makes the derivative of the quadratic term especially simple. Assuming $Q$ is symmetric, the gradient is
$$
\nabla F(\mathbf{x})=Q\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{b}
$$
The gradient points in the direction of fastest increase in cost. Continuous gradient descent moves in the opposite direction, so its dynamics are
$$
\dot{\mathbf{x}}=-\nabla F(\mathbf{x})
=\mathbf{b}-Q\mathbf{x}
$$
The dot in $\dot{\mathbf{x}}$ means a derivative with respect to time: $\dot{\mathbf{x}}=d\mathbf{x}/dt$. This equation lets us compute the instantaneous velocity of the optimizer. The vector $\mathbf{b}$ acts as a steady drive, while $Q\mathbf{x}$ produces a state-dependent opposing term.

For a one-dimensional example, take $F(x)=\frac{1}{2}(2)x^2-6x$. Its gradient is $2x-6$, giving $\dot{x}=6-2x$. At $x=1$, the velocity is $4$, so the state moves toward larger values. At $x=4$, the velocity is $-2$, so it moves toward smaller values. The velocity vanishes at $x=3$, the minimum of the quadratic. Gradient descent therefore behaves like motion that continually adjusts its direction according to the local slope.

Now impose linear constraints
$$
A\mathbf{x}\leq \mathbf{c}
$$
Where each row of $A$ describes one constraint and the corresponding entry of $\mathbf{c}$ gives its boundary value. If unconstrained descent points outward at an active boundary, a correction must remove that outward component. Introduce a vector $\boldsymbol{\lambda}(t)$ whose entries measure the correction associated with each constraint. The constrained dynamics become
$$
\dot{\mathbf{x}}
=\mathbf{b}-Q\mathbf{x}-A^{\mathsf T}\boldsymbol{\lambda}
$$
The term $A^{\mathsf T}\boldsymbol{\lambda}$ converts constraint-level corrections into motion in the coordinates of $\mathbf{x}$. Its minus sign makes the correction push toward the feasible side of the boundary. Each multiplier satisfies $\lambda_i\geq 0$, and a correction becomes active when its associated constraint reaches the boundary and the objective-driven motion would carry the state outward. In the usual complementary picture, $\lambda_i$ vanishes for an inactive constraint and may become positive when that constraint is active.

Imagine a two-dimensional state approaching a straight boundary. The objective supplies a diagonal velocity aimed partly along the boundary and partly through it. The boundary correction cancels the outward part while preserving the useful motion along the boundary. The state can then continue descending without abandoning feasibility. This correction is the optimization counterpart of a brief event that redirects an evolving dynamical system.

To see how constraint satisfaction itself evolves, assign each constraint a voltage-like quantity
$$
\mathbf{v}=A\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{c}
$$
The entry $v_i$ measures the signed displacement of constraint $i$ from its boundary. A negative value lies on the feasible side, zero marks the boundary, and a positive value signals violation. Calling $\mathbf{v}$ a **constraint voltage** is useful because it turns constraint pressure into a time-varying state that can rise toward a threshold.

Differentiate this definition with respect to time. Since $A$ and $\mathbf{c}$ are fixed,
$$
\dot{\mathbf{v}}=A\dot{\mathbf{x}}
$$
Substituting the constrained gradient-descent dynamics gives
$$
\dot{\mathbf{v}}
=A\mathbf{b}-AQ\mathbf{x}-AA^{\mathsf T}\boldsymbol{\lambda}
$$
Every term now has a direct dynamical interpretation. The term $A\mathbf{b}$ supplies drive to the constraint voltages. The term $-AQ\mathbf{x}$ feeds the current optimizer state back into their evolution. The term $-AA^{\mathsf T}\boldsymbol{\lambda}$ communicates boundary corrections across constraints. Its diagonal entries describe how strongly a constraint's own correction changes its voltage, while off-diagonal entries describe interactions between constraints whose directions overlap.

A particularly transparent form appears when $Q=qI$, where $q>0$ is a scalar and $I$ is the identity matrix. Because $A\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{v}+\mathbf{c}$,
$$
AQ\mathbf{x}
=qA\mathbf{x}
=q(\mathbf{v}+\mathbf{c})
$$
The voltage dynamics can therefore be rearranged as
$$
\dot{\mathbf{v}}
=
\underbrace{(A\mathbf{b}-q\mathbf{c})}_{\text{input drive}}
-
\underbrace{q\mathbf{v}}_{\text{leak-like decay}}
-
\underbrace{AA^{\mathsf T}\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{\text{boundary correction}}
$$
This equation lets us read constrained optimization in neural-dynamical language. The constant term drives the voltages, the term proportional to $-\mathbf{v}$ pulls them back like a leak, and the correction term produces coupled voltage changes when constraints become active. If an active correction is delivered as a brief event, $\boldsymbol{\lambda}$ behaves like a spike train: an event occurs at a constraint boundary and causes an immediate corrective influence determined by $AA^{\mathsf T}$.

**Question.** Why use constraint voltage instead of checking $A\mathbf{x}\leq\mathbf{c}$ after every descent step?

**Answer.** Constraint voltage makes feasibility part of the dynamics. Its value shows the distance from each boundary, and its derivative shows whether the trajectory is approaching or leaving that boundary. A threshold at $v_i=0$ can therefore trigger correction at the moment it becomes necessary, giving an event-driven interpretation rather than a separate repair procedure.

**Question.** Does every term in the voltage equation correspond to one biological neuron mechanism?

**Answer.** The correspondence is mathematical: drive, decay, threshold activation, and event-mediated correction have the same structural roles as familiar spiking-network voltage terms. The construction supports a spiking optimizer interpretation in which network dynamics solve a constrained problem; its scope is this dynamical mapping.

The full mental model is a state rolling downhill on a quadratic cost while linear boundaries monitor its motion. Each boundary converts the current state into a constraint voltage. Objective-driven motion raises or lowers those voltages, leak-like feedback regulates their evolution, and a voltage reaching its boundary activates a corrective event. The resulting spikes can be understood as precisely timed optimization actions that keep descent feasible while guiding the state toward lower cost.
