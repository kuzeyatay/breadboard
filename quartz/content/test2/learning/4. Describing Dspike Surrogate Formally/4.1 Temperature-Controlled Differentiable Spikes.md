## Temperature-Controlled Differentiable Spikes

Surrogate gradients give a spiking network a usable learning signal around the firing threshold. The remaining question is how wide that useful region should be. Imagine that a neuron's normalized input $x$ lies near a threshold: values below the threshold should approach silence, values above it should approach a spike, and values close to it should change smoothly enough for gradient-based learning. A broad transition supplies gradients across many nearby inputs, while a sharp transition more closely resembles a discrete spike. A **temperature parameter** controls this trade-off by changing the steepness of the transition.

The Differential Spike, or **Dspike**, surrogate formalizes this idea with a piecewise activation:
$$
\operatorname{Dspike}(x,b)=
\begin{cases}
1, & x>1,\\[4pt]
\dfrac{\tanh\!\bigl(b(x-0.5)\bigr)+\tanh\!\left(\frac{b}{2}\right)}
{2\tanh\!\left(\frac{b}{2}\right)}, & 0\leq x\leq 1,\\[10pt]
0, & x<0.
\end{cases}
$$
Here, $x$ is the input to the surrogate and $b$ is its temperature parameter. Inputs below $0$ produce $0$, inputs above $1$ produce $1$, and inputs between those boundaries pass through a smooth transition built from the hyperbolic tangent function $\tanh$. The midpoint $x=0.5$ acts as the center of that transition.

The normalization in the denominator makes the middle branch meet the outer branches cleanly. At $x=0$,
$$
\tanh\!\bigl(b(0-0.5)\bigr)
=
\tanh\!\left(-\frac{b}{2}\right)
=
-\tanh\!\left(\frac{b}{2}\right)
$$
So the numerator becomes zero and $\operatorname{Dspike}(0,b)=0$. At $x=1$, the two terms in the numerator are equal, giving
$$
\operatorname{Dspike}(1,b)
=
\frac{2\tanh(b/2)}{2\tanh(b/2)}
=
1
$$
The smooth branch therefore connects the silent state to the firing state while preserving the intended boundary values.

The role of $b$ becomes clearer by examining the derivative within $0\leq x\leq 1$. Since the derivative of $\tanh(z)$ is $\operatorname{sech}^{2}(z)$, differentiating the middle branch gives
$$
\frac{\partial\,\operatorname{Dspike}(x,b)}{\partial x}
=
\frac{b\,\operatorname{sech}^{2}\!\bigl(b(x-0.5)\bigr)}
{2\tanh(b/2)}
$$
The function $\operatorname{sech}^{2}(z)$ reaches its maximum at $z=0$, so the largest derivative occurs at $x=0.5$. Its value there is
$$
\left.
\frac{\partial\,\operatorname{Dspike}(x,b)}{\partial x}
\right|_{x=0.5}
=
\frac{b}{2\tanh(b/2)}
$$
A larger $b$ concentrates the derivative more tightly around $x=0.5$. Inputs very close to the midpoint receive a strong learning signal, while inputs farther away quickly approach the stable outputs $0$ and $1$. A smaller $b$ spreads the derivative across more of the interval. For example, when $b$ is small, the approximation $\tanh(z)\approx z$ makes the middle branch behave approximately like
$$
\operatorname{Dspike}(x,b)
\approx
\frac{b(x-0.5)+b/2}{b}
=
X
$$
The transition is then close to a straight ramp from $0$ to $1$. As $b$ grows, the ramp bends into an increasingly abrupt switch centered at $x=0.5$. Temperature therefore determines both the fidelity of the spike approximation and the range of inputs that receive an appreciable derivative.

Dspike can adapt this temperature from one training epoch to the next. Let $b_{e-1}$ be the selected temperature from the previous epoch and let $\Delta b$ be a temperature step size. At epoch $e$, three candidates are considered:
$$
B_{e-1},\qquad b_{e-1}+\Delta b,\qquad b_{e-1}-\Delta b
$$
For each candidate, the resulting surrogate gradient can be compared with a finite-difference gradient estimate. The candidate producing the greatest cosine similarity is selected. Cosine similarity measures directional agreement: a larger value means that the two gradient vectors point more nearly in the same direction. The schedule can consequently sharpen the surrogate, smooth it, or retain its current shape according to the gradient behavior observed during training. It is an epoch-dependent search over nearby temperatures rather than a predetermined increase or decrease.

**Question.** Why can a sharper Dspike produce a less broadly useful gradient even though its slope near the midpoint is larger?

**Answer.** Increasing $b$ raises and narrows the derivative peak around $x=0.5$. Inputs extremely close to the midpoint receive a stronger derivative, but the derivative decays more rapidly away from that point. Fewer input values then lie inside the region carrying a substantial learning signal. Sharpness describes how rapidly the activation changes; gradient coverage describes how wide a range of inputs participates in that change.

A related construction uses an explicitly differentiable spike activation whose sharpness changes with the epoch:
$$
\phi_i(x)
=
\frac{1}{2}\tanh\!\bigl(K_i(x-V_{\mathrm{th}})\bigr)
+
\frac{1}{2}
$$
Here, $x$ is the activation input, $V_{\mathrm{th}}$ is the firing threshold, $i$ is the current epoch index, and $K_i$ is the epoch-dependent sharpness parameter. When $x=V_{\mathrm{th}}$, the argument of $\tanh$ is zero, so $\phi_i(x)=1/2$. Inputs progressively farther below or above the threshold approach $0$ or $1$. The derivative is
$$
\frac{\partial \phi_i(x)}{\partial x}
=
\frac{K_i}{2}
\operatorname{sech}^{2}\!\bigl(K_i(x-V_{\mathrm{th}})\bigr)
$$
Which forms a peak centered directly on the threshold. Increasing $K_i$ makes this peak taller and narrower, transforming a smooth activation into a closer approximation of a discrete threshold.

For epochs $i\in\{0,\ldots,N-1\}$, one proposed schedule is
$$
K_i
=
\frac{1}{9}
\left[
\left(10\frac{i}{N}-1\right)K_{\max}
+
\left(10-10\frac{i}{N}\right)K_{\min}
\right]
$$
Where $N$ is the total number of epochs and $K_{\min}$ and $K_{\max}$ set the schedule's lower and upper sharpness scales. To see how the schedule evolves, collect the terms containing $i$:
$$
K_i
=
\frac{10i}{9N}(K_{\max}-K_{\min})
+
\frac{10K_{\min}-K_{\max}}{9}
$$
Its change per epoch is therefore
$$
K_{i+1}-K_i
=
\frac{10}{9N}(K_{\max}-K_{\min})
$$
When $K_{\max}>K_{\min}$, this quantity is positive and constant, so $K_i$ grows monotonically across epochs. Early activations are smoother and distribute gradients across a wider neighborhood of $V_{\mathrm{th}}$; later activations become sharper and increasingly resemble binary spike decisions.

**Question.** How does the $K_i$ schedule differ from the Dspike temperature update?

**Answer.** The $K_i$ schedule prescribes sharpness directly as a function of the epoch index, producing a monotonic progression when $K_{\max}>K_{\min}$. The Dspike procedure chooses among a temperature, a slightly larger temperature, and a slightly smaller temperature by comparing gradient directions. One follows an explicit epoch formula, while the other adapts locally using observed gradient agreement.

Both constructions create the same useful mental model: a differentiable spike is a movable bridge between continuous learning and discrete firing. Its temperature controls the bridge's shape. A smooth setting lets many near-threshold inputs influence learning, while a sharp setting concentrates learning around the eventual firing boundary. An epoch schedule then controls how that bridge changes over time, either through local gradient-guided choices or through a prescribed increase in sharpness.
