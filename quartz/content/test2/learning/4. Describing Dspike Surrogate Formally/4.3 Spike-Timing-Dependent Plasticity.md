## Spike-Timing-Dependent Plasticity

After learning how surrogate gradients make spike-based networks trainable through differentiable approximations, consider a single synapse operating with information available nearby. A presynaptic neuron sends a spike across the synapse, and a postsynaptic neuron fires shortly afterward. Their close timing suggests that the arriving spike contributed to the postsynaptic response, so the connection should become stronger. If the order is reversed and the postsynaptic neuron fires before the incoming spike arrives, the connection should become weaker. Spike-timing-dependent plasticity, abbreviated **STDP**, turns this temporal relationship into a synaptic weight update.

Imagine that neuron $j$ connects to neuron $i$ through a synapse with weight $w_{ij}$. The weight represents the strength of the connection: a larger value gives spikes from neuron $j$ a greater influence on neuron $i$. Let $t_j$ be the time when the presynaptic neuron $j$ spikes, and let $t_i$ be the time when the postsynaptic neuron $i$ spikes. The synapse can compare these two locally observable times and calculate their difference,
$$
\Delta t = t_j-t_i
$$
Here, $\Delta t$ is the relative spike timing. If neuron $j$ fires before neuron $i$, then $t_j<t_i$, giving $\Delta t<0$. If neuron $j$ fires after neuron $i$, then $t_j>t_i$, giving $\Delta t>0$. The sign of $\Delta t$ therefore records the order of the two events.

Suppose the presynaptic spike occurs at $t_j=12\text{ ms}$ and the postsynaptic spike occurs at $t_i=15\text{ ms}$. Their difference is
$$
\Delta t=12-15=-3\text{ ms}
$$
The negative result means that the presynaptic event came first. The synapse responds with **timing-dependent potentiation**, meaning an increase in synaptic strength. Now reverse the order: if $t_i=12\text{ ms}$ and $t_j=15\text{ ms}$, then $\Delta t=3\text{ ms}$. The positive result selects **timing-dependent depression**, meaning a decrease in synaptic strength.

This timing rule can be written as a piecewise function:
$$
\Delta w_{ij}=
\begin{cases}
A_{+}w_{ij}(1-w_{ij}), & \text{if } t_j-t_i\leq 0,\\[4pt]
A_{-}w_{ij}(1-w_{ij}), & \text{if } t_j-t_i>0.
\end{cases}
$$
The quantity $\Delta w_{ij}$ is the change applied to the synaptic weight. The current connection strength is $w_{ij}$. The coefficient $a_{+}$ is positive and controls the size of potentiation when the presynaptic spike occurs before or at the same time as the postsynaptic spike. The coefficient $a_{-}$ is negative and controls the size of depression when the presynaptic spike occurs afterward. The spike times $t_j$ and $t_i$ determine which branch of the function is used. After calculating the change, the synapse can update its strength through
$$
W_{ij}^{\text{new}}=w_{ij}^{\text{old}}+\Delta w_{ij}
$$
Where $w_{ij}^{\text{old}}$ is the weight before learning and $w_{ij}^{\text{new}}$ is the weight afterward.

The factor $w_{ij}(1-w_{ij})$ makes the update depend on the weight's current value. For weights represented between $0$ and $1$, this factor is small near either boundary and largest in the middle. At $w_{ij}=0.5$,
$$
W_{ij}(1-w_{ij})=0.5(1-0.5)=0.25
$$
At $w_{ij}=0.9$, the same factor becomes
$$
W_{ij}(1-w_{ij})=0.9(1-0.9)=0.09
$$
A connection near the middle of its range can therefore change more strongly than one already close to an extreme. The update naturally slows as the weight approaches $0$ or $1$, helping keep the connection within its intended range.

For a concrete potentiation example, take $w_{ij}=0.5$, $a_{+}=0.04$, $t_j=10\text{ ms}$, and $t_i=13\text{ ms}$. Since
$$
T_j-t_i=10-13=-3\text{ ms}
$$
The upper branch applies. The weight change is
$$
\Delta w_{ij}
=0.04(0.5)(1-0.5)
=0.04(0.25)
=0.01
$$
The updated weight is $0.5+0.01=0.51$. The presynaptic spike preceded the postsynaptic spike, so the connection becomes slightly stronger.

For depression, keep $w_{ij}=0.5$, choose $a_{-}=-0.02$, and let the postsynaptic neuron fire at $10\text{ ms}$ while the presynaptic neuron fires at $13\text{ ms}$. Now $t_j-t_i=3\text{ ms}$, so the lower branch applies:
$$
\Delta w_{ij}
=-0.02(0.5)(1-0.5)
=-0.005
$$
The updated weight is $0.5-0.005=0.495$. The later presynaptic spike receives less influence because its timing provides weaker evidence that it contributed to the earlier postsynaptic event.

The complete mapping from relative timing to weight change is called the **STDP learning window**. In this piecewise rule, the window divides timing into two regions: pre-before-post timing produces potentiation, while post-before-pre timing produces depression. The coefficients $a_{+}$ and $a_{-}$ set the strengths of those two responses, and $w_{ij}(1-w_{ij})$ scales the response according to the synapse's present state.

**Question.** Why does a negative value of $t_j-t_i$ produce potentiation?

**Answer.** The presynaptic time is subtracted from the postsynaptic relationship as $t_j-t_i$. A negative value means $t_j<t_i$, so the presynaptic neuron fired first. The incoming event therefore occurred before the postsynaptic response, and the rule strengthens that connection by selecting the positive coefficient $a_{+}$.

**Question.** Does a larger current weight always produce a larger STDP update?

**Answer.** The update depends on the product $w_{ij}(1-w_{ij})$, which grows from zero to its maximum at $w_{ij}=0.5$ and then shrinks toward zero as $w_{ij}$ approaches $1$. Middle-strength synapses change most readily, while synapses near either boundary change more slowly.

STDP can therefore be pictured as a small timing-sensitive mechanism attached to each synapse. It observes when an incoming spike arrives, compares that time with the postsynaptic spike, chooses strengthening or weakening from the order of events, scales the change by the current weight, and applies the result locally. Repeated across many spike pairs, these local updates shape connection strengths around temporal relationships in neural activity.
