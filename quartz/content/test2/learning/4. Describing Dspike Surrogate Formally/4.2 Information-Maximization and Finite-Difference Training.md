## Information-Maximization and Finite-Difference Training

Surrogate gradients provide a smooth training signal near a neuron's firing threshold, allowing gradient-based learning to continue through a binary spike. Yet some training situations call for a signal that avoids directly assigning a derivative to the spike function. Two useful alternatives begin from different questions. Information-maximization asks whether the emitted spikes preserve enough of the variation carried by membrane potentials. Finite differences ask how the loss changes when the network's weights are perturbed by a small, measurable amount. Together, they show that an SNN can receive useful guidance from the states surrounding a spike or from changes in the network's final loss.

Consider a neuron whose membrane potential varies continuously while its output has only two values: silence, represented by $0$, and a spike, represented by $1$. Many distinct membrane potentials therefore produce the same output. A potential far below threshold and one barely below threshold both produce $0$, even though the second state is much closer to firing. Quantization is the act of mapping that richer continuous state into a smaller set of discrete values, and the spike function performs an extreme form of it.

Let $V$ denote the tensor containing membrane potentials across the relevant neurons, layers, or time steps, and let $S$ denote the corresponding tensor of binary spike activity. The statistical dependence between these quantities can be described by their **mutual information**:
$$
I(V;S)=H(S)-H(S\mid V)
$$
Here, $I(V;S)$ is the mutual information between membrane potentials and spikes, $H(S)$ is the entropy of the spike activity, and $H(S\mid V)$ is the remaining uncertainty about the spikes once the membrane potentials are known. Entropy measures how variable or unpredictable a quantity is. A binary output that almost always equals $0$ has low entropy because its value is easy to guess. A binary output that uses $0$ and $1$ with equal probability has maximum binary entropy because either outcome is equally plausible.

The spike activity is determined by the membrane potential and firing threshold. Once $V$ is known, the corresponding $S$ is fixed, giving the proposed simplification
$$
H(S\mid V)=0
\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
I(V;S)=H(S)
$$
This formula says that maximizing the mutual information reduces to maximizing the entropy of the binary spike activity under this deterministic relationship. The proposed objective therefore encourages a balanced use of the two available spike states. Imagine a group of neurons that remains silent for nearly every input. Its spike tensor contains little variation, so it communicates little through the binary activity itself. Moving the membrane-potential distribution toward the firing threshold makes both outcomes more accessible: some states fall below threshold and produce $0$, while others cross it and produce $1$.

A practical loss expresses this idea through the distance between the average membrane potential and the threshold:
$$
L_{\mathrm{IM}}
=
\frac{1}{L}
\sum_{l=1}^{L}
\left(\bar V^{\,l}-V_{\mathrm{th}}\right)^2
$$
In this expression, $L_{\mathrm{IM}}$ is the information-maximization loss, $L$ is the number of included layers, $l$ identifies a layer, $\bar V^{\,l}$ is the mean membrane potential in layer $l$, and $V_{\mathrm{th}}$ is the firing threshold. The squared term measures the layer's distance from the threshold, and averaging those terms produces one scalar loss for the included layers. Minimizing it pulls each layer's mean membrane potential toward the firing boundary.

The reasoning uses the observation that membrane potentials are often approximately Gaussian distributed. A Gaussian distribution is symmetric, so its mean and median coincide. If that shared center lies at $V_{\mathrm{th}}$, half of the potential values lie on each side of the threshold. The resulting binary activity is then encouraged toward an even division between $0$ and $1$, where binary entropy is largest. For example, if a layer has $\bar V^{\,l}=0.7$ and $V_{\mathrm{th}}=1.0$, its contribution is $(0.7-1.0)^2=0.09$. Moving the mean to $0.9$ lowers that contribution to $0.01$, indicating that the layer's potential distribution is centered closer to the firing boundary.

**Question.** Does minimizing the threshold-distance loss force every neuron to spike half the time?

**Answer.** The loss acts on a layer-level mean membrane potential. It encourages the distribution's center to approach the threshold, and the link to equally likely spike states relies on the distribution being approximately symmetric. Individual neurons and individual time steps can still have different firing patterns.

A second route obtains a training direction by evaluating the loss at nearby weight settings. Let all weight tensors in the SNN be flattened into one vector
$$
W=
\begin{bmatrix}
W_1 & w_2 & \cdots & w_k
\end{bmatrix}^{\mathsf T}
\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
$$
Flattening changes the arrangement of the parameters while preserving each parameter as one coordinate. Here, $k$ is the total number of scalar weights, and $w_i$ is the weight at coordinate $i$. Let $e_i$ be the standard basis vector whose $i$-th entry is $1$ and whose other entries are $0$. Then $w+\varepsilon e_i$ increases only $w_i$ by $\varepsilon$, while $w-\varepsilon e_i$ decreases only that weight by the same amount.

Evaluating the loss on both sides gives the central finite-difference estimate
$$
\hat{\nabla}_{\varepsilon,w}L
=
\frac{1}{2\varepsilon}
\begin{bmatrix}
L(w+\varepsilon e_1)-L(w-\varepsilon e_1)\\
L(w+\varepsilon e_2)-L(w-\varepsilon e_2)\\
\vdots\\
L(w+\varepsilon e_k)-L(w-\varepsilon e_k)
\end{bmatrix}
$$
Here, $L(w)$ is the network loss at weights $w$, $\varepsilon$ is the perturbation size, and $\hat{\nabla}_{\varepsilon,w}L$ is the estimated gradient vector. Each component measures the loss slope along one weight coordinate. If increasing $w_i$ produces a larger loss than decreasing it, the corresponding estimate is positive, suggesting that reducing $w_i$ would move downhill. Formally, as $\varepsilon$ approaches zero, the finite-difference estimate approaches the gradient where that limiting gradient is well defined.

The perturbation size carries special weight in an SNN. A very small change can leave every threshold crossing unchanged, causing the two evaluated losses to match and producing a zero estimate. A larger change can alter spike activity and reveal a useful loss difference, although it samples the loss over a wider interval and gives a coarser local direction. Selecting $\varepsilon$ therefore balances sensitivity to discrete spike changes against fidelity to the nearby loss landscape.

**Question.** How can finite differences train a network without differentiating each spike?

**Answer.** They estimate how the final loss responds to finite changes in each coordinate of the flattened weight vector. The forward computations, including threshold crossings, are evaluated normally at the perturbed weights. The difference between the resulting losses supplies the training signal.

These alternatives build guidance from quantities that remain observable around discrete firing events. Information-maximization shapes membrane-potential distributions so binary activity carries greater variability, with threshold distance providing a computable loss. Finite differences probe nearby parameter settings and turn measured loss changes into an estimated direction. The resulting mental model is a threshold surrounded by two usable sources of learning information: the continuous membrane states approaching it and the loss changes produced when the weights move across it.
