## Action Potentials and Spike Trains

Discrete spike-based computation begins with a physical process that changes continuously. Imagine a neuron receiving many small influences from nearby cells. Each influence nudges an electrical quantity called the **membrane potential**, the voltage difference between the inside and outside of the neuron's membrane. Some nudges raise this voltage, while others lower it. As these effects accumulate, the membrane potential traces a continuous path through time.

Small voltage changes alone would provide an awkward signal for event-driven communication. A receiving cell would need to track every tiny fluctuation and decide which variations were meaningful. A neuron instead converts sufficient accumulated change into a distinctive electrical event: an **action potential**, often called a **spike**. This conversion gives communication a clear temporal marker. The membrane potential evolves continuously, but reaching a critical level triggers a rapid, recognizable voltage trajectory.

Before a spike begins, the neuron rests at a relatively low membrane potential. Incoming stimulation can move the voltage upward toward a **threshold**, the level at which the spike-generating process is activated. Once threshold is reached, voltage-gated channels in the membrane change state. Sodium channels open rapidly, allowing positively charged sodium ions to move in a direction that drives the membrane potential sharply upward. This rising phase is called **depolarization**.

The figure below follows the voltage through this progression while showing how sodium and potassium channel states change. The steep rise aligns with the rapid opening of sodium channels. Near the top of the spike, sodium-channel activity subsides and potassium channels become more influential. Potassium movement then drives the voltage downward during **repolarization**. Because potassium channels remain active for part of the recovery, the voltage can briefly fall below its resting level during **hyperpolarization**. The channel states therefore explain the spike's ordered shape: rapid rise, peak, fall, undershoot, and return toward rest.

A useful analogy is a wave created by tipping a balanced object past a critical point. Gentle pushes may shift the object without producing a full transition. Once the critical point is crossed, the object follows a larger, structured motion determined by its own mechanism. In the neuron, the threshold marks that critical point, while changing channel states generate the action potential's characteristic course.

The recovery period also affects when another spike can occur. Immediately after an action potential begins, the membrane and its channels require time to return to conditions that support another full event. This interval helps separate successive spikes in time. Rather than allowing voltage peaks to blur into one another, the recovery dynamics give each event a distinct temporal identity.

The voltage trace of one action potential makes the continuous physical process especially clear. Voltage rises from its resting region, accelerates into a narrow peak, and then returns more gradually through recovery. The sharp peak marks a specific time, yet the underlying waveform extends across a short interval. Its asymmetry also carries mechanistic meaning: depolarization is rapid, while repolarization and the following recovery unfold along a different trajectory.

An SNN usually represents this rich waveform with an abstract event at the spike time. If the peak occurs at time $t_1$, the model records a spike at $t_1$. The detailed rise and recovery remain the biological motivation for the event, while the computational representation focuses on whether the event occurred and when it occurred. This abstraction allows later processing to operate on discrete events instead of repeatedly transmitting the full voltage trajectory.

Consider a membrane potential that rises as inputs arrive at 2, 5, and 7 milliseconds. The first two inputs move the voltage closer to threshold, and the third carries it across. A spike then occurs near 7 milliseconds. After recovery, a later group of inputs may produce another spike near 14 milliseconds. The resulting message contains two event times, 7 and 14 milliseconds, even though the membrane voltage changed continuously throughout the interval.

**Question.** If an action potential has a continuous voltage shape, why is it treated as a discrete spike?

**Answer.** The continuous waveform creates a sharply identifiable event time. An SNN keeps that event time as the information passed between units. The abstraction preserves the occurrence and timing of the action potential while avoiding the need to reproduce every point along its voltage curve.

A neuron commonly produces repeated action potentials rather than a single isolated one. The ordered sequence of their occurrence times is called a **spike train**. It can be written as a set of times,
$$
\mathcal{S}=\{t_1,t_2,\ldots,t_N\}
$$
Where $\mathcal{S}$ is the spike train, $t_i$ is the time of the $i$-th spike, and $N$ is the total number of spikes observed during the chosen interval. This representation lets us identify both how many spikes occurred and how they were arranged in time.

For example, the trains $\{10,20,30\}$ milliseconds and $\{5,10,30\}$ milliseconds each contain three spikes. Their temporal structures differ: the first has evenly spaced events, while the second contains two closely spaced events followed by a longer gap. Counting gives the same total for both trains, whereas the spike times preserve their different patterns.

The observed voltage trace below shows how repeated action potentials become a temporally structured sequence. Each tall excursion is a full voltage event with depolarization and recovery. Viewed across the longer time axis, those waveforms form a pattern of peaks separated by intervals of varying length. An SNN can reduce that trace to the times at which the peaks occur, producing a spike train whose event count and spacing carry the relevant temporal structure.

**Question.** Does a taller action-potential peak represent a stronger spike?

**Answer.** Spike-based representations generally treat each completed action potential as one event. Greater accumulated stimulation can influence when spikes occur or how frequently they repeat. Signal strength can therefore appear through spike count and timing across an interval rather than through assigning a different numerical size to every spike.

The complete mental model begins with continuously changing membrane potential. Incoming influences move that voltage toward threshold; crossing threshold initiates an action potential whose rising and falling phases emerge from changing sodium and potassium channel states. The peak supplies a precise event time, allowing the continuous waveform to be represented as a discrete spike. As the membrane recovers and the process repeats, individual spikes become a spike train. Information then lives in the temporal pattern: which events occurred, how many appeared, and how their times were spaced.
