Imagine that backpropagation through time has already unfolded a spiking layer across a batch of inputs and many simulation steps. For every neuron at every step, training could evaluate and store a surrogate gradient, even though most membrane potentials may lie far from the firing threshold. The LIF threshold gives a useful way to decide where detailed gradient computation is worthwhile: a neuron whose voltage is close enough to threshold is treated as **active**, while one outside that threshold-centered region receives a simpler approximation.

Recall why a surrogate gradient is needed. A neuron emits a binary spike
$$
S_i^l(t)=f\!\left(V_i^l(t)\right)
$$
Where $V_i^l(t)$ is the membrane voltage of neuron $i$ in layer $l$ at time $t$, and $S_i^l(t)\in\{0,1\}$. The firing function $f$ switches from $0$ to $1$ when the voltage reaches the firing threshold $V_{\mathrm{th}}$. That abrupt switch has no ordinary useful derivative around most voltages, so gradient-based training replaces its derivative with a smooth surrogate. One such surrogate is
$$
G(V)=\frac{1}{\left(\beta\left|V-V_{\mathrm{th}}\right|+1\right)^2}
$$
Where $V$ is the membrane voltage being evaluated, $V_{\mathrm{th}}$ is the firing threshold, and $\beta$ controls how quickly the surrogate decreases as the voltage moves away from threshold. The formula computes a gradient-like sensitivity: it reaches its largest value when $V=V_{\mathrm{th}}$, then shrinks with the voltage's distance from threshold.

For example, at $V=V_{\mathrm{th}}$, the absolute difference is zero and $g(V)=1$. If $\beta=10$ and the voltage lies $0.2$ voltage units from threshold, then
$$
G(V)=\frac{1}{(10\times 0.2+1)^2}
     =\frac{1}{9}
$$
At a distance of $0.5$, the value falls to $1/36$. A voltage far from threshold therefore contributes a much smaller surrogate derivative than a voltage poised near a possible change in spike state.

Sparse surrogate-gradient training turns this gradual decrease into an explicit computational condition. Let $B_{\mathrm{th}}$ denote an activity boundary around the firing threshold. A neuron-time entry is classified as active when its membrane voltage lies sufficiently close to threshold:
$$
\left|V_i^l(t)-V_{\mathrm{th}}\right|<B_{\mathrm{th}}
$$
Here, $V_i^l(t)$ is the voltage of neuron $i$ in layer $l$ at time $t$, $V_{\mathrm{th}}$ is the firing threshold, and $B_{\mathrm{th}}$ is the permitted voltage distance from that threshold. This condition lets training construct a set of entries whose voltages occupy the threshold neighborhood. Increasing $B_{\mathrm{th}}$ admits a wider range of voltages and classifies more entries as active; decreasing it retains only entries very close to firing.

The word *active* in this method refers to the threshold-based condition used for gradient computation. An active entry can include a neuron whose present binary output is zero but whose voltage lies close enough to threshold that a small change could affect its spike state. Consider a threshold of $1.0$ and an activity boundary of $0.1$. Voltages such as $0.94$, $1.00$, and $1.07$ fall inside the active region, while $0.50$ and $1.30$ fall outside it. The classification tracks proximity to the spike transition rather than merely counting output spikes.

Once the active set has been identified, the derivative used during the backward pass becomes piecewise:
$$
\frac{\partial S_i^l(t)}{\partial V_i^l(t)}
\approx
\begin{cases}
G\!\left(V_i^l(t)\right),
& \left|V_i^l(t)-V_{\mathrm{th}}\right|<B_{\mathrm{th}},\\[4pt]
0,
& \left|V_i^l(t)-V_{\mathrm{th}}\right|\ge B_{\mathrm{th}}.
\end{cases}
$$
The upper branch applies the smooth surrogate $g$ to an active neuron-time entry. The lower branch approximates the derivative of an inactive entry as zero. During backpropagation through time, gradient work can therefore concentrate on the entries satisfying the active-neuron condition. The boundary $B_{\mathrm{th}}$ controls the approximation directly: a wide boundary retains more surrogate-gradient evaluations, while a narrow boundary produces a sparser backward computation.

Suppose a layer contains $100$ neurons and runs for $20$ timesteps on a batch of $8$ inputs. The unfolded layer contains
$$
8\times20\times100=16000
$$
Possible neuron-time-batch entries. If $2400$ of those entries lie within the activity boundary, the sparse rule applies the surrogate branch to those $2400$ entries and the zero approximation to the remaining $13600$. The count $2400$ describes the absolute amount of active computation, but it does not by itself reveal whether activity is dense or sparse. The same count would mean something very different in a layer containing ten times as many possible entries.

A normalized metric resolves that ambiguity. The **active-neuron ratio**, expressed as a percentage, is
$$
A=\frac{100a}{BTN}
$$
Where $A$ is the active-neuron ratio in percent, $a$ is the number of entries classified as active, $B$ is the batch size, $T$ is the number of timesteps, and $N$ is the number of neurons in the layer. The denominator $BTN$ counts every possible neuron-time-batch entry over which activity was measured. Dividing $a$ by this total gives a fraction, and multiplying by $100$ converts that fraction into a percentage.

For the preceding example,
$$
A=\frac{100\times2400}{8\times20\times100}=15\%
$$
An activity ratio of $15\%$ means that fifteen out of every hundred possible entries, on average, satisfied the chosen activity condition. The complementary $85\%$ fell into the inactive branch of the sparse surrogate approximation. Because $A$ is normalized across neurons, batch elements, and timesteps, it supports meaningful comparisons between measurements with different tensor sizes, provided that the same active-neuron condition is used.

**Question.** Does an active-neuron ratio of $15\%$ mean that exactly $15\%$ of the layer's neurons fired?

**Answer.** It means that $15\%$ of the measured neuron-time-batch entries met the definition of active. One physical neuron can contribute many active entries by being active at several timesteps or for several batch elements. Activity may also be defined by proximity to threshold, so an entry can be active for sparse gradient computation without emitting a spike at that instant.

**Question.** Why can changing $B_{\mathrm{th}}$ change the active-neuron ratio even when the forward spike train stays the same?

**Answer.** The forward spike train is determined by whether voltages cross $V_{\mathrm{th}}$, whereas the sparse backward rule uses a neighborhood around that threshold. Enlarging $B_{\mathrm{th}}$ classifies more nearby voltages as active and retains their surrogate gradients. Shrinking it classifies fewer entries as active and replaces more derivatives with zero.

The complete mental model is a threshold-centered backward pass. LIF dynamics produce membrane voltages and binary spikes; the smooth surrogate assigns the greatest sensitivity near the firing threshold; the activity boundary selects which of those sensitivities training will retain; and the piecewise derivative replaces the remaining entries with zero. The active-neuron ratio then reports the selected amount as a percentage of all neuron, batch, and timestep opportunities, connecting a local voltage condition to a layer-level measure of sparse gradient computation.
