## Backpropagation Through Time

The voltage dynamics developed earlier give each neuron a memory: its state at one timestep depends on its state and inputs at earlier timesteps. Training must therefore follow the same temporal chain. Imagine that a neuron receives an input at time $1$, retains part of its resulting voltage at time $2$, and contributes to an incorrect output at time $3$. A useful learning rule must assign some responsibility for the error at time $3$ to the weight that processed the input at time $1$. This assignment of later outcomes to earlier events is called **temporal credit assignment**.

Let the recurrent state of a spiking network at timestep $t$ be
$$
\mathbf{h}_t = F(\mathbf{h}_{t-1}, \mathbf{x}_t; \mathbf{W})
$$
Where $\mathbf{h}_t$ contains the network variables carried through time, including neuron voltages and any other state needed by the dynamics; $\mathbf{h}_{t-1}$ is the preceding state; $\mathbf{x}_t$ is the input at timestep $t$; $\mathbf{W}$ is the set of trainable weights; and $F$ is the state-update rule. The network produces an output
$$
\mathbf{y}_t = G(\mathbf{h}_t)
$$
Where $G$ maps the current state to the output used to evaluate performance.

Suppose the network is observed for $T$ timesteps. An error can occur at any of them, so training uses a loss $\mathcal{L}_t$ at each timestep and accumulates those losses:
$$
\mathcal{L} = \sum_{t=1}^{T} \mathcal{L}_t
$$
Here, $\mathcal{L}$ is the total loss for the sequence, $\mathcal{L}_t$ measures the error at timestep $t$, and $T$ is the number of simulated timesteps. This equation lets us combine performance across the entire temporal sequence into one training objective. If the losses over three timesteps are $0.2$, $0.5$, and $0.1$, then the total loss is $0.8$. The larger contribution from time $2$ tells us where more error was observed, while the recurrent dynamics determine which earlier states and weights helped produce it.

Because differentiation distributes across a sum, the gradient of the total loss with respect to a weight matrix is
$$
\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \mathbf{W}}
=
\sum_{t=1}^{T}
\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}_t}{\partial \mathbf{W}}
$$
The quantity $\partial \mathcal{L}/\partial \mathbf{W}$ tells us how a small change in the weights would change the accumulated loss. Each term $\partial \mathcal{L}_t/\partial \mathbf{W}$ is the contribution associated with the loss observed at one timestep. The training gradient therefore accumulates credit and blame across time rather than being determined by a single final output.

To understand each contribution, picture the recurrent network unfolded into a chain. The same network appears once for every timestep: $\mathbf{h}_0$ feeds $\mathbf{h}_1$, which feeds $\mathbf{h}_2$, continuing through $\mathbf{h}_T$. Every copy uses the same weights $\mathbf{W}$. This **unrolled recurrence** is a way of displaying temporal dependencies; it turns repeated use of one dynamical system into a visible sequence of state updates.

A weight can influence the loss at time $t$ through two routes. The **direct gradient path** runs through the computation performed at time $t$: the weight affects $\mathbf{h}_t$, and $\mathbf{h}_t$ affects $\mathcal{L}_t$. The **indirect gradient path** begins at an earlier state: the weight changes $\mathbf{h}_k$ for some $k<t$, that change propagates through every intervening recurrence, and the altered state eventually changes $\mathcal{L}_t$.

Applying the chain rule makes both routes explicit:
$$
\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}_t}{\partial \mathbf{W}}
=
\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}_t}{\partial \mathbf{h}_t}
\frac{\partial \mathbf{h}_t}{\partial \mathbf{W}}
$$
Here, $\partial \mathcal{L}_t/\partial \mathbf{h}_t$ measures how sensitive the timestep loss is to the current state, while $\partial \mathbf{h}_t/\partial \mathbf{W}$ measures how sensitive that state is to the weights. The second factor contains the temporal structure because $\mathbf{h}_t$ inherits information from $\mathbf{h}_{t-1}$.

Differentiating the recurrent update gives
$$
\frac{\partial \mathbf{h}_t}{\partial \mathbf{W}}
=
\left.\frac{\partial \mathbf{h}_t}{\partial \mathbf{W}}\right|_{\mathrm{direct}}
+
\frac{\partial \mathbf{h}_t}{\partial \mathbf{h}_{t-1}}
\frac{\partial \mathbf{h}_{t-1}}{\partial \mathbf{W}}
$$
The direct term describes how $\mathbf{W}$ enters the update at timestep $t$. The second term carries weight sensitivity forward from the previous state. Repeatedly expanding that previous-state derivative reveals contributions from every earlier timestep:
$$
\frac{\partial \mathbf{h}_t}{\partial \mathbf{W}}
=
\sum_{k=1}^{t}
\left(
\prod_{j=k+1}^{t}
\frac{\partial \mathbf{h}_j}{\partial \mathbf{h}_{j-1}}
\right)
\left.\frac{\partial \mathbf{h}_k}{\partial \mathbf{W}}\right|_{\mathrm{direct}}
$$
In this expression, $k$ identifies the timestep at which the weight acts directly; $j$ indexes the later state transitions; $\partial \mathbf{h}_j/\partial \mathbf{h}_{j-1}$ measures how a state change survives one recurrent step; and the product combines those transitions from time $k$ to time $t$. When $k=t$, there are no intervening transitions, so the product behaves like an identity transformation and leaves the direct current-time contribution.

For a concrete interpretation, consider a weight that changes the state at time $1$ by a small amount. Suppose the transition from time $1$ to $2$ passes half of that state change forward, and the transition from time $2$ to $3$ passes one quarter of the remaining change forward. The influence reaching time $3$ is scaled by
$$
\frac{1}{2}\times\frac{1}{4}=\frac{1}{8}
$$
The product of recurrent derivatives is therefore a mathematical record of how strongly an earlier event can affect a later loss. A large product preserves a strong path for temporal credit, while a small product assigns less influence to the distant event.

Substituting the expanded state derivative into the accumulated loss gradient produces the central backpropagation-through-time expression:
$$
\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \mathbf{W}}
=
\sum_{t=1}^{T}
\sum_{k=1}^{t}
\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}_t}{\partial \mathbf{h}_t}
\left(
\prod_{j=k+1}^{t}
\frac{\partial \mathbf{h}_j}{\partial \mathbf{h}_{j-1}}
\right)
\left.\frac{\partial \mathbf{h}_k}{\partial \mathbf{W}}\right|_{\mathrm{direct}}
$$
This formula computes the total weight gradient by considering every loss time $t$ and every earlier weight-use time $k$ that could influence it. The outer sum accumulates losses across timesteps. The inner sum traces each loss backward to current and earlier applications of the shared weights. The derivative product transports sensitivity through the recurrent dynamics. Backpropagation through time, or **BPTT**, is the procedure of evaluating these chain-rule paths backward along the unrolled network.

**Question.** If the same weight is used at every timestep, why does its gradient contain many separate terms?

**Answer.** Each use of the weight occurs in a different temporal context. At time $k$, the current input and recurrent state determine one direct effect on $\mathbf{h}_k$. That effect can then influence losses at times $k,k+1,\ldots,T$. The final gradient adds all of these consequences because changing the shared weight changes every one of its temporal uses simultaneously.

**Question.** How can a loss at time $t$ train a weight that acted much earlier?

**Answer.** The earlier weight changes an earlier state, and that state contributes to the following state through the recurrence. The resulting change continues forward until it reaches time $t$. During differentiation, the chain rule follows the same dependency in reverse: loss sensitivity moves from $\mathbf{h}_t$ through the intervening state transitions and reaches the direct weight dependence at the earlier timestep.

The resulting mental model is a chain of shared computations carrying state forward and responsibility backward. Inputs and voltages move through time to produce a sequence of outputs and losses. Training begins by adding those losses, then follows every dependency from each loss through the current state, through earlier recurrent states, and finally to each temporal use of the weights. BPTT turns the network's memory into explicit gradient paths, allowing an error observed now to shape the parameters that influenced the sequence many steps earlier.
