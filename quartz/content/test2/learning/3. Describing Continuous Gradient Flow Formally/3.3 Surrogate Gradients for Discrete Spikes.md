## Surrogate Gradients for Discrete Spikes

Backpropagation through time has already given us a way to trace how an error at a later time depends on membrane potentials and synaptic weights at earlier times. That chain of dependence reaches a difficult point when a neuron decides whether to emit a spike. Imagine that the neuron's membrane potential sits just below its firing threshold. A small increase in an incoming weight may push the potential across the threshold and produce a spike, while a slightly smaller increase leaves the output silent. The output therefore changes in a sudden jump rather than through a gradual transition.

Let $u_t$ denote the membrane potential at time $t$, and let $\vartheta$ denote the firing threshold. The spike output $s_t$ can be written as a step function:
$$
S_t = H(u_t-\vartheta)
=
\begin{cases}
1, & u_t \geq \vartheta,\\
0, & u_t < \vartheta.
\end{cases}
$$
Here, $H$ is the threshold or Heaviside step function, $s_t=1$ means that the neuron spikes at time $t$, and $s_t=0$ means that it remains silent. This rule captures discrete spike generation directly: membrane potential varies continuously, but the emitted event has only two possible values.

Gradient descent needs more information than the output value alone. To adjust a synaptic weight $w$, it asks how a small change in $w$ would change the loss $L$. During backpropagation, the chain rule produces a factor of the form
$$
\frac{\partial L}{\partial w}
=
\frac{\partial L}{\partial s_t}
\frac{\partial s_t}{\partial u_t}
\frac{\partial u_t}{\partial w}
$$
The symbol $L$ is the training loss, which measures the discrepancy between the network's behavior and its target. The factor $\partial L/\partial s_t$ describes how the loss responds to the spike output. The factor $\partial u_t/\partial w$ describes how the weight influences the membrane potential. Between them lies $\partial s_t/\partial u_t$, the derivative of the spike function. It must communicate how changing the membrane potential changes the spike.

For the step function, that derivative is zero whenever the membrane potential lies strictly below or above the threshold. At the threshold itself, the ordinary derivative is undefined because the output jumps immediately from $0$ to $1$. As a result, ordinary differentiation supplies zero almost everywhere and no finite derivative at the exact switching point. Multiplying the chain-rule factors by zero blocks the learning signal from reaching earlier weights. A weight may place the membrane potential extremely close to producing a useful spike, yet the derivative still reports no local change.

A concrete example makes the obstruction visible. Suppose $\vartheta=1.0$. A neuron with $u_t=0.99$ emits $s_t=0$, while one with $u_t=1.01$ emits $s_t=1$. The two membrane potentials differ by only $0.02$, but the outputs differ by a complete event. At $u_t=0.99$, the exact local derivative of the step function is zero, so gradient descent receives no hint that a small upward adjustment could cross the threshold. The learning procedure needs a usable direction around this boundary.

A **surrogate derivative** supplies that direction. The forward computation continues to apply the discrete threshold rule, preserving spikes as binary events. During the backward computation, the unusable derivative of the step is replaced by a simple approximation that remains nonzero near the threshold. One piecewise choice, called a Differential Spike surrogate, can be written as
$$
\frac{\widetilde{\partial s_t}}{\partial u_t}
=
\begin{cases}
1, & |u_t-\vartheta|<\Delta,\\
0, & |u_t-\vartheta|\geq\Delta.
\end{cases}
$$
The tilde marks this quantity as an approximate derivative used for training. The term $u_t$ is the membrane potential, $\vartheta$ is the firing threshold, and $\Delta>0$ sets the half-width of a narrow neighborhood around the threshold. Within that neighborhood, the surrogate assigns a derivative of $1$, allowing the loss gradient to pass through the spike-generation step. Farther away, it assigns zero because a small local adjustment is less likely to alter whether the neuron spikes.

Using this surrogate changes the weight-gradient calculation to
$$
\widetilde{\frac{\partial L}{\partial w}}
=
\frac{\partial L}{\partial s_t}
\frac{\widetilde{\partial s_t}}{\partial u_t}}
\frac{\partial u_t}{\partial w}
$$
This expression computes an approximate direction for adjusting $w$. If the neuron sits near threshold, the middle factor is nonzero, so information about the loss can flow backward through spike generation. If the neuron sits far from threshold, the middle factor vanishes and the update receives no contribution through that particular spike decision.

For example, let $\vartheta=1.0$ and $\Delta=0.1$. A membrane potential of $u_t=0.96$ falls inside the interval from $0.9$ to $1.1$, giving a surrogate derivative of $1$. If $\partial L/\partial s_t=-0.4$ and $\partial u_t/\partial w=0.5$, then
$$
\widetilde{\frac{\partial L}{\partial w}}
=
(-0.4)(1)(0.5)
=
-0.2
$$
The optimizer now has a finite training signal. Under a standard gradient-descent update, subtracting this negative gradient increases the weight, which can raise the membrane potential and make the desired spike more likely. With $u_t=0.7$, the potential lies outside the surrogate window, so this spike decision contributes zero through the same local path.

**Question.** If the surrogate derivative differs from the exact derivative, what is gradient descent optimizing?

**Answer.** The forward pass still computes the loss from the network's actual discrete spikes. The backward pass uses an approximate local sensitivity to decide how the parameters should move. The resulting update is therefore a gradient approximation: it aims to reduce the real spike-based loss while borrowing a smoother direction around firing thresholds.

**Question.** Why should the surrogate derivative be concentrated near the threshold?

**Answer.** Near the threshold, a small change in membrane potential can change the spike decision, so passing a gradient there gives the optimizer useful credit-assignment information. Far from the threshold, a similarly small change usually leaves the spike decision unchanged. A threshold-centered surrogate focuses the training signal where parameter changes can most directly affect spike generation.

The resulting mental model has two coordinated paths. Time moves forward through membrane dynamics and discrete threshold crossings, producing actual spikes. Error information moves backward through time and encounters each threshold as a blocked derivative. The surrogate opens a narrow training channel around that threshold, allowing nearby membrane potentials and the weights that produced them to receive corrective signals. Discrete events therefore remain the language of network activity, while a locally smoothed gradient provides the continuous direction needed to learn their timing.
