Surrogate gradients give a spiking network a usable local slope near each firing threshold, allowing ordinary gradient-based machinery to continue through a discrete spike. Sometimes a different route is useful: estimate how the loss changes by perturbing the network itself, or define an objective around the information carried by spikes and membrane potentials. These routes address two separate needs. **Finite differences** provide a gradient estimate without differentiating the spike-generation rule, while **information-based objectives** specify what useful spike activity should preserve.

Imagine that every trainable weight in a network has been collected into one long vector,
$$
\mathbf{w}=(w_1,w_2,\ldots,w_N)
$$
Where $N$ is the number of trainable weights. Flattening the weights changes only their bookkeeping: weights from different layers or synapses retain their roles during simulation, but they can now be described by a single coordinate vector. After the network processes an input, a loss $L(\mathbf{w})$ measures how well it performed. Training needs to know which small change in $\mathbf{w}$ will reduce that loss.

A derivative normally answers this question by considering an extremely small change. A finite-difference estimate replaces the infinitesimal change with a small, measurable perturbation. For weight $w_i$, choose a positive perturbation size $\varepsilon$, increase that weight by $\varepsilon$, run the network, and observe the new loss. Then decrease the original weight by $\varepsilon$, run the network again, and observe that loss. The resulting estimate is
$$
\frac{\partial L}{\partial w_i}
\approx
\frac{L(\mathbf{w}+\varepsilon\mathbf{e}_i)
-
L(\mathbf{w}-\varepsilon\mathbf{e}_i)}
{2\varepsilon}
$$
Here, $L$ is the training loss, $\mathbf{w}$ is the flattened weight vector, $w_i$ is its $i$-th weight, $\varepsilon$ is the perturbation magnitude, and $\mathbf{e}_i$ is a vector containing a $1$ in position $i$ and $0$ everywhere else. Therefore, $\mathbf{w}+\varepsilon\mathbf{e}_i$ changes only $w_i$, while $\mathbf{w}-\varepsilon\mathbf{e}_i$ changes the same weight in the opposite direction. The numerator measures the loss difference across a total weight interval of $2\varepsilon$, so dividing by $2\varepsilon$ gives the loss change per unit change in that weight.

Suppose a weight begins at $w_i=0.40$, and choose $\varepsilon=0.01$. A simulation with $w_i=0.41$ produces a loss of $0.72$, while a simulation with $w_i=0.39$ produces a loss of $0.76$. The estimate becomes
$$
\frac{\partial L}{\partial w_i}
\approx
\frac{0.72-0.76}{0.02}
=-2
$$
The negative value means that increasing $w_i$ locally decreases the loss. A gradient-descent update,
$$
W_i \leftarrow w_i-\eta\frac{\partial L}{\partial w_i}
$$
Therefore increases this weight when the learning rate $\eta$ is positive. If $\eta=0.005$, the estimated update gives $w_i\leftarrow0.40-0.005(-2)=0.41$. The two perturbed simulations have supplied directional information even though the spike function itself has a discontinuous threshold.

The same process can be applied to every coordinate of $\mathbf{w}$, producing an estimated gradient vector
$$
\widehat{\nabla_{\mathbf{w}}L}
=
\left(
\widehat{\frac{\partial L}{\partial w_1}},
\widehat{\frac{\partial L}{\partial w_2}},
\ldots,
\widehat{\frac{\partial L}{\partial w_N}}
\right)
$$
Each component requires evaluating how the loss responds to perturbations. Consequently, coordinate-by-coordinate finite differences can require many network evaluations when $N$ is large. The perturbation size also requires care. A very large $\varepsilon$ measures behavior across a broad interval and can miss the local direction, while an extremely small $\varepsilon$ can make the loss difference difficult to distinguish from numerical or simulation variation. Finite differences are best understood as direct measurements of the loss landscape: perturb a parameter, observe the response, and use the response to estimate a slope.

**Question.** How can a finite difference estimate a gradient when spikes change abruptly at threshold?

**Answer.** The estimate operates on the complete mapping from weights to final loss. It runs the network at nearby weight settings and measures the losses produced by those runs. Individual spike times or spike counts may change abruptly, yet the two observed loss values still reveal whether the chosen perturbation improved or worsened the objective. The estimate therefore requires loss evaluations, rather than a derivative of the spike function.

A second route begins by asking what relationship the network should create between its internal state and its emitted events. Let $V$ represent a neuron's membrane potential and $S$ represent its spike activity. For a time step, $S$ may take the value $1$ when the neuron spikes and $0$ when it remains silent. The membrane potential $V$ contains graded internal information, while $S$ is the discrete event communicated onward.

The statistical dependence between these variables can be measured by their **mutual information**:
$$
I(S;V)
=
\sum_s \sum_v
P(s,v)
\log
\frac{p(s,v)}{p(s)p(v)}
$$
Here, $I(S;V)$ is the mutual information between spike activity and membrane potential, $p(s,v)$ is their joint probability, $p(s)$ is the probability of spike state $s$, and $p(v)$ is the probability of membrane-potential value $v$. The sums range over possible spike states and membrane-potential values; continuous potentials can be grouped into ranges when estimating these probabilities from observations. The logarithmic ratio compares how often $s$ and $v$ occur together with how often their independent frequencies would predict that pairing.

If a particular range of membrane potentials strongly predicts a spike, its joint probability with $S=1$ becomes informative. If spike activity varies with little relationship to membrane potential, observing the spike reveals less about the internal state. Maximizing $I(S;V)$ encourages spike events to retain information about the membrane dynamics that produced them. Because training procedures usually minimize a loss, an information-maximization objective can be written as
$$
L_{\mathrm{info}}=-I(S;V)
$$
Where $L_{\mathrm{info}}$ is the loss to minimize. Lowering $L_{\mathrm{info}}$ raises the shared information between $S$ and $V$.

Threshold deviation makes this relationship more directly tied to firing. Let $\theta$ be the firing threshold and $V$ the membrane potential. Define
$$
D=V-\theta
$$
Where $D$ is the signed threshold deviation. Positive values place the potential above threshold, negative values place it below threshold, and values near zero place the neuron near the firing boundary. For example, if $\theta=1.0$ and $V=0.92$, then $D=-0.08$; if $V=1.07$, then $D=0.07$. These values express distance and direction using a single threshold-centered coordinate.

Spike activity can then be related to $D$ through an information-oriented threshold-deviation loss,
$$
L_{\mathrm{TD}}=-I(S;D)
$$
Here, $L_{\mathrm{TD}}$ is the threshold-deviation loss, $S$ is spike activity, $D=V-\theta$ is membrane-potential deviation from threshold, and $I(S;D)$ measures their shared information. Minimizing this loss encourages observed spikes and silences to carry information about where the membrane potential lies relative to threshold. The objective turns continuous threshold proximity into a trainable statistical relationship, so learning can be organized around membrane state and event activity without requiring a direct derivative at the firing discontinuity.

**Question.** Why use threshold deviation instead of the raw membrane potential?

**Answer.** Threshold deviation expresses every potential in the coordinates most relevant to spike generation. A value of $V=0.8$ has different firing significance when $\theta=0.9$ than when $\theta=1.5$, while $D=V-\theta$ immediately states the potential's position relative to the boundary. This centered variable makes the information objective focus on the relationship between spike activity and readiness to fire.

The resulting mental model has two complementary paths around a difficult spike derivative. Finite differences treat the whole network as a system that can be probed: move flattened weights slightly, measure the loss response, and assemble those responses into an estimated gradient. Information maximization begins from the representation the network should learn: spikes should preserve useful dependence on membrane state. Threshold deviation then centers that state on the firing boundary, turning distance from threshold into the variable whose relationship with spike activity guides training.
