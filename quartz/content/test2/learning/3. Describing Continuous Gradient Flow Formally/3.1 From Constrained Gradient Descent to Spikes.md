## From Constrained Gradient Descent to Spikes

The previous feasible-region picture tells us which optimization states are allowed. Now imagine the state moving through that region while trying to reduce a quadratic objective. In the interior, it can follow the downhill direction smoothly. At an active boundary, part of that downhill motion may point outside the allowed region, so the dynamics need a precisely timed correction. When the boundary condition is represented by a voltage, each correction appears as a brief event triggered at a threshold. This is the bridge from continuous constrained optimization to spiking dynamics.

Let the optimization state be a vector $\mathbf{x}(t)$, whose entries change with continuous time $t$. Consider the quadratic objective
$$
E(\mathbf{x})
=
\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{x}^{\mathsf T}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{x}
-
\mathbf{b}^{\mathsf T}\mathbf{x}
$$
Here, $E(\mathbf{x})$ is the quantity being minimized, $\mathbf{Q}$ is a matrix that determines the curvature and interactions of the quadratic objective, $\mathbf{b}$ is a vector that drives the state toward a desired solution, and ${\mathsf T}$ denotes transpose. The factor $1/2$ makes the derivative of the quadratic term especially simple. Assuming $\mathbf{Q}$ is symmetric, the gradient is
$$
\nabla E(\mathbf{x})
=
\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{b}
$$
The gradient points in the direction of increasing objective value, so continuous gradient descent moves in the opposite direction:
$$
\dot{\mathbf{x}}
=
-\nabla E(\mathbf{x})
=
\mathbf{b}-\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{x}.
\qquad \text{(1)}
$$
The dot in $\dot{\mathbf{x}}$ means a derivative with respect to time: $\dot{\mathbf{x}}=d\mathbf{x}/dt$. Equation (1) lets us compute the instantaneous velocity of the optimization state. The term $\mathbf{b}$ supplies a constant drive, while $-\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{x}$ supplies state-dependent feedback. For a one-dimensional example with $E(x)=\tfrac12 qx^2-bx$, the dynamics become $\dot{x}=b-qx$. If $q=2$, $b=6$, and $x=1$, then $\dot{x}=6-2=4$, so $x$ initially rises. At $x=3$, the velocity reaches zero, matching the minimum of the quadratic.

Constraints change this smooth trajectory. Write a collection of linear boundary functions as
$$
\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{x})
=
\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}-\boldsymbol{\theta}
$$
Where each row of $\mathbf{A}$ selects a particular weighted combination of the state variables and each entry of $\boldsymbol{\theta}$ specifies the corresponding boundary level. A component $g_i(\mathbf{x})$ therefore measures how close the state is to boundary $i$. The feasible side can be expressed as $g_i(\mathbf{x})\le 0$. At $g_i(\mathbf{x})=0$, the state lies directly on that boundary.

Suppose ordinary gradient flow reaches such a boundary while its velocity would carry the state toward $g_i(\mathbf{x})>0$. Continuing Equation (1) would violate the constraint. The dynamics therefore acquire a boundary-correction term:
$$
\dot{\mathbf{x}}
=
\mathbf{b}
-
\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{x}
-
\mathbf{A}^{\mathsf T}\boldsymbol{\lambda}.
\qquad \text{(2)}
$$
The vector $\boldsymbol{\lambda}(t)$ contains the strengths of the active corrections. Its component $\lambda_i$ acts along the direction $\mathbf{a}_i$, where $\mathbf{a}_i^{\mathsf T}$ is row $i$ of $\mathbf{A}$. That direction is perpendicular to the associated linear boundary. The product $\mathbf{A}^{\mathsf T}\boldsymbol{\lambda}$ combines all active boundary responses into one correction of the optimization-state velocity.

A useful physical analogy is a bead sliding downhill on a surface enclosed by rigid rails. Gravity supplies the downhill drive. A rail supplies a force only when the bead presses against it, and that force points back into the permitted region. In Equation (2), $\mathbf{b}-\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{x}$ plays the role of the downhill drive, while $-\mathbf{A}^{\mathsf T}\boldsymbol{\lambda}$ plays the role of the rail's response.

**Question.** How can a correction preserve descent if it changes the negative-gradient direction?

**Answer.** At a boundary, the raw negative gradient can contain both an allowed component along the boundary and a forbidden component through it. The correction removes the forbidden component while preserving motion that remains feasible. The state then follows a constrained downhill direction: it decreases the objective as far as the boundary permits and settles when no feasible downhill direction remains.

To express the boundary condition as a neural quantity, define the **constraint voltage**
$$
\mathbf{v}
=
\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}-\boldsymbol{\theta}.
\qquad \text{(3)}
$$
Each component $v_i$ measures the signed distance-like quantity associated with constraint $i$, scaled by the corresponding row of $\mathbf{A}$. The threshold occurs at $v_i=0$. Values approaching zero indicate that the optimization state is approaching the boundary. This voltage converts a geometric fact about $\mathbf{x}$ into a local signal that can be monitored continuously.

Because $\mathbf{A}$ and $\boldsymbol{\theta}$ are fixed, differentiating Equation (3) gives
$$
\dot{\mathbf{v}}
=
\mathbf{A}\dot{\mathbf{x}}.
\qquad \text{(4)}
$$
Equation (4) says that the voltage changes according to the optimization-state velocity viewed through the constraint matrix. Substituting the corrected state dynamics from Equation (2) produces
$$
\dot{\mathbf{v}}
=
\mathbf{A}\mathbf{b}
-
\mathbf{A}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{x}
-
\mathbf{A}\mathbf{A}^{\mathsf T}\boldsymbol{\lambda}.
\qquad \text{(5)}
$$
This expansion exposes three contributions. The term $\mathbf{A}\mathbf{b}$ is an input drive toward or away from the constraints. The term $-\mathbf{A}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{x}$ is recurrent, state-dependent feedback generated by the quadratic objective. The term $-\mathbf{A}\mathbf{A}^{\mathsf T}\boldsymbol{\lambda}$ carries the boundary corrections into voltage space. Its matrix $\mathbf{A}\mathbf{A}^{\mathsf T}$ also describes how a correction associated with one constraint affects the voltages of other constraints.

Now consider a correction concentrated at isolated times $t_i^k$, where $t_i^k$ is the $k$-th time voltage $v_i$ reaches its boundary threshold. Such an event can be represented by a Dirac impulse $\delta(t-t_i^k)$, an idealized pulse with zero duration and unit integrated area. Define the event train for constraint $i$ as
$$
S_i(t)
=
\sum_k \delta(t-t_i^k)
$$
And collect these event trains into the vector $\mathbf{s}(t)$. If each boundary encounter produces a fixed corrective amount, the correction signal can be written as $\boldsymbol{\lambda}(t)=\mathbf{D}\mathbf{s}(t)$, where the matrix $\mathbf{D}$ sets the correction size and can include scaling between constraints. Equation (5) then becomes
$$
\dot{\mathbf{v}}
=
\mathbf{A}\mathbf{b}
-
\mathbf{A}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{x}
-
\mathbf{A}\mathbf{A}^{\mathsf T}\mathbf{D}\mathbf{s}(t).
\qquad \text{(6)}
$$
Between events, $\mathbf{s}(t)$ is silent and the voltage evolves continuously under its input and recurrent terms. When a component reaches threshold, an impulse enters through the final term and shifts one or more voltages away from the boundary. The impulse is therefore a **spike-like corrective event**: its timing announces that a constraint has become active, and its effect enforces feasibility.

For a concrete interpretation, imagine one voltage rising steadily toward zero. While $v_i<0$, the associated constraint remains inactive and no correction is needed. At time $t_i^1$, the voltage reaches zero. The event $s_i(t)=\delta(t-t_i^1)$ generates a rapid corrective change through $-\mathbf{A}\mathbf{A}^{\mathsf T}\mathbf{D}\mathbf{s}(t)$. The voltage moves back into its allowed range, then begins integrating the smooth drive again. Repeated pressure against the same boundary creates a sequence of threshold times, so the optimizer's corrective actions form a spike train.

**Question.** Does a spike represent the value of the optimization variable?

**Answer.** A spike represents a boundary-triggered correction at a particular time. The optimization state is carried by the evolving continuous variables, while the spike time records when a constraint voltage reaches threshold. A sequence of such events shapes the state trajectory by repeatedly applying the corrections required to keep the optimization feasible.

The resulting mental model joins geometry, dynamics, and neural signaling. A quadratic objective creates a smooth vector field that pulls the state downhill. Linear constraints create boundary voltages that track how close the state is to leaving the feasible region. Threshold crossings activate brief corrections, and those corrections feed back through the coupled voltage dynamics. Continuous integration governs motion between boundaries; discrete spikes govern what happens at boundary encounters. A spiking network can therefore be read as a constrained optimizer whose voltages monitor feasibility and whose spikes are the timed corrective actions that guide the state toward the constrained minimum.
