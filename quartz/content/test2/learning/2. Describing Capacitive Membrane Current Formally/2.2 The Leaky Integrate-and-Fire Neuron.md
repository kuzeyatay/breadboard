## The Leaky Integrate-and-Fire Neuron

With membrane potential established as the neuron's internal state and spikes established as discrete output events, imagine several incoming spikes arriving close together. Each arrival nudges the membrane potential upward. If those nudges accumulate strongly enough, the neuron emits its own spike. If the arrivals are sparse or weak, their effects fade before they can combine. A leaky integrate-and-fire neuron captures this entire process with one evolving voltage, one threshold rule, and one reset rule.

<!-- learning-unit:U5:after-introduction -->

The membrane behaves like a small electrical capacitor. A capacitor stores separated charge, and its voltage changes when current adds or removes charge. Membrane capacitance therefore gives the neuron a short-lived memory of its input: current arriving now can influence the voltage a little later. If the membrane had capacitance alone, every input would remain accumulated indefinitely. Realistic temporal processing also needs leakage, which continuously pulls the voltage back toward a resting level. Integration preserves recent input; leakage gradually erases it.

The connection between current and voltage begins with the capacitor relation
$$
I_C(t)=C_m\frac{dV(t)}{dt}
$$
Here, $I_C(t)$ is the current charging the membrane at time $t$, $C_m$ is the membrane capacitance, $V(t)$ is the membrane potential, and $dV(t)/dt$ is the rate at which that potential changes. The formula says that a larger current changes the voltage more rapidly, while a larger capacitance makes the same current change the voltage more slowly. Capacitance acts like the size of a container: a small amount poured into a small container changes its level sharply, whereas the same amount poured into a large container changes its level gently.

Incoming current shares the membrane with a leak current. The leak grows as the membrane potential moves away from its resting value, so it can be written as
$$
I_L(t)=g_L\bigl(V(t)-E_L\bigr)
$$
The symbol $I_L(t)$ denotes leak current, $g_L$ is the leak conductance, and $E_L$ is the resting or leak-reversal potential toward which the voltage relaxes. The difference $V(t)-E_L$ measures displacement from rest. A large displacement produces a strong restoring pull, while a small displacement produces a weak one.

Balancing incoming drive against leakage gives the subthreshold membrane equation
$$
C_m\frac{dV(t)}{dt}
=
-g_L\bigl(V(t)-E_L\bigr)+I(t)
$$
The input current $I(t)$ collects the drive reaching the neuron at time $t$. The minus sign before the leak term makes the voltage move toward $E_L$: when $V(t)$ lies above $E_L$, leakage lowers it; when $V(t)$ lies below $E_L$, leakage raises it. This equation lets us compute how rapidly the membrane potential changes before a spike occurs.

Dividing by $g_L$ reveals the time scale of that change. Define the membrane time constant
$$
\tau_m=\frac{C_m}{g_L}
$$
And the membrane resistance
$$
R_m=\frac{1}{g_L}
$$
The dynamics then become
$$
\tau_m\frac{dV(t)}{dt}
=
-\bigl(V(t)-E_L\bigr)+R_m I(t)
$$
The time constant $\tau_m$ controls how long previous input remains influential. Larger capacitance increases $\tau_m$, allowing voltage to change more slowly and retain input effects longer. Greater leak conductance decreases $\tau_m$, making those effects fade faster. The quantity $R_m I(t)$ expresses the voltage displacement that a sustained input tends to produce.

Suppose the input current suddenly becomes constant. At first, the voltage rises because the incoming drive exceeds the leak. As the voltage rises, the leak becomes stronger. Eventually the two balance, and the voltage approaches
$$
V_{\infty}=E_L+R_m I
$$
Here, $V_{\infty}$ is the steady voltage approached under constant input $I$. The approach is gradual rather than instantaneous. Starting from $V(0)$, the voltage follows
$$
V(t)=V_{\infty}
+\bigl(V(0)-V_{\infty}\bigr)e^{-t/\tau_m}
$$
The exponential factor $e^{-t/\tau_m}$ describes how much of the initial displacement remains after time $t$. After one time constant, about $37\%$ remains; after several time constants, very little remains. A brief input pulse therefore raises the voltage and leaves a fading trace. Another pulse arriving soon can build on that trace, while a pulse arriving much later encounters a voltage already close to rest.

**Question.** How can several individually weak inputs make the neuron fire?

**Answer.** Each input raises the membrane potential for a limited time. When inputs arrive within roughly the same temporal window, the earlier voltage increments have only partly leaked away, so later increments add to them. Their combined effect can carry the membrane potential across the firing threshold even when each input alone would leave it below threshold.

A simulation advances this continuous process in small time steps. Let $\Delta t$ be the duration of one step and define
$$
\beta=e^{-\Delta t/\tau_m}
$$
The factor $\beta$ is the fraction of the previous voltage displacement retained across one step. For a constant input during that step, a provisional membrane potential can be written as
$$
\widetilde{V}_{k+1}
=
E_L+\beta\bigl(V_k-E_L\bigr)
+(1-\beta)R_m I_k
$$
The index $k$ labels the current time step, $V_k$ is the current membrane potential, $I_k$ is the input during the step, and $\widetilde{V}_{k+1}$ is the voltage reached before checking for a spike. The term $\beta(V_k-E_L)$ retains part of the previous displacement from rest. The factor $(1-\beta)$ determines how strongly the current input influences the updated voltage. Together, the terms express retained voltage, leakage toward rest, and incoming drive in a single update.

For a concrete interpretation, take $E_L=0$, $R_m I_k=1$, $\beta=0.8$, and $V_k=0.5$. The provisional voltage is
$$
\widetilde{V}_{k+1}
=
0+0.8(0.5)+(1-0.8)(1)
=
0.6
$$
The old voltage contributes $0.4$, while the current input contributes $0.2$. If the input disappears during the following step, the voltage becomes $0.8\times 0.6=0.48$, showing leakage directly.

Integration and leakage govern the voltage until it reaches the firing threshold $\vartheta$. Spike emission can be expressed as
$$
S_{k+1}
=
H\bigl(\widetilde{V}_{k+1}-\vartheta\bigr)
$$
Where $S_{k+1}$ is the spike output and $H(x)$ is a step function that returns $1$ when $x\geq 0$ and $0$ when $x<0$. Thus $S_{k+1}=1$ marks a spike, while $S_{k+1}=0$ marks silence. The threshold converts a continuously evolving internal voltage into a discrete event.

After a spike, the membrane enters a defined reset state:
$$
V_{k+1}
=
\begin{cases}
V_{\mathrm{reset}}, & \widetilde{V}_{k+1}\geq\vartheta,\\[4pt]
\widetilde{V}_{k+1}, & \widetilde{V}_{k+1}<\vartheta.
\end{cases}
$$
The value $V_{\mathrm{reset}}$ is the post-spike membrane potential. Reset prevents accumulated voltage from carrying unchanged through the spike and gives the neuron a fresh starting state for integrating later input. In the numerical example, if $\vartheta=0.55$, the provisional value $0.6$ produces $S_{k+1}=1$, and the stored membrane state becomes $V_{\mathrm{reset}}$ rather than $0.6$.

**Question.** Does the threshold supply the energy that creates a spike?

**Answer.** The threshold is a decision boundary applied to the membrane state. Integration and leakage determine the voltage trajectory; crossing $\vartheta$ determines when the model emits a spike event. The reset then determines the state from which the next trajectory begins.

The complete mental model is a leaky accumulator watched by an event detector. Incoming current pushes the membrane potential, capacitance spreads that influence through time, and leakage pulls the state toward rest. The threshold reads the accumulated state and emits a spike when it becomes large enough. Reset closes the cycle by placing the membrane at its post-spike value. Repeating this update over time turns a stream of inputs into a precisely timed sequence of output spikes.
