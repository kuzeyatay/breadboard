## Constraint Voltages and Boundary Correction

Once the quadratic objective supplies a preferred direction of motion, the remaining difficulty is keeping that motion inside the feasible region. Imagine a point moving downhill across a shallow bowl while a set of invisible walls limits where it may go. In the interior, the walls have no effect: the point simply follows the slope. At a wall, however, part of the downhill direction may point outside the allowed region. The dynamics then need a correction that removes the forbidden motion while preserving as much downhill progress as possible. This combination of continuous descent and boundary-triggered correction creates the structure that can be interpreted as neuron-like voltage dynamics.

Let the optimization state be the vector $\mathbf{x}(t)$, whose components change with time. For the quadratic objective already established, write its gradient in the linear form
$$
\nabla f(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{c}
$$
Where $f(\mathbf{x})$ is the objective value, $\mathbf{Q}$ is the matrix that determines the curvature and coupling of the quadratic objective, and $\mathbf{c}$ is a constant vector that tilts the objective. The gradient points toward increasing objective value, so descending the objective requires motion in the opposite direction:
$$
\tau_x\dot{\mathbf{x}}
=
-\nabla f(\mathbf{x})
=
-\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{c}
$$
Here, $\dot{\mathbf{x}}=d\mathbf{x}/dt$ is the state's rate of change, and $\tau_x>0$ is a time constant that controls how quickly the state moves. This equation lets us compute the instantaneous downhill velocity from the current state. A larger $\tau_x$ produces slower motion along the same path, while a smaller $\tau_x$ produces faster motion.

For a concrete interpretation, suppose one component satisfies $\tau_x\dot{x}_1=3-2x_1$. When $x_1=0$, its velocity is positive, so it rises. When $x_1=1$, the velocity is still positive but smaller. At $x_1=1.5$, the velocity reaches zero. The dynamics therefore move $x_1$ toward the point where the downhill force vanishes.

Constraints add boundaries to this motion. Write a collection of linear constraints as
$$
\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}\leq \mathbf{b}
$$
Where each row of $\mathbf{A}$ defines the orientation of one boundary and the corresponding component of $\mathbf{b}$ sets its location. For boundary $i$, the quantity $(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x})_i-b_i$ measures how close the state is to crossing that boundary. Negative values lie inside the permitted side, zero lies on the boundary, and positive values lie beyond it.

Unconstrained gradient flow can point through one of these boundaries. A boundary correction therefore enters the state dynamics:
$$
\tau_x\dot{\mathbf{x}}
=
-\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{c}
-\mathbf{A}^{\mathsf T}\boldsymbol{\lambda}
$$
The vector $\boldsymbol{\lambda}$ contains the correction strengths, and $\mathbf{A}^{\mathsf T}$ maps corrections associated with individual constraints back into the coordinates of $\mathbf{x}$. The minus sign makes each active correction push opposite to the outward-facing direction of its boundary. A constraint well inside the feasible region has $\lambda_i=0$. When the state reaches that constraint and the downhill force would carry it outward, $\lambda_i$ becomes positive enough to cancel the forbidden component of motion.

A useful picture is a puck sliding downhill inside a fenced area. Away from the fence, gravity alone determines its velocity. At the fence, a brief inward push changes the puck's direction. The push does not choose the destination; the slope still supplies the underlying drive. It only enforces the permitted region.

**Question.** How can a correction preserve downhill motion instead of stopping the state completely?

**Answer.** The correction acts along the boundary's normal direction, which is the direction pointing through the wall. Motion parallel to the boundary can continue. If the downhill velocity has one outward component and one sideways component, the correction cancels the outward component while retaining the sideways descent. The state then travels along the boundary toward a lower objective value.

To express this boundary mechanism in voltage-like variables, assign each constraint a **constraint voltage**:
$$
\mathbf{v}
=
\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{b}
$$
The component $v_i$ records the signed distance-like value for constraint $i$, up to the scaling set by its row of $\mathbf{A}$. A negative voltage represents available room before the boundary, while $v_i=0$ marks boundary contact. Differentiating this definition gives
$$
\dot{\mathbf{v}}
=
\mathbf{A}\dot{\mathbf{x}}
$$
Because $\mathbf{A}$ and $\mathbf{b}$ are constant. This relation turns motion in optimization space into changes in constraint voltage: whenever $\mathbf{x}$ moves toward a boundary, the corresponding voltage rises toward zero.

Substituting the constrained state dynamics into the voltage derivative yields
$$
\tau_x\dot{\mathbf{v}}
=
-\mathbf{A}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{x}
-\mathbf{A}\mathbf{c}
-\mathbf{A}\mathbf{A}^{\mathsf T}\boldsymbol{\lambda}
$$
Every term now has a neuron-like interpretation. The term $-\mathbf{A}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{A}\mathbf{c}$ supplies the continuous drive that changes the constraint voltages. Its value depends on the current optimization state and on how the objective's gradient aligns with each boundary. The term $-\mathbf{A}\mathbf{A}^{\mathsf T}\boldsymbol{\lambda}$ carries boundary corrections into the voltage dynamics. Its diagonal entries describe how a correction lowers the voltage of the constraint that triggered it, while its off-diagonal entries describe how that correction changes other constraint voltages.

For example, two boundaries may face similar directions. A correction away from one then also moves the state away from the other, so both voltages decrease. If their directions oppose each other, correcting one can move the state closer to the other, causing the second voltage to increase. The matrix $\mathbf{A}\mathbf{A}^{\mathsf T}$ captures these interactions through dot products between boundary directions.

When a constraint voltage reaches its boundary value, the corresponding correction can be represented as a brief event. That event rapidly lowers or resets the triggering voltage and simultaneously affects other voltages through the coupling matrix. Between events, the voltages evolve continuously under the objective-driven term. At an event, a discrete correction protects feasibility. The resulting pattern resembles spiking computation: continuous voltage accumulation leads to a threshold encounter, the encounter triggers a discrete corrective event, and that event influences a network of coupled voltage variables.

**Question.** Does reaching a boundary mean that the optimization has finished?

**Answer.** Reaching one boundary means that one constraint has become active. The state may continue moving along that boundary, and other constraints may later become active as well. The dynamics settle only when the objective's downhill drive is balanced by the active boundary corrections, leaving no feasible direction that continues to lower the objective.

The full mental model is therefore a downhill trajectory equipped with event-sensitive walls. The quadratic objective continuously drives the state, the constraints determine which motions remain feasible, and each constraint voltage tracks how close the state is to its boundary. When a voltage reaches its boundary, a correction event redirects the state and propagates through the coupled voltages. Continuous gradient flow supplies accumulation; boundary correction supplies threshold-triggered events; constraint voltages provide the variables through which constrained optimization takes on the form of neuron-like dynamics.
