## Optimization Objectives and Spiking Voltage Dynamics

A leaky integrate-and-fire neuron already gives us a voltage-like state that accumulates input, leaks over time, and can trigger a spike. Now imagine many such neurons cooperating to choose a vector of activities that best satisfies a goal. Their internal voltages can carry more than recent input: each voltage can measure how strongly the current network state should move in a particular direction to improve that goal. This creates a bridge between membrane-style dynamics and optimization.

Begin with a vector $\mathbf{x}$, whose components $x_1,x_2,\ldots,x_n$ describe the network's current candidate solution. A simple goal can be expressed by a **quadratic objective**,
$$
E(\mathbf{x})
=
\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{x}^{\mathsf T}Q\mathbf{x}
-
\mathbf{b}^{\mathsf T}\mathbf{x}
$$
Here, $E(\mathbf{x})$ is the objective value that the network seeks to minimize, $\mathbf{x}$ is the candidate solution, $Q$ is a matrix describing how the components of the solution interact, $\mathbf{b}$ is a vector describing the external drive toward useful values, and the superscript $\mathsf T$ denotes a transpose. The factor $1/2$ is included because it cancels the factor of $2$ that would otherwise appear when differentiating the quadratic term.

The shape of this objective is bowl-like when $Q$ gives positive curvature. Moving downhill decreases $E$, while the bottom of the bowl gives the preferred solution. To find the downhill direction, differentiate the objective with respect to the vector $\mathbf{x}$:
$$
\nabla E(\mathbf{x}) = Q\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{b}
$$
The symbol $\nabla E$ is the gradient, a vector pointing toward the direction in which the objective increases most rapidly. Reversing it gives the direction of improvement:
$$
-\nabla E(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{b}-Q\mathbf{x}
$$
This expression already resembles a neuron's input balance. The vector $\mathbf{b}$ acts like a drive pushing the state forward, while $Q\mathbf{x}$ acts like state-dependent feedback opposing or redirecting that drive. If the state changes continuously in the downhill direction, its dynamics can be written as
$$
\tau_x\frac{d\mathbf{x}}{dt}
=
\mathbf{b}-Q\mathbf{x}
$$
Where $t$ is time, $d\mathbf{x}/dt$ is the rate at which the candidate solution changes, and $\tau_x$ is a positive time constant controlling how quickly it responds. This equation lets us compute the instantaneous motion of the optimization state from its present position.

For a concrete one-dimensional example, take
$$
E(x)=\frac{1}{2}(2)x^2-6x
$$
Its gradient is $2x-6$, so the downhill motion is $6-2x$. At $x=1$, the motion is positive because $6-2(1)=4$, pushing $x$ upward. At $x=4$, the motion is negative because $6-2(4)=-2$, pushing $x$ downward. At $x=3$, the motion is zero. The opposing pushes meet there, placing the state at the minimum of the objective.

Real optimization problems can restrict which states are allowed. A linear constraint can be written as
$$
A\mathbf{x}\leq \mathbf{c}
$$
Where $A$ contains the directions of the constraint boundaries and $\mathbf{c}$ contains their limits. Each row of this inequality defines a permitted half-space. The overlap of all permitted half-spaces forms the **feasible region**, the set of candidate solutions that satisfy every constraint.

The curved objective in the figure supplies an unconstrained direction of improvement: from a given point, downhill motion heads toward lower objective values. The flat constraint surfaces divide space into allowed and disallowed half-spaces. Their intersection creates the feasible region. While the state remains inside that region, it can follow the unconstrained downhill direction. When that direction reaches a boundary and points beyond the allowed region, the dynamics need an additional correction. That correction redirects the state along an admissible direction, allowing optimization to continue while preserving the constraint.

Voltage-like variables provide a convenient way to represent the need for such corrections. For neuron $i$, a generic capacitive voltage equation has the form
$$
C_i\frac{dv_i}{dt}
=
I_i^{\mathrm{drive}}
-
I_i^{\mathrm{feedback}}
-
I_i^{\mathrm{correction}}
$$
Here, $v_i$ is the neuron's voltage-like state, $C_i$ is its effective capacitance, $dv_i/dt$ is the rate of voltage change, $I_i^{\mathrm{drive}}$ is the current pushing the optimization forward, $I_i^{\mathrm{feedback}}$ is the current produced by the present network state, and $I_i^{\mathrm{correction}}$ represents activity that enforces the allowed region. Dividing by $C_i$ gives the rate at which the voltage changes. A larger capacitance produces a slower response to the same total current, just as a larger container requires more inflow to change its level quickly.

The correspondence becomes clear by placing the optimization and voltage equations side by side. The term $\mathbf{b}$ supplies a drive, $Q\mathbf{x}$ supplies state-dependent feedback, and a constraint-related term supplies boundary correction. In vector form, the voltage dynamics can therefore be organized as
$$
C\frac{d\mathbf{v}}{dt}
=
\mathbf{b}
-
Q\mathbf{x}
-
\mathbf{r}
$$
Where $\mathbf{v}$ collects the voltage-like variables, $C$ sets their response scale, and $\mathbf{r}$ collects corrections associated with active constraints. The quantity $\mathbf{b}-Q\mathbf{x}$ is also the negative gradient of the quadratic objective. Voltage consequently records a local optimization pressure: its evolution reflects how much drive remains after the current solution and any active constraints have been taken into account.

**Question.** Why use both a solution vector $\mathbf{x}$ and a voltage vector $\mathbf{v}$?

**Answer.** They play different roles in the interpretation. The vector $\mathbf{x}$ describes the network's current candidate solution, often through accumulated spike-based activity. The vector $\mathbf{v}$ carries the instantaneous pressure to change that solution. When the present solution fits the objective and constraints well, the competing currents balance and the voltage pressure diminishes. When a mismatch remains, voltage evolves and can help produce events that update the candidate solution.

A spike then becomes a discrete adjustment generated by continuous voltage evolution. Inputs and recurrent interactions build the voltage, much as the negative gradient builds pressure toward lower objective values. When the voltage reaches the event condition, the neuron emits a spike, and that event changes the represented solution or the network feedback. The altered feedback changes later voltage dynamics, producing a loop of evaluation and correction.

**Question.** What happens when the unconstrained minimum lies outside the feasible region?

**Answer.** Downhill motion approaches a constraint boundary before reaching that minimum. At the boundary, correction dynamics cancel the component of motion that would violate the constraint while preserving motion that can still reduce the objective. The resulting optimum lies on the boundary where further feasible improvement is exhausted.

The resulting mental model is a moving point on an objective landscape. The quadratic objective shapes the landscape, its negative gradient supplies the natural downhill drive, and the constraints carve out the region in which the point may travel. Voltage-like states carry the remaining pressure to move, capacitance determines how quickly that pressure changes, and spikes apply discrete updates that reshape the network's current solution. Through this chain, spiking voltage dynamics become an evolving physical representation of constrained optimization.
