## The Hodgkin-Huxley Membrane Model

The phases of an action potential describe how membrane potential changes over time. To explain what produces that changing voltage, imagine a small patch of neuronal membrane receiving an electrical current. Some of the incoming current changes the electrical charge stored across the membrane, while the rest flows through pathways associated with sodium, potassium, and other ions. The Hodgkin-Huxley model turns this current sharing into a precise equation.

<!-- learning-unit:U3:after-introduction -->

A membrane can store separated electric charge because its inner and outer fluids conduct electricity while the membrane between them acts as an insulating layer. This arrangement behaves like a **capacitor**, an electrical component that stores charge across two conducting regions. If the charge difference changes, the voltage across the membrane changes with it. The current used to change that stored charge is called the **capacitive membrane current**.

For an ideal capacitor, stored charge $Q$ is proportional to voltage $V$:
$$
Q = C_m V
$$
Here, $Q$ is the charge stored across a chosen membrane area, $V$ is the membrane potential, and $C_m$ is the membrane capacitance for that area. Capacitance measures how much charge must be separated to produce a given voltage.

Current measures how quickly charge moves. If charge changes over time, the current associated with that change is
$$
I_C = \frac{dQ}{dt}
$$
Substituting $Q=C_mV$, and treating the membrane capacitance as constant, gives
$$
I_C = C_m\frac{dV}{dt}
$$
The symbol $I_C$ is the capacitive current, $C_m$ is the membrane capacitance, $V$ is the membrane potential, $t$ is time, and $dV/dt$ is the rate at which membrane potential changes. This formula lets us compute how much current is being used to move the membrane voltage at any instant. A large positive $dV/dt$ means the voltage is rising rapidly and therefore requires a correspondingly large capacitive current.

Consider a membrane patch with capacitance $C_m=1\,\mu\text{F}$ whose voltage is rising at $20\,\text{V/s}$. Its capacitive current is
$$
I_C=(1\,\mu\text{F})(20\,\text{V/s})=20\,\mu\text{A}
$$
The example gives a direct interpretation of $C_m\,dV/dt$: it measures the current devoted to changing the membrane's stored charge. Once the voltage stops changing, $dV/dt=0$, so the capacitive branch carries zero current at that instant even though ionic currents may still flow.

The remaining current travels through ion-specific membrane pathways. Each pathway is represented by a conductance. Conductance, written $g$, measures how readily current can pass through a pathway. The current through a conductance also depends on a **driving voltage**, the difference between the present membrane potential and an ion's equilibrium potential. For potassium, this current has the form
$$
I_K=g_K(V-E_K)
$$
Where $I_K$ is potassium current, $g_K$ is potassium conductance, and $E_K$ is the potassium equilibrium potential. The difference $V-E_K$ gives the electrical drive on potassium ions. If $V=E_K$, the driving voltage is zero and the potassium current is zero for this model, even when $g_K$ is positive.

Sodium and leak currents follow the same structure:
$$
I_{Na}=g_{Na}(V-E_{Na})
$$
$$
I_L=g_L(V-E_L)
$$
Here, $g_{Na}$, $g_K$, and $g_L$ are the sodium, potassium, and leak conductances. The quantities $E_{Na}$, $E_K$, and $E_L$ are their corresponding equilibrium or reversal potentials. The leak branch collects background ionic flow into one effective conductance and one effective reversal potential. Sodium and potassium conductances can vary as the membrane state changes; the detailed channel kinetics are separate from the current decomposition developed here.

Current conservation now connects the pieces. An applied membrane current must be divided among the capacitive, potassium, sodium, and leak components:
$$
I(t)
=
C_m\frac{dV}{dt}
+
G_K(V-E_K)
+
G_{Na}(V-E_{Na})
+
G_L(V-E_L)
$$
The quantity $I(t)$ is the total current delivered to the membrane at time $t$. Every term on the right has units of current. The first term changes the stored membrane charge. The other three terms describe ionic current through potassium, sodium, and leak pathways. The signs of the terms depend on the chosen current-direction convention; within one convention, the equation consistently accounts for where the total current goes.

*Hodgkin-Huxley equivalent circuit of a neuron membrane section* *(p. 3)*

The capacitor branch corresponds exactly to $C_m\,dV/dt$. Each ionic branch contains a conductance paired with a voltage source: the potassium branch maps to $g_K(V-E_K)$, the sodium branch maps to $g_{Na}(V-E_{Na})$, and the leak branch maps to $g_L(V-E_L)$. The voltage sources represent the distinct equilibrium potentials. All branches share the same membrane voltage $V$, while their different reversal potentials give them different driving voltages. The diagram therefore shows current division physically: total membrane current enters a parallel circuit and separates among charge storage and three ionic routes.

The equation can also be rearranged to calculate the voltage's rate of change:
$$
\frac{dV}{dt}
=
\frac{
I(t)-I_K-I_{Na}-I_L
}{C_m}
$$
At a particular instant, suppose the applied current is $12\,\mu\text{A}$ and the potassium, sodium, and leak currents sum to $9\,\mu\text{A}$ under the selected sign convention. The remaining $3\,\mu\text{A}$ becomes capacitive current. With $C_m=1\,\mu\text{F}$,
$$
\frac{dV}{dt}
=
\frac{3\,\mu\text{A}}{1\,\mu\text{F}}
=
3\,\text{V/s}
$$
The membrane voltage therefore rises at that instant. If the ionic currents instead exactly balance the applied current, the numerator becomes zero and the membrane voltage remains momentarily steady.

**Question.** Why can current flow through an ionic branch while the membrane voltage stays constant?

**Answer.** Constant voltage means $dV/dt=0$, so the capacitive current is zero. Ionic currents can still flow because they depend on conductance and driving voltage. The membrane voltage remains constant when the applied current and the combined ionic currents balance, leaving no current available to change stored charge.

**Question.** Does a large conductance always produce a large ionic current?

**Answer.** Ionic current depends on both conductance and driving voltage: $I_{\text{ion}}=g_{\text{ion}}(V-E_{\text{ion}})$. A large conductance produces little current when $V$ lies close to the corresponding equilibrium potential. A smaller conductance can carry substantial current when its driving voltage is large.

The Hodgkin-Huxley membrane model can therefore be held as one circuit-based mental picture: the membrane capacitor stores charge, ionic conductances provide parallel routes for current, and each route is driven by the distance between the current membrane voltage and its own equilibrium potential. At every moment, the applied current is accounted for by these branches. Whatever portion remains after the ionic currents is the capacitive current, and that remainder determines how quickly the membrane potential moves.
