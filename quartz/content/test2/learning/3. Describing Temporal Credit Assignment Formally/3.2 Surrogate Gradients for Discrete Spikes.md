## Surrogate Gradients for Discrete Spikes

Backpropagation through time assigns credit by tracing how an error depends on earlier membrane potentials, spikes, and synaptic weights. Imagine that this backward-moving learning signal reaches a neuron whose membrane potential sits just below its firing threshold. A tiny increase in an incoming weight could push the neuron across the threshold and create a spike, potentially changing every later state influenced by that event. Gradient-based learning needs a numerical signal that describes this sensitivity. The difficulty is that the neuron's spike changes abruptly from silence to firing, so ordinary derivatives cannot describe what happens at the crossing.

Let $V_t$ be the neuron's membrane potential at time $t$, and let $V_{\mathrm{th}}$ be its firing threshold. Its spike $S_t$ can be written using a step function:
$$
S_t = H(V_t - V_{\mathrm{th}})
=
\begin{cases}
0, & V_t < V_{\mathrm{th}},\\
1, & V_t \geq V_{\mathrm{th}}.
\end{cases}
$$
Here, $H$ is the Heaviside step function, $S_t$ is the binary spiking activity, and $V_t-V_{\mathrm{th}}$ is the neuron's distance from threshold. This formula computes the event used during the network's forward evolution: the neuron emits either zero spikes or one spike at that time step.

The abrupt jump creates the optimization problem. Away from the threshold, a small change in $V_t$ leaves $S_t$ unchanged, so the ordinary derivative is zero. At the threshold, the output jumps instantly, so the ordinary derivative is undefined; in an idealized continuous description, it is represented by a Dirac delta concentrated at the threshold. Neither behavior gives gradient descent a useful neighborhood in which to adjust a weight. A neuron at $V_t=0.999$ with $V_{\mathrm{th}}=1$ receives the same ordinary derivative as a neuron at $V_t=0.1$, even though the first neuron is much closer to changing its firing decision.

The chain rule reveals exactly where this obstruction enters. If a weight $W$ affects the membrane potential, and the resulting spike affects the loss $L$, then one contribution to the weight gradient has the form
$$
\frac{\partial L}{\partial W}
=
\frac{\partial L}{\partial S_t}
\frac{\partial S_t}{\partial V_t}
\frac{\partial V_t}{\partial W}
$$
In this expression, $L$ measures the network's error, $W$ is a synaptic weight, $\partial L/\partial S_t$ measures how the loss responds to the spike, $\partial S_t/\partial V_t$ measures how the spike responds to membrane voltage, and $\partial V_t/\partial W$ measures how the voltage responds to the weight. Backpropagation through time combines terms of this kind across time steps. The problematic factor is $\partial S_t/\partial V_t$, because the discrete spike function supplies no ordinary, smoothly varying derivative.

A **surrogate gradient** replaces that problematic derivative during the backward calculation with a smooth or piecewise-smooth approximation. The forward calculation still uses the discrete spike $S_t\in\{0,1\}$. During learning, however, the backward calculation uses a chosen function $D_{\mathrm{spike}}$ near the threshold:
$$
\frac{\partial S_t}{\partial V_t}
\;\approx\;
D_{\mathrm{spike}}(V_t-V_{\mathrm{th}})
$$
The symbol $D_{\mathrm{spike}}$ names the surrogate derivative, and its input $V_t-V_{\mathrm{th}}$ measures how far the membrane potential lies above or below threshold. This substitution lets the chain rule compute a useful approximate gradient for weights that can move a neuron toward or away from firing.

A simple piecewise $D_{\mathrm{spike}}$ can assign gradient only within a narrow interval around the threshold:
$$
D_{\mathrm{spike}}(x;b)
=
\begin{cases}
\dfrac{1}{b}, & |x|<\dfrac{b}{2},\\[6pt]
0, & |x|\geq\dfrac{b}{2},
\end{cases}
\qquad
X=V_t-V_{\mathrm{th}}
$$
Here, $x$ is the threshold-relative membrane potential and $b>0$ is a **surrogate temperature** controlling the width of the active gradient region. A smaller $b$ creates a narrower, taller region concentrated close to the threshold. A larger $b$ creates a wider, lower region, allowing more near-threshold membrane potentials to receive a learning signal. The factor $1/b$ keeps the total area of this rectangular surrogate equal to one, so changing $b$ redistributes the gradient rather than simply increasing its total mass.

For example, let $V_{\mathrm{th}}=1$ and $b=0.2$. The surrogate is active when $|V_t-1|<0.1$, covering membrane potentials between $0.9$ and $1.1$, and its value inside that interval is $1/0.2=5$. A neuron at $V_t=0.96$ therefore passes a backward signal through the spike operation, while one at $V_t=0.70$ receives zero from this particular surrogate. If $b$ grows to $0.6$, the active interval expands from $0.7$ to $1.3$, while the surrogate's height falls to $1/0.6$. Temperature therefore determines which neurons count as close enough to threshold for learning and how strongly each one transmits gradient.

The figure shows how this backward approximation turns an abrupt optimization landscape into one with usable local direction. With discrete spikes alone, small parameter changes often leave the spike pattern unchanged, producing flat regions, while a threshold crossing can cause a sudden jump in loss. The surrogate spreads sensitivity around each crossing. The resulting backward landscape supplies slopes that guide weights toward lower loss, while the forward path continues to generate discrete spikes. Smoothing belongs to the credit-assignment calculation; the neuron's communicated event remains binary.

**Question.** If the backward pass uses a smooth signal, does the neuron emit fractional spikes?

**Answer.** The neuron emits $S_t=0$ or $S_t=1$ during the forward computation. The surrogate value appears only when estimating how the loss would respond to a change in membrane potential. A surrogate value such as $5$ is a derivative used to scale a learning signal, rather than a spike count or firing amplitude.

**Question.** How should I interpret a large surrogate temperature?

**Answer.** A large $b$ treats a wider range of membrane potentials as relevant to the threshold decision. More neurons can receive gradient, but the piecewise surrogate assigns a smaller value to each one because its height is $1/b$. A small $b$ concentrates stronger gradient near the exact crossing. The temperature therefore controls the width and magnitude of the backward pathway around a fixed firing threshold.

The complete mental model has two coordinated views of the same neuron. Forward in time, the membrane potential crosses a threshold and produces a discrete event. Backward through time, the learning process treats nearby membrane potentials as carrying graded sensitivity, using $D_{\mathrm{spike}}$ to pass credit through the threshold operation. The surrogate turns proximity to firing into an optimization signal, allowing earlier weights to learn how their voltage changes contribute to later error while preserving discrete spikes in the network's actual computation.
