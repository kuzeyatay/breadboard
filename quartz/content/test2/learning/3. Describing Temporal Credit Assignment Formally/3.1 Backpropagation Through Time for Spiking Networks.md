## Backpropagation Through Time for Spiking Networks

Earlier, discrete-time neuron dynamics gave us a way to update a spiking neuron one timestep at a time. The membrane state at time $t$ depends on the state at time $t-1$, the current input, and the synaptic weights. That dependence creates a new question during learning: when an error appears late in a spike sequence, which earlier weights and states helped produce it?

<!-- learning-unit:U9:after-introduction -->

Imagine that a neuron receives input for four timesteps and emits a spike on the fourth. A loss evaluated at that final timestep may indicate that the spike occurred too early, too late, or with the wrong downstream effect. Changing the fourth-timestep calculation alone cannot fully correct the behavior, because the fourth membrane state was built from states accumulated during the preceding timesteps. Learning therefore has to follow the chain of influence backward through the neuron's temporal dynamics.

A compact description begins with a recurrent state equation:
$$
H_t = F(h_{t-1}, x_t; W)
$$
Here, $t$ is the timestep, $h_t$ is the network state at that timestep, $h_{t-1}$ is the preceding state, $x_t$ is the current input, $W$ is a synaptic weight or collection of weights, and $F$ is the update rule. In a spiking network, $h_t$ can include membrane voltages and any other variables carried between timesteps. Spikes are produced from the current state and can influence the state of other neurons.

Suppose the network is evaluated throughout the sequence. Let $L_t$ be the loss associated with timestep $t$. The total loss over a sequence of $T$ timesteps is
$$
L = \sum_{t=1}^{T} L_t
$$
This formula says that each timestep can contribute an error signal. Differentiating the sum gives the total gradient of the loss with respect to a recurrent weight:
$$
\frac{dL}{dW}
=
\sum_{t=1}^{T}
\frac{dL_t}{dW}
$$
The symbol $dL/dW$ measures how a small change in $W$ changes the total sequence loss. Each term $dL_t/dW$ measures how the same weight changes the loss evaluated at one particular timestep. This is the basic temporal credit-assignment rule: a recurrent weight receives a contribution from every timestep whose loss it influences.

Consider a three-timestep sequence with $L=L_1+L_2+L_3$. If the weight affects all three states, then its gradient is
$$
\frac{dL}{dW}
=
\frac{dL_1}{dW}
+
\frac{dL_2}{dW}
+
\frac{dL_3}{dW}
$$
A positive contribution from $L_1$ and a negative contribution from $L_3$ can partially cancel. The final update reflects their combined effect rather than assigning the weight to a single moment. This sum of timestep contributions expresses the role of S1.P5.E6: losses distributed across time jointly determine the weight gradient.

To calculate one contribution, the chain rule follows the effect of the weight through the state used by that loss. If $L_t$ depends on $h_t$, then
$$
\frac{dL_t}{dW}
=
\frac{\partial L_t}{\partial h_t}
\frac{dh_t}{dW}
$$
The factor $\partial L_t/\partial h_t$ describes how sensitive the timestep loss is to the current state. The factor $dh_t/dW$ describes how sensitive that state is to the weight. Their product computes the loss sensitivity transmitted through the state.

The recurrent update makes $dh_t/dW$ contain two routes. The weight can affect $h_t$ directly during the current update, and it can affect $h_t$ indirectly through $h_{t-1}$:
$$
\frac{dh_t}{dW}
=
\frac{\partial h_t}{\partial W}
+
\frac{\partial h_t}{\partial h_{t-1}}
\frac{dh_{t-1}}{dW}
$$
The term $\partial h_t/\partial W$ is the direct influence of the weight at timestep $t$. The term $\partial h_t/\partial h_{t-1}$ measures how strongly the previous state affects the current state. Multiplying it by $dh_{t-1}/dW$ carries forward every earlier influence of the weight.

For example, changing a synaptic weight at timestep $1$ may alter $h_1$. The changed $h_1$ alters $h_2$, which alters $h_3$, which then changes $L_3$. The corresponding chain is
$$
\frac{\partial L_3}{\partial h_3}
\frac{\partial h_3}{\partial h_2}
\frac{\partial h_2}{\partial h_1}
\frac{\partial h_1}{\partial W}
$$
Reading from right to left follows the forward causal path: the weight changes the first state, the first state changes the second, the second changes the third, and the third changes the loss. Reading from left to right follows the backward gradient calculation. S1.P5.E7 captures this recurrent pathway: a weight used earlier can receive credit from a later loss through all intervening states.

Repeatedly expanding the state derivative gives
$$
\frac{dh_t}{dW}
=
\sum_{s=1}^{t}
\left(
\prod_{k=s+1}^{t}
\frac{\partial h_k}{\partial h_{k-1}}
\right)
\frac{\partial h_s}{\partial W}
$$
Here, $s$ is the timestep at which the weight exerts a direct influence, $t$ is the timestep at which that influence is evaluated, and $k$ indexes the recurrent transitions between them. The product multiplies the state-to-state sensitivities along the temporal path from $s$ to $t$. When $s=t$, the path contains no intervening transition, so the product acts as $1$, leaving the direct effect $\partial h_t/\partial W$.

Substituting this expansion into the total gradient produces the ordered-pair form:
$$
\frac{dL}{dW}
=
\sum_{t=1}^{T}
\sum_{s=1}^{t}
\frac{\partial L_t}{\partial h_t}
\left(
\prod_{k=s+1}^{t}
\frac{\partial h_k}{\partial h_{k-1}}
\right)
\frac{\partial h_s}{\partial W}
$$
This expression lets us compute the recurrent weight gradient by summing over every valid pair $(s,t)$ with $s\leq t$. The origin timestep $s$ marks where the weight changes the trajectory. The target timestep $t$ marks where a loss evaluates the consequence. Their ordering follows causality: an influence introduced at $s$ can travel to the same timestep or a later one. S1.P5.E8 makes this structure explicit by representing the gradient as a sum over ordered influencing and evaluated timesteps.

Spiking introduces a sharp threshold into these paths. When a path passes through spike generation, the chain rule also contains a local derivative relating a spike to its membrane state. Training methods supply a usable local gradient for that step, while the temporal organization remains the same: gradients from later losses travel backward through the unrolled sequence and accumulate at each shared weight.

**Question.** Why is the same recurrent weight included many times in the gradient?

**Answer.** The network reuses that weight at multiple timesteps. Each use can alter the current state, and each altered state can affect several later losses. The gradient therefore contains one contribution for every temporally valid route from a use of the weight to an evaluated loss.

**Question.** What does "unrolling" the network through time accomplish?

**Answer.** Unrolling draws each timestep as a separate copy of the state update while keeping the weight $W$ shared across all copies. The recurrent loop becomes a forward chain $h_1 \rightarrow h_2 \rightarrow \cdots \rightarrow h_T$, making each temporal dependency available to the chain rule.

Backpropagation through time can therefore be pictured as credit flowing through an unfolded history. Forward dynamics build each membrane state from earlier states, inputs, spikes, and shared weights. Losses inspect the resulting trajectory at one or more times. The backward calculation traces every loss through that history, assigns a contribution to each earlier use of a weight, and adds all contributions into one recurrent weight gradient.
