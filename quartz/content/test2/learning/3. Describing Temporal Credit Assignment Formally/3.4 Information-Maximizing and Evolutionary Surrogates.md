## Information-Maximizing and Evolutionary Surrogates

Earlier surrogate-gradient methods gave a spiking neuron a smooth backward path through its firing threshold. That path makes temporal credit assignment possible, yet a fixed surrogate treats every stage of training in the same way. Consider what happens near the threshold during learning. Early on, membrane potentials may be scattered widely because the network has barely organized its internal activity. Later, many potentials may gather close to the threshold as the network refines when and where spikes occur. Information-maximizing loss and evolutionary surrogate gradients address these two parts of the problem: the loss encourages membrane potentials to produce informative spike patterns, while the evolving surrogate changes the backward approximation as training progresses.

Let $V$ denote the tensor containing membrane potentials and $S$ the corresponding tensor of emitted spikes. Each spike value is binary: $S=1$ when the membrane potential crosses the firing threshold and $S=0$ otherwise. Passing from $V$ to $S$ compresses a continuously valued quantity into a binary event. For example, potentials of $0.81$, $0.94$, and $1.37$ could all produce a spike when the threshold is $0.8$. Their distinct values disappear from the forward spike representation because all three become $1$.

A useful objective therefore asks how much information the spikes retain about the membrane potentials. This relationship is measured by **mutual information**, written $I(V;S)$. It can be expressed as
$$
I(V;S)=H(S)-H(S\mid V)
$$
Where $H(S)$ is the entropy of the spike tensor and $H(S\mid V)$ is the remaining uncertainty about the spikes once the membrane potentials are known. Entropy measures how unpredictable a variable is. A binary spike variable has low entropy when it almost always takes the same value and reaches its greatest entropy when $0$ and $1$ are equally likely.

The threshold rule determines $S$ from $V$. Once a membrane potential and its threshold are known, the corresponding spike value is fixed, so
$$
H(S\mid V)=0
$$
The mutual-information objective then simplifies to
$$
\underset{V,S}{\arg\max}\;I(V;S)
=
\underset{V,S}{\arg\max}\;H(S)
$$
This formula says that maximizing the information shared by potentials and spikes reduces to maximizing spike entropy under the deterministic firing rule. Here, $\arg\max$ means the values of $V$ and $S$ that make the objective as large as possible. The objective favors a spike tensor in which both binary outcomes occur substantially, because a neuron that always remains silent or always fires produces a highly predictable binary output.

Imagine ten membrane-potential observations from one layer. If all ten remain below threshold, the resulting spike sequence is $0000000000$. Seeing one entry tells you what every other entry is likely to be, so the sequence carries little variation. A sequence such as $0101100011$ contains both outcomes more evenly. Its spike values are harder to predict in advance, giving the binary representation greater entropy.

**Question.** Does maximum spike entropy mean that every individual neuron must fire on exactly half of all timesteps?

**Answer.** The objective concerns the distribution represented by the spike tensor being optimized. Its practical effect is to discourage strongly one-sided activity in which nearly every relevant entry is $0$ or nearly every entry is $1$. Exact half-firing behavior for every neuron at every training stage is a much stronger condition than the entropy argument requires.

The entropy objective still needs a differentiable quantity that training can adjust. The membrane potentials provide that quantity. Suppose the potentials in a layer are approximately Gaussian distributed. A Gaussian distribution is symmetric, so its mean and median coincide. Denote the mean membrane potential in layer $l$ by $\bar V^{\,l}$ and the firing threshold by $V_{\mathrm{th}}$. When the threshold lies at the median, approximately half of the potentials fall below it and half lie above it. The threshold then divides the distribution into the two binary outcomes needed for high spike entropy.

This reasoning leads to the information-maximization loss
$$
L_{\mathrm{IM}}
=
\frac{1}{L}
\sum_{l=1}^{L}
\left(\bar V^{\,l}-V_{\mathrm{th}}\right)^2
$$
Where $L_{\mathrm{IM}}$ is the information-maximization loss, $L$ is the number of included layers, $l$ identifies a layer, $\bar V^{\,l}$ is the mean membrane potential in that layer, and $V_{\mathrm{th}}$ is the firing threshold. The squared term measures the distance between the layer's mean potential and the threshold. Minimizing the loss moves that mean toward the threshold, placing the threshold near the center of the assumed symmetric potential distribution.

For a concrete interpretation, let $V_{\mathrm{th}}=1.0$. If a layer has $\bar V^{\,l}=0.4$, its contribution is $(0.4-1.0)^2=0.36$. If learning moves the mean to $0.9$, the contribution becomes $(0.9-1.0)^2=0.01$. The smaller value indicates that the threshold now lies much closer to the distribution's center, where it can divide potentials more evenly between silent and spiking outcomes. The threshold-distance loss is therefore a tractable route from an information objective to a quantity that gradient-based training can minimize.

The backward pass still requires an approximation for how spike activity responds to voltage. An evolutionary surrogate represents that approximation as an activation whose parameter depends on the training epoch:
$$
\widetilde S_e
=
\phi_{\alpha_e}\!\left(V-V_{\mathrm{th}}\right)
$$
Here, $\widetilde S_e$ is the smooth spike estimate used during backward computation at epoch $e$, $\phi$ is a differentiable surrogate activation, $V-V_{\mathrm{th}}$ is the signed distance from threshold, and $\alpha_e$ controls the activation's shape. Its derivative supplies the evolutionary surrogate gradient:
$$
\frac{\partial \widetilde S_e}{\partial V}
=
\phi'_{\alpha_e}\!\left(V-V_{\mathrm{th}}\right)
$$
The epoch dependence is written formally as
$$
\alpha_e=\alpha(e),
\qquad e=0,1,\ldots,E
$$
Where $e$ is the current epoch, $E$ is the final training epoch, and $\alpha(e)$ is the prescribed evolution of the surrogate parameter. Changing $\alpha_e$ changes how broadly or sharply backward credit is assigned around the firing threshold. Early epochs can use a shape that supplies useful gradient information across a wider range of threshold distances. Later epochs can use a closer approximation to the spike transition as membrane potentials become more organized.

**Question.** How do the information-maximization loss and the evolutionary surrogate work together?

**Answer.** They act on complementary parts of learning. The information-maximization loss shapes the membrane-potential distribution by drawing each layer's mean toward the firing threshold. The evolutionary surrogate controls how errors pass backward through the threshold at each epoch. One arranges potentials so the binary spike representation remains informative; the other adapts the smooth path used to assign credit as training develops.

The resulting mental model has two moving pieces around one threshold. Forward activity compresses membrane potentials into binary spikes, so the information objective keeps the threshold near the center of the potential distribution and encourages a richer balance of spike outcomes. Backward learning follows a smooth surrogate whose epoch-dependent parameter changes the reach and shape of gradient credit. Together, information maximization and surrogate evolution replace a permanently fixed approximation with a training process that shapes both what crosses the threshold and how credit flows around it.
