## Neurons, Synapses, and Membrane Potential

A spike becomes useful only when one neuron can receive it, respond to it, and pass a new spike onward. Imagine a spike arriving at the boundary between two neurons. The arriving event cannot simply jump across open space. It must cross a specialized connection, influence the receiving neuron's electrical state, and contribute to whether that neuron produces its own spike. This path-from reception to integration to transmission-depends on the neuron's structure and on the movement of charged particles across its membrane.

A biological neuron has several regions that support this flow of information. Branching **dendrites** receive signals from other neurons. These branches lead toward the main body of the cell, where incoming effects are combined. Extending away from the cell body is the **axon**, a long projection that carries an action potential toward other cells. The axon ends in terminals positioned near the dendrites of receiving neurons. Together, these regions form a directional pathway: signals arrive through dendrites, alter the electrical condition of the neuron, and may trigger an action potential that travels along the axon to its terminals.

The small connection between an axon terminal and a receiving dendrite is called a **synapse**. The neuron sending a signal is the **presynaptic neuron**, while the neuron receiving it is the **postsynaptic neuron**. Synapses allow activity in one cell to change the electrical behavior of another.

Many synapses transmit signals chemically. When an action potential reaches a presynaptic terminal, it triggers the release of molecules called **neurotransmitters**. These molecules cross the synaptic space and bind to receptors embedded in the postsynaptic cell membrane. Receptor activation then produces an electrical effect in the receiving neuron. A spike has therefore been translated into a chemical signal and then back into an electrical signal.

Picture this process as passing a message across a narrow river. The action potential brings the message to one bank. Neurotransmitter molecules act like small carriers that cross the gap, and postsynaptic receptors recognize what they deliver. The receiving neuron does not copy the arriving spike directly. Instead, the synaptic event changes its electrical state, contributing to the conditions under which it may later generate a spike.

Synaptic transmission can also be electrical. At an electrical synapse, **gap junctions** connect the presynaptic and postsynaptic neurons so that current can pass directly between them. This direct route supports quicker transmission than the chemical sequence of release, crossing, and receptor binding. Both forms of synapse serve the same larger function: they allow one neuron's activity to influence another neuron.

To understand what the postsynaptic neuron does with that influence, focus on its membrane. The cell membrane separates the neuron's interior from its external environment. The two sides contain different distributions of charged ions, so an electrical voltage develops across the membrane. This voltage is the neuron's **membrane potential**.

Membrane potential describes the difference in electrical potential between the inside and outside of the cell. A useful analogy is a capacitor: separated charge stores electrical potential, and current changes how much charge is separated. The neuronal membrane behaves similarly because it separates charged regions. When synaptic activity changes the flow of ions, it changes the membrane potential. The neuron can therefore integrate its incoming signals as changes in a shared electrical state.

Suppose several synaptic events occur close together. Each event produces an electrical influence in the postsynaptic neuron. Those influences contribute to the evolving membrane potential, rather than immediately becoming separate outgoing spikes. The membrane potential provides a meeting place where incoming activity can accumulate and interact. In this sense, integration means that the neuron's present electrical state reflects the effects of signals it has recently received.

**Question.** Does every presynaptic spike force the postsynaptic neuron to produce another spike?

**Answer.** A presynaptic spike changes the postsynaptic neuron through a synapse, usually by altering ion flow and therefore membrane potential. The receiving neuron integrates that influence with its existing electrical state and with other incoming signals. An outgoing spike occurs when the membrane potential rises enough to activate the mechanism that initiates an action potential.

That initiating mechanism depends on **voltage-gated ion channels**, which are proteins in the membrane that open or close in response to membrane voltage. When the membrane potential increases enough to cross an intrinsic biological threshold, particular voltage-gated channels open. Charged ions then flow through the membrane, driven by differences in ion concentration and electrical charge between the inside and outside of the cell.

This ion flow raises the membrane potential in a process called **depolarization**. The changing voltage opens channels in a neighboring segment of the axon membrane, which produces another local rise and fall in membrane potential. The sequence continues along the axon as a continuous chain of depolarization events. The resulting traveling electrical event is an **action potential**, or spike.

A row of falling dominoes provides a helpful interpretation. Each axon segment undergoes its own voltage change, and that local change triggers the adjacent segment. The action potential therefore propagates along the axon even though individual ions need only cross the membrane locally. By the time the event reaches the axon terminals, it can initiate synaptic transmission to the next neuron.

**Question.** If an action potential involves continuously changing voltage, why is it treated as a discrete spike?

**Answer.** The membrane voltage follows a continuous rise-and-fall trajectory during an action potential, but the neuron communicates the occurrence of that complete event as a distinct signal. Downstream neurons receive a spike at a particular time. The event is discrete, while the biological process that produces and propagates it depends on continuous membrane dynamics.

The whole pathway can now be pictured as a repeating cycle. Dendrites receive synaptic influences, and the membrane integrates them as changes in voltage. A sufficient increase in membrane potential opens voltage-gated ion channels, beginning an action potential. That action potential propagates along the axon to its terminals, where it affects another neuron through a chemical or electrical synapse. Neuronal communication therefore joins two complementary forms of information: a continuously evolving membrane potential inside each neuron and discrete spikes that carry events between neurons.
