## Why Spike Derivatives Need Surrogates

Backpropagation through time has already given us a way to trace how activity at many time steps contributes to a final error. Imagine that this trace reaches a neuron whose membrane voltage sits just below its firing threshold. A small increase in an incoming synaptic weight could push the voltage across the threshold and produce a spike. That spike could then affect later neurons, later time steps, and eventually the network's output. Gradient descent needs to estimate how changing the weight would change the error, so it must pass a useful learning signal through the threshold operation.

The difficulty comes from the way a spiking neuron converts voltage into an event. Let the membrane voltage at a particular time be $V$, and let the firing threshold be $V_{\mathrm{th}}$. The neuron emits a spike value $S=1$ when $V$ reaches or exceeds $V_{\mathrm{th}}$, while it emits $S=0$ when $V$ remains below the threshold. This hard threshold creates a discrete jump: the output stays fixed at zero as voltage rises toward the threshold, then switches immediately to one when the threshold is crossed.

A derivative measures how rapidly an output changes when its input changes by a tiny amount. Away from the threshold, a tiny voltage change usually leaves the spike value unchanged. The local slope is therefore zero throughout those regions. At the threshold itself, the spike value jumps abruptly, so an ordinary derivative has no finite value there. This makes the threshold a **non-differentiable spike function**.

Consider a neuron with threshold $1.0$. Raising its voltage from $0.70$ to $0.71$ leaves its output at zero. Raising it from $1.20$ to $1.21$ leaves its output at one. Yet raising it from $0.99$ to $1.01$ changes the output from zero to one. The function responds decisively near the threshold, but its ordinary derivative supplies zero almost everywhere and becomes undefined at the jump. A learning algorithm following those derivatives receives little guidance about which subthreshold voltages are close to producing a useful spike.

The obstruction becomes clearer when the spike participates in a chain of computations. A synaptic weight influences the membrane voltage, the voltage determines whether the neuron spikes, and the spike influences the loss. Gradient descent applies the chain rule through that sequence. The gradient associated with the weight contains a factor representing the derivative of the spike with respect to voltage. When that factor is zero, the learning signal passed to earlier weights is also driven toward zero. When the voltage lies exactly at the jump, the required ordinary derivative is unavailable. Backpropagation through time can organize dependencies across time, but the hard threshold still interrupts the backward signal at each spike-generation step.

A **surrogate derivative** supplies a practical route through this interruption. During the forward computation, the neuron continues to use the hard threshold and emits discrete spikes. During the backward computation, the learning algorithm replaces the unusable threshold derivative with the derivative of a smooth, threshold-centered function. The replacement usually gives its largest values near $V_{\mathrm{th}}$ and smaller values farther away. A neuron close to firing therefore receives a stronger adjustment signal than a neuron whose voltage lies far from the threshold.

For example, suppose two silent neurons have voltages $0.99$ and $0.20$ with a threshold of $1.0$. Their forward spike values are both zero. A threshold-centered surrogate can assign a substantial backward signal to the neuron at $0.99$, because a modest parameter change may alter its future spike decision. It can assign a much smaller signal to the neuron at $0.20$, where a similarly small change is unlikely to cross the threshold. Gradient descent can then distinguish two situations that look identical when represented only by their current binary spike values.

This use of separate forward and backward rules creates a **forward-backward mismatch**. The forward path answers the computational question, "Did the membrane voltage cross the threshold?" The backward path answers the learning question, "Which direction of parameter change is likely to improve the loss near this threshold?" The hard spike function governs network activity, while the surrogate derivative guides parameter updates. The mismatch is deliberate: each path receives a rule suited to its role.

The effect can be understood through the shape of the optimization problem. Hard spike decisions can make small parameter changes appear to have no effect until a threshold crossing suddenly changes the network's output. Across many neurons and time steps, these abrupt changes produce an irregular, non-smooth relationship between parameters and loss. Ordinary gradient descent struggles on such a surface because the local slope can vanish across broad regions and fail at the jumps.

The figure contrasts the difficult landscape created by hard spike transitions with the smoother effective landscape used during surrogate-gradient learning. In the non-smooth view, abrupt ridges and flat regions provide unstable or uninformative local directions. In the smoothed view, changes around those abrupt boundaries become gradual enough to produce a usable downhill direction. The network's forward computation still travels through discrete spike states; smoothing changes the backward estimate used to choose the next parameter update. The resulting **effective loss landscape** is therefore a learning aid rather than a replacement for spike-based computation.

**Question.** If the surrogate derivative is only an approximation, why can gradient descent use it?

**Answer.** Gradient descent needs a direction that tends to reduce the loss. The hard threshold's ordinary derivative provides almost no direction, while a threshold-centered surrogate expresses sensitivity near the place where a small voltage change can alter a spike. The update follows this informative local estimate, and repeated forward evaluations reveal whether the resulting parameters improve the network's output.

**Question.** Does a smooth surrogate make the neuron emit fractional spikes?

**Answer.** The forward rule still emits discrete values such as zero or one. Smooth values appear in the backward calculation as estimates of sensitivity. A neuron can therefore communicate with spikes during the forward pass while receiving graded credit during learning.

A useful mental model is a railway switch controlled by a rigid threshold. The train takes one track or the other, giving the forward process a discrete outcome. Learning needs information about how close the switch was to changing position, so the backward process uses a smooth sensitivity signal concentrated around the switching point. Backpropagation through time carries that signal across temporal dependencies, and gradient descent uses it to adjust synaptic weights. Surrogate gradients thus connect discrete spike generation to gradual learning by preserving hard events in activity while supplying smooth guidance for optimization.
