## Neurons, Synapses, and Signal Flow

A spiking neural network becomes easier to understand when each neuron is viewed as a small signal-processing cell connected to many others. Information moves through the network as brief electrical events called **spikes**, and each spike begins with a physical sequence inside a biological neuron: signals arrive through branching inputs, influence the cell's electrical state, and may cause a new signal to travel toward other cells. The shape of the neuron supports this direction of flow.

Incoming signals usually reach a neuron through its **dendrites**, the branching structures that act as its main receiving region. A neuron may have many dendritic branches, allowing it to receive signals from many presynaptic neurons. A **presynaptic neuron** is the cell sending a signal across a connection. The receiving cell is called the **postsynaptic neuron**.

The dendrites carry the effects of incoming signals toward the **soma**, or cell body. The soma serves as the central integration region: it brings together the electrical influences arriving through the neuron's inputs. Some incoming signals push the neuron's electrical state toward producing a spike, while others push it away. Their combined influence changes the voltage across the cell membrane.

That voltage difference is called the **membrane potential**. The cell membrane separates the inside of the neuron from the surrounding environment, and the two sides have different electrical charge conditions. Because of this separation, the membrane can store an electrical difference somewhat like a capacitor. The membrane potential describes the neuron's current electrical state and changes as signals arrive.

Imagine a neuron receiving three closely timed inputs. Two inputs raise its membrane potential, while one lowers it. The soma integrates their combined effects over time. If the upward influences are sufficiently strong, the membrane potential reaches the conditions required to initiate an **action potential**, the brief electrical event represented as a spike. If the combined input is weaker, the membrane potential changes without producing an outgoing spike. The neuron therefore converts a changing collection of inputs into discrete output events.

The timing of the inputs affects this process. Inputs arriving close together can combine before their effects fade, while widely separated inputs may influence the membrane potential at different moments. Inputs from different synapses can also arrive together and contribute to the same change. The membrane potential therefore gives the neuron a short-lived internal state: recent events influence how the cell responds to a new event.

**Question.** Does each incoming signal immediately cause the neuron to emit a spike?

**Answer.** An incoming signal changes the postsynaptic neuron's membrane potential. The soma integrates that change with the effects of other recent inputs. A spike occurs when the combined electrical state reaches the conditions for an action potential, so the response depends on both the incoming signal and the neuron's current membrane potential.

Once initiated, the action potential travels along the **axon**, a long projection that carries the neuron's output away from the soma. A single axon can branch near its end, allowing one neuron's spike to influence multiple postsynaptic neurons. The ends of these branches are called axon terminals. Each terminal approaches a receiving region, commonly a dendrite, on another neuron.

The communication point between the axon terminal and the receiving neuron is a **synapse**. More precisely, the synapse includes the small space and associated structures through which the presynaptic neuron passes a biological signal to the postsynaptic neuron. The spike reaches the axon terminal, triggers transmission across this connection, and produces an electrical effect in the receiving cell. This process is called **synaptic transmission**.

Chemical synaptic transmission proceeds through an intermediate messenger. Activation of the presynaptic neuron triggers the release of chemicals called **neurotransmitters**. These molecules cross the small space between the cells and bind to receptors in the postsynaptic cell membrane. Receptor activation then causes an electrical signal to propagate through the postsynaptic neuron, changing its membrane potential. The sequence is therefore electrical within the sending neuron, chemical across the synaptic gap, and electrical again within the receiving neuron.

A simple analogy is a relay in which one runner reaches a handoff point and passes a baton to the next runner. The arriving action potential is the first runner, neurotransmitter release performs the handoff, and the resulting electrical change in the postsynaptic neuron begins the next stage. The handoff preserves a clear causal chain even though the signal changes physical form while crossing the synapse.

Electrical synapses provide another route. Here, the presynaptic and postsynaptic neurons are connected through **gap junctions**, which allow current to pass directly from one cell to the other. This direct electrical connection supports quicker transmission. Both chemical and electrical synapses connect the output of one neuron to the evolving membrane potential of another, creating paths along which activity can move through a neural network.

**Question.** Is a synapse simply the end of an axon?

**Answer.** The axon terminal forms the sending side of the connection. The synapse is the communication region between that terminal and the postsynaptic cell, including the gap and the structures that transmit and receive the signal. It links one neuron's outgoing action potential to an electrical change in another neuron.

The entire signal path can now be followed as one continuous process. Dendrites receive synaptic influences from other neurons. Those influences alter the membrane potential as the soma integrates activity arriving across space and time. When the electrical state produces an action potential, the spike travels down the axon. Axon terminals then transmit the signal across synapses, changing the membrane potentials of downstream neurons.

This chain supplies the biological foundation for event-driven computation. Each neuron maintains an internal electrical state through its membrane potential, while communication between neurons occurs through distinct events. A spike marks the moment when integrated activity becomes an outgoing message. Across a network, many such cells form a repeating cycle of reception, integration, spike generation, and synaptic transmission, allowing patterns of timed events to carry signals from neuron to neuron.
