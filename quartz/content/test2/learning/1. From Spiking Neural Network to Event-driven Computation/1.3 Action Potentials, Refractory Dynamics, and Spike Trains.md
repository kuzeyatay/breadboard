A neuron already has a membrane potential, and incoming synaptic signals can push that voltage upward or downward. Imagine several incoming signals arriving close together and raising the membrane potential toward a critical level. Small voltage changes can remain local and fade, but once the voltage crosses the cell's threshold, voltage-sensitive channels in the membrane begin a self-reinforcing process. The resulting rapid rise and fall in voltage is an **action potential**, commonly called a **spike**. This converts a gradually changing membrane voltage into a discrete communication event.

<!-- learning-unit:U3:after-introduction -->

The conversion begins with channels that respond to voltage. When the membrane potential reaches threshold, voltage-gated sodium channels open and allow positively charged sodium ions to flow into the cell. Ion movement across the membrane is an electrical current, so this inward flow rapidly raises the membrane potential. The rising voltage opens additional sodium channels, which admit more positive charge and make the voltage rise even faster. This self-reinforcing rise is called **depolarization**.

Depolarization gives spike generation its event-like character. Before threshold, an added synaptic input may create only a modest voltage change. After threshold, the opening of voltage-gated channels drives a large, rapid excursion. Threshold therefore acts as the transition between accumulating input and emitting a spike. A useful analogy is a row of spring-loaded switches: pressing the first switch far enough releases it, and its movement triggers neighboring switches. The complete event follows from crossing the release point rather than from continuously pushing every switch through its full motion.

The inward sodium current cannot keep raising the voltage indefinitely. As the membrane potential reverses, sodium channels change state and potassium channels open. Positively charged potassium ions then flow outward, carrying positive charge away from the cell interior. The membrane potential falls back toward its resting level during **repolarization**. Sodium entry therefore drives the rising phase, while potassium exit drives the falling phase.

*Action potential phases and voltage-gated sodium and potassium channel states* *(p. 2)*

Follow the voltage curve in the figure together with the channel states. Near rest, the membrane is ready to respond to sufficient input. Crossing threshold opens sodium channels, and the steep upward slope records rapid sodium influx. Near the peak, the sodium-driven rise ends as sodium channels cease conducting and potassium channels open. The downward slope then records outward potassium flow during repolarization. Potassium conductance can persist long enough to carry the voltage below its resting level, producing **hyperpolarization**, the undershoot visible after the main spike. The shape is therefore a record of an ordered channel sequence: sodium-driven rise, potassium-driven fall, and gradual recovery toward rest.

Recovery takes time because the channels must return to states from which another full action potential can begin. This recovery interval is the **refractory period**. Immediately after a spike, the membrane has recently completed a large voltage excursion, sodium channels are recovering, and potassium-driven effects may still be pulling the voltage downward. The neuron's ability to respond is consequently constrained by its recent activity. As recovery proceeds, the membrane and channel states again become ready for another threshold crossing.

The refractory period gives a spike temporal extent even when the spike is represented computationally as a single event. A spike at one instant changes what the neuron can do at the following instants. If fresh synaptic input arrives during recovery, the same input that would usually bring the membrane to threshold may be insufficient because the voltage is lower or the relevant channels have not fully recovered. Later input has a greater chance of producing another spike as the membrane returns toward its resting condition.

**Question.** If threshold has already been crossed, why does the voltage come back down instead of remaining high?

**Answer.** Threshold initiates an ordered dynamical process rather than a permanent voltage change. Sodium channels create the rapid inward current that raises the voltage, but their conducting state is temporary. As the voltage reaches its peak, sodium entry subsides and voltage-gated potassium channels support an outward current. Positive charge leaving the cell lowers the membrane potential through repolarization. Continued potassium influence can briefly carry the voltage below rest, after which channel recovery and membrane dynamics return the neuron toward its resting state.

A single action potential describes one complete voltage excursion. Neural activity usually unfolds over a longer interval containing multiple such events. The ordered sequence of spike times is a **spike train**. For example, suppose a neuron emits spikes at 10, 28, 45, and 63 milliseconds after observation begins. Each event has a similar action-potential waveform, while the train also contains the intervals between events: 18, 17, and 18 milliseconds in this example. Those intervals reveal a nearly regular firing pattern over that period.

*Observed neuronal spike train and isolated action potential voltage traces* *(p. 3)*

The graph separates two useful timescales. The isolated voltage traces show the short, stereotyped shape of individual action potentials: each spike rises sharply and then falls through repolarization and recovery. The longer recording shows those events distributed across time as a spike train. Individual spike heights and shapes remain broadly similar, while the spacing between spikes varies. Information can therefore reside in **when** spikes occur and **how frequently** they recur over an interval, rather than requiring each spike to carry a different amplitude.

A common beginner question follows from seeing a continuous voltage trace converted into discrete spikes.

**Question.** Does representing a spike as a discrete event discard the membrane dynamics that produced it?

**Answer.** The discrete event marks the outcome of the underlying voltage process: the membrane reached threshold and initiated an action potential. Depolarization, repolarization, and refractory recovery still determine whether a spike occurs and when another one can follow. Event-based representations focus communication on the spike times while the neuron's internal state continues to evolve between those times. The event is therefore connected to a physical sequence and to a recovery constraint.

This chain forms the bridge from membrane dynamics to event-driven computation. Synaptic inputs change the membrane potential; sufficient depolarization crosses threshold; voltage-gated channel dynamics generate a spike; repolarization and refractory recovery shape the timing of the following opportunity to spike; and repeated events form a spike train. The resulting mental model is a stateful event generator: voltage integrates ongoing influence, threshold triggers a stereotyped event, recovery carries the memory of recent firing, and the temporal pattern of those events becomes the signal passed onward.
