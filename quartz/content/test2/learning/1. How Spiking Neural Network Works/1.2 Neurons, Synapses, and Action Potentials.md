## Neurons, Synapses, and Action Potentials

A spiking neural network communicates through the discrete spikes introduced earlier, yet each spike begins with a continuously changing electrical state inside a neuron. Imagine one neuron receiving many small signals from nearby neurons. Each incoming signal slightly shifts the neuron's electrical condition. These shifts accumulate, and when the electrical state reaches a critical level, the neuron produces a rapid pulse that travels onward. Understanding this conversion-from many graded inputs to one discrete event-requires following membrane voltage, ion channels, and synapses as parts of one signaling process.

A neuron is enclosed by a thin membrane that separates its interior from the surrounding fluid. Electrically charged particles called ions are distributed across this membrane, creating a voltage difference between the inside and outside. This difference is the **membrane potential**. Voltage describes electrical potential difference, so membrane potential tells us how strongly electrical charges are separated across the membrane. A resting neuron maintains a relatively stable membrane potential while it waits for incoming signals.

The membrane contains proteins that form channels through which particular ions can pass. Some channels respond to changes in membrane potential and are called **voltage-gated ion channels**. "Gated" means that the channel can open or close, while "voltage-gated" means that its state depends on the voltage across the membrane. Two channel types play central roles in producing a spike: voltage-gated sodium channels and voltage-gated potassium channels. Their opening and closing shape the rise and fall of the membrane voltage.

The process begins when signals arriving at the neuron raise the membrane potential toward a threshold. The **threshold** is the voltage level that triggers the rapid spike-generating sequence. Once the membrane potential crosses this level, voltage-gated sodium channels open. Sodium ions move through these channels, causing the membrane potential to rise rapidly. This rising phase is called **depolarization** because the electrical separation across the membrane becomes smaller and then reverses during the spike.

The rapid voltage pulse produced by this sequence is an **action potential**. Its shape comes from channel states changing in a coordinated order. Sodium channels drive the steep upward phase. Near the peak, sodium entry subsides while voltage-gated potassium channels open. Potassium movement then drives the membrane potential downward during **repolarization**, returning the voltage toward its resting level. Continued potassium-channel activity can briefly carry the voltage beyond its resting level before the membrane settles back toward rest.

Read the figure from left to right as a chain of causes. The membrane begins near its resting voltage. A sufficient rise reaches threshold, sodium channels open, and the curve climbs sharply during depolarization. Around the peak, sodium-channel behavior changes and potassium channels become active. The voltage then falls during repolarization. The delayed closing of potassium channels helps produce the lower portion of the trajectory before the membrane returns toward its resting voltage. The curve therefore records the combined electrical effect of channel-state changes over time.

A useful analogy is a coordinated pair of gates controlling a reservoir. Opening the sodium gate rapidly drives the level upward. As that gate stops dominating and the potassium gate opens, the level is driven downward. The action potential's recognizable shape emerges from the timing of these two responses. The membrane voltage, channel states, and action potential are therefore different views of the same event: voltage controls channel opening, ion movement changes voltage, and the changing voltage recruits the following phase.

The graph follows one event from rest through threshold crossing, rapid depolarization, a peak, repolarization, and the return toward resting voltage. The steep rise shows that spike generation becomes rapid after threshold is reached. The falling side shows the membrane recovering through changing ion-channel activity. This waveform explains how a biological neuron creates one spike; in a spiking neural network, that event is represented as a discrete occurrence at a particular time.

**Question.** If membrane potential changes continuously, why is the output described as a discrete spike?

**Answer.** The membrane potential integrates incoming effects through continuous voltage changes, while threshold crossing selects a distinct output event. Once the voltage reaches threshold, the action-potential mechanism generates a rapid, recognizable pulse. A spiking model records the occurrence and timing of that pulse as a discrete spike. Continuous internal dynamics therefore determine when a discrete event is emitted.

The incoming effects that move a neuron toward or away from threshold arrive through **synapses**, the communication junctions between neurons. When an action potential reaches the transmitting side of a synapse, it causes a signal to influence the receiving neuron. This process is called **synaptic transmission**. The receiving neuron experiences a change in its membrane potential, and many such changes can combine over time. If their combined effect carries the membrane potential across threshold, the receiving neuron generates its own action potential.

Consider three neurons sending signals to one receiving neuron. One incoming event may cause only a small voltage change. Several events arriving close together can produce a larger accumulated change. When that accumulation reaches threshold, the receiving neuron emits a spike. Its action potential then travels to its own synapses, allowing the event to influence further neurons. A network-wide pattern grows from this repeated cycle: synaptic input changes membrane potential, threshold crossing initiates channel dynamics, and an action potential delivers a new synaptic signal.

Neural activity usually contains many action potentials distributed across time. This sequence is called a **spike train**:

Each narrow peak in the graph marks a separate action potential. The gaps show periods during which no spike occurs. One graph answers "What happens during a spike?" and the other answers "When do spikes occur?"

**Question.** Does a tall peak in a spike train automatically represent a stronger message?

**Answer.** Spike-based signaling centers on the occurrence and timing of action potentials. Once threshold initiates an action potential, the event has a characteristic waveform created by sodium- and potassium-channel activity. A sequence can convey changing activity through how frequently spikes occur and where they appear in time. Reading the train therefore begins by identifying discrete event times and the intervals between them.

The complete mental model is a loop. Synapses deliver influences from other neurons, those influences change membrane potential, and the membrane integrates them over time. Threshold crossing opens voltage-gated sodium channels and launches rapid depolarization. Changing sodium-channel behavior and opening potassium channels produce repolarization and recovery. The resulting action potential reaches synapses and affects the membrane potentials of other neurons. Repeated across a network, this loop turns continuously evolving membrane voltages into timed sequences of discrete spikes.
