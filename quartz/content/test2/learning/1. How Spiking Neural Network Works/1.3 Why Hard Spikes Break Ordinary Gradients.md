## Why Hard Spikes Break Ordinary Gradients

A neuron's membrane value can accumulate incoming signals over time until it crosses a threshold and emits a binary spike. Backpropagation through time, or BPTT, then tries to trace the final loss backward through those threshold decisions so that each weight can receive a useful update. The difficulty appears exactly where the membrane value becomes a spike: a tiny change in a weight may leave the spike unchanged, while another tiny change may suddenly move the neuron across threshold and flip its output.

Consider a neuron with membrane value $u$ and firing threshold $\theta$. Its spike $s$ can be written as
$$
S = H(u-\theta)
$$
Where $s$ is the binary output, $u$ is the membrane value, $\theta$ is the threshold, and $H$ is a hard step function. The function returns $0$ while $u<\theta$ and $1$ when $u\geq\theta$. This equation lets us compute whether the neuron fires at a particular time.

Imagine that $\theta=1$. Membrane values such as $0.90$, $0.95$, and $0.99$ all produce the same output, $s=0$. Once the value reaches $1.00$, the output jumps to $s=1$. The output has no gradual transition between silence and firing. That abrupt jump gives spikes their clear event-like character, but it also creates the training obstacle.

Gradient-based training relies on derivatives. A derivative measures how rapidly an output changes when an input changes by a very small amount. For the spike function, the derivative needed during backpropagation is
$$
\frac{\partial s}{\partial u}
$$
Here, $\partial s/\partial u$ measures the sensitivity of the spike $s$ to a small change in the membrane value $u$. Away from the threshold, moving $u$ slightly leaves the spike unchanged, so the derivative is zero:
$$
\frac{\partial s}{\partial u}=0 \qquad \text{for } u\neq\theta
$$
At the threshold, the output jumps abruptly from $0$ to $1$. There is no single finite slope that describes this vertical jump, so the derivative is undefined at $u=\theta$. The hard spike function is therefore non-differentiable at the threshold and has a zero derivative everywhere else.

This local derivative enters the chain of derivatives used to train an earlier weight. If a weight $w$ affects the membrane value $u$, which affects the spike $s$, which then affects the loss $L$, the chain rule gives
$$
\frac{\partial L}{\partial w}
=
\frac{\partial L}{\partial s}
\frac{\partial s}{\partial u}
\frac{\partial u}{\partial w}
$$
In this expression, $L$ is the training loss, $w$ is the weight being adjusted, $\partial L/\partial s$ measures how the loss responds to the spike, $\partial s/\partial u$ is the hard threshold's derivative, and $\partial u/\partial w$ measures how the weight changes the membrane value. The formula computes the direction and sensitivity needed to update $w$. When $\partial s/\partial u=0$, the entire product becomes zero even if changing the neuron's firing behavior could eventually reduce the loss. At the threshold, the required derivative is undefined, so ordinary differentiation still provides no stable update direction.

The same obstruction appears when the loss is viewed as a function of a weight. Suppose increasing $w$ raises the membrane value. Across a range of nearby weight values, the neuron remains below threshold and emits the same spike pattern, so the loss stays flat. Once $w$ becomes large enough to change a threshold crossing, the spike pattern changes suddenly and the loss jumps to another level. Repeating this across many neurons and time steps produces a stepped loss landscape: broad flat regions separated by abrupt boundaries.

The stepped landscape in the figure shows why an ordinary local gradient carries so little guidance. On a flat step, the local slope is zero, even when a lower-loss step lies nearby. At a sharp boundary, the slope is undefined. Gradient descent expects a surface whose local tilt indicates which small parameter change will move downhill, but the hard landscape supplies either a flat reading or an abrupt discontinuity.

Surrogate-gradient smoothing supplies the missing local direction. During the forward computation, the neuron still applies the hard threshold and produces a binary spike. During the backward computation, training uses the slope of a smooth approximation around the threshold in place of the unusable hard-step derivative. The smoothed curve in the figure turns the abrupt boundary into a region with a gradual slope, allowing descent to point toward parameter values associated with lower loss. The smooth function acts as a training signal while the forward spike remains a discrete event.

A useful analogy is walking down a wide staircase while sensing only the floor directly beneath your feet. In the middle of a step, the floor feels perfectly level, so local slope alone suggests no direction. A smoothed representation treats the staircase more like a ramp during the calculation of direction. The walker can then receive a downhill cue even while the actual structure still consists of steps.

**Question.** If the loss changes when a weight eventually causes a different spike, why can the gradient still be zero?

**Answer.** A gradient measures the effect of an infinitesimally small change at the current parameter value. While the neuron remains on the same side of its threshold, sufficiently small weight changes preserve the spike and therefore preserve the local loss. A larger change may reach a step boundary and alter the loss, but the zero derivative on the current flat region cannot reveal where that boundary lies.

**Question.** Does surrogate-gradient training replace spikes with continuous outputs?

**Answer.** The forward pass continues to generate binary spikes through the hard threshold. The smooth approximation is used during the backward pass to assign a usable sensitivity near threshold. The neuron therefore communicates through discrete events while learning through an approximate local slope.

The resulting mental model has three connected layers: a hard threshold turns membrane values into binary events, those events divide parameter space into flat regions separated by jumps, and the resulting zero or undefined derivatives obstruct ordinary gradient descent. Surrogate smoothing bridges the backward calculation across those jumps, giving BPTT a practical descent direction while preserving hard spikes in the network's forward dynamics.
