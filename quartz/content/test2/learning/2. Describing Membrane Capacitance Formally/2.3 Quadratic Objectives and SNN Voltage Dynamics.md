## Quadratic Objectives and SNN Voltage Dynamics

A leaky integrate-and-fire neuron already gives us a voltage that accumulates input, decays over time, and triggers an event at a threshold. We can now give that voltage a more formal computational meaning. Imagine several neurons jointly adjusting an output vector $y$ while trying to satisfy a set of requirements. Their voltages can measure how close those requirements are to being violated, and their spikes can provide brief corrections whenever the evolving output reaches a boundary.

Begin with an output vector
$$
Y =
\begin{bmatrix}
Y_1\\
\vdots\\
Y_M
\end{bmatrix}
\in \mathbb{R}^M
$$
Each component $y_j$ is one adjustable output or decision variable. Restricting the output to nonnegative values means
$$
Y_j \geq 0
\qquad \text{for every } j
$$
Or, more compactly, $y\geq 0$. Geometrically, each condition $y_j\geq 0$ removes the side of the solution space where that coordinate is negative. In two dimensions, $y_1\geq 0$ keeps the region to the right of the vertical axis, while $y_2\geq 0$ keeps the region above the horizontal axis. Their intersection is the quadrant in which both output components are allowed.

The network needs a rule for deciding which allowed output is preferable. A quadratic objective provides that rule:
$$
E(y)=\frac{\lambda}{2}y^\mathrm{T}y+b^\mathrm{T}y
$$
Here, $E(y)$ is the cost assigned to the candidate output $y$; $y^\mathrm{T}y=\sum_{j=1}^{M}y_j^2$ is its squared magnitude; $\lambda>0$ controls the curvature of the quadratic cost; and $b\in\mathbb{R}^M$ is a vector whose components tilt the objective toward particular values of $y$. The factor $1/2$ makes the derivative cleaner. This formula lets us compute how desirable any candidate output is: smaller values of $E$ correspond to better candidates.

For a one-dimensional example, let $\lambda=2$ and $b=-4$. Then
$$
E(y)=y^2-4y
$$
The graph is an upward-opening parabola whose minimum occurs at $y=2$. Since $2\geq 0$, the preferred value is also permitted by the nonnegative-output constraint. If the same parabola were tilted so that its unconstrained minimum occurred at $y=-2$, the nonnegative restriction would make $y=0$ the best permitted value. The objective determines the downhill direction, while the constraint determines how far that direction may be followed.

To move downhill continuously, differentiate the objective with respect to $y$. Because the gradient of $\frac{\lambda}{2}y^\mathrm{T}y$ is $\lambda y$, and the gradient of $b^\mathrm{T}y$ is $b$,
$$
\frac{\partial E}{\partial y}=\lambda y+b
$$
Gradient descent moves opposite this gradient:
$$
\dot y=-\frac{\partial E}{\partial y}
      =-\lambda y-b
$$
The symbol $\dot y=dy/dt$ is the rate at which the output changes over time. The term $-\lambda y$ pulls the output toward smaller magnitude, while $-b$ supplies a constant directional influence. Together they describe the unconstrained descent dynamics.

The output may also have to satisfy input-dependent linear inequalities. For an input $x\in\mathbb{R}^K$, write them as
$$
Fx-Gy\leq T
$$
Here, $F$ maps the input $x$ into the constraint space, $G$ maps the output $y$ into the same space, and $T\in\mathbb{R}^N$ contains one boundary value for each of the $N$ constraints. The $i$-th row produces
$$
G_i^\mathrm{T}y\geq F_i^\mathrm{T}x-T_i
$$
Where $G_i^\mathrm{T}$ and $F_i^\mathrm{T}$ are the $i$-th rows of $G$ and $F$, and $T_i$ is the corresponding threshold. Each inequality divides the output space into a feasible half-plane, where the inequality is satisfied, and an infeasible half-plane, where it is violated. The complete feasible region is the intersection of all feasible half-planes, together with any nonnegative coordinate restrictions placed on $y$.

The figure shows how several inequalities carve a shared permitted region out of the $y$-space. Crossing any one boundary enters the infeasible half-plane for that constraint. A valid optimization trajectory therefore follows the quadratic objective while staying inside the intersection, or receives a correction that returns it when it reaches a boundary. Changing $x$ shifts the boundaries because the right-hand side $F_i^\mathrm{T}x-T_i$ changes, so the feasible region itself can move with the input.

A boundary correction can be added to the descent dynamics:
$$
\dot y=-\lambda y-b+Ds(t)
$$
The vector $s(t)\in\mathbb{R}^N$ contains brief delta-like events associated with boundary contacts, and $D\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times N}$ contains the directions in which those events move $y$. Choosing the correction directions orthogonal to the touched boundaries gives $D\propto G$. A spike can therefore be interpreted as a discrete event that redirects the continuous optimization trajectory when a constraint becomes active.

**Question.** Why are spikes represented by $s(t)$ instead of another smooth force?

**Answer.** Boundary contact happens at particular times. A delta-like event captures a rapid correction concentrated at such a time, while the terms $-\lambda y-b$ continue to govern the smooth motion between contacts. This division matches an SNN's combination of continuous voltage evolution and discrete spikes.

The connection to voltage appears by defining the constraint residual as
$$
V=Fx-Gy\leq T
$$
The vector $V\in\mathbb{R}^N$ now represents neuronal voltages, and $T$ represents their thresholds. A component $V_i$ approaching $T_i$ means that the corresponding inequality is approaching its boundary. Differentiating gives
$$
\dot V=F\dot x-G\dot y
$$
Substituting the corrected output dynamics yields
$$
\dot V
=F\dot x+\lambda Gy+Gb-GDs(t)
$$
Because $V=Fx-Gy$, we have $Gy=Fx-V$. Substituting this identity produces
$$
\dot V
=-\lambda V+F(\lambda x+\dot x)+Gb-GDs(t)
$$
Every term now has a recognizable role. The term $-\lambda V$ is voltage leak, $F(\lambda x+\dot x)$ carries the input and its change into the neurons, $Gb$ reflects the linear part of the objective, and $-GDs(t)$ describes spike-triggered interactions that correct the represented output at active boundaries.

**Question.** What does a threshold crossing mean in the optimization picture?

**Answer.** It means one linear inequality has reached the edge of feasibility. The corresponding spike applies a directed correction through $D$ and changes the voltages through $-GD s(t)$, allowing the network's trajectory to continue evolving under the objective while respecting the constrained region.

The resulting mental model joins the pieces into one process: the quadratic objective creates a smooth downhill tendency, nonnegativity and linear inequalities carve out the permissible outputs, voltages encode proximity to those boundaries, and spikes act as precisely timed corrections. Membrane-voltage dynamics can therefore be read as the dynamics of a constrained optimization process unfolding continuously in time.
