The network voltage state developed earlier gives a compact description of how close each neuron is to emitting a spike. That state can also be viewed geometrically. Imagine a point moving through a landscape while walls restrict where it may go. The landscape assigns a cost to every position, and the walls encode the conditions under which the neurons remain below threshold. Spiking computation can then be interpreted as a search for the lowest-cost position that stays inside the allowed region.

To make this picture precise, begin with a single number $x$. A simple bowl-shaped cost has the form
$$
E(x)=\frac{1}{2}q x^2+c x
$$
Where $E(x)$ is the cost assigned to the value $x$, $q>0$ controls the curvature of the bowl, and $c$ tilts the bowl toward one side. The factor $1/2$ makes the derivative especially simple:
$$
\frac{dE}{dx}=qx+c
$$
Setting this slope to zero locates the bottom of the bowl:
$$
Qx+c=0
\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
X^*=-\frac{c}{q}
$$
Here, $x^*$ is the unconstrained optimizer: the value that minimizes the cost when every value of $x$ is allowed. The derivative also gives a direction of improvement. If $qx+c$ is positive, decreasing $x$ lowers the cost; if it is negative, increasing $x$ lowers the cost. The state therefore moves downhill toward $x^*$.

A neural network contains many interacting quantities, so the scalar $x$ becomes a vector $\mathbf{x}$. Each component of $\mathbf{x}$ can represent one coordinate of the network's optimization state. The bowl-shaped cost becomes the **quadratic objective**
$$
E(\mathbf{x})
=
\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{x}^{\mathsf T}Q\mathbf{x}
+
\mathbf{c}^{\mathsf T}\mathbf{x}
$$
The vector $\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^n$ is the optimization variable, $n$ is the number of state coordinates, $Q\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}$ is a matrix that determines the curvature and orientation of the objective, $\mathbf{c}\in\mathbb{R}^n$ supplies the linear tilt, and $\mathbf{x}^{\mathsf T}$ denotes the transpose of $\mathbf{x}$. The value $E(\mathbf{x})$ is a single number measuring the cost of the current state.

When $Q$ is positive definite, the objective forms a multidimensional bowl with one lowest point. Its gradient is
$$
\nabla E(\mathbf{x})=Q\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{c}
$$
Where $\nabla E(\mathbf{x})$ is the vector of slopes in all coordinate directions. The downhill direction is $-\nabla E(\mathbf{x})$. If every state were allowed, the optimizer would satisfy
$$
Q\mathbf{x}^*+\mathbf{c}=\mathbf{0}
$$
So an invertible $Q$ gives
$$
\mathbf{x}^*=-Q^{-1}\mathbf{c}
$$
The matrix $Q$ shapes the geometry of the bowl. A diagonal $Q$ produces curvature aligned with the coordinate axes, while off-diagonal entries couple coordinates and rotate the bowl's contours. The vector $\mathbf{c}$ shifts the location toward which the unconstrained state tends to move.

Spiking thresholds introduce restrictions on that motion. A collection of linear inequalities can be written compactly as
$$
A\mathbf{x}\leq \mathbf{b}
$$
The matrix $A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}$ contains one constraint per row, $m$ is the number of constraints, and $\mathbf{b}\in\mathbb{R}^m$ contains their bounds. For row $i$, the inequality is
$$
\mathbf{a}_i^{\mathsf T}\mathbf{x}\leq b_i
$$
Where $\mathbf{a}_i^{\mathsf T}$ is row $i$ of $A$, and $b_i$ is the corresponding entry of $\mathbf{b}$. In two dimensions, the equality $\mathbf{a}_i^{\mathsf T}\mathbf{x}=b_i$ draws a line. In three dimensions, it draws a plane. The inequality selects one side of that boundary, called a **constraint half-plane** in two dimensions or a half-space in higher dimensions.

The overlap of all allowed half-planes or half-spaces is the **feasible region**. Constrained quadratic optimization asks for the point with the smallest objective value among all points in this region:
$$
\underset{\mathbf{x}}{\operatorname{minimize}}
\quad
\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{x}^{\mathsf T}Q\mathbf{x}
+
\mathbf{c}^{\mathsf T}\mathbf{x}
\qquad
\text{subject to}
\qquad
A\mathbf{x}\leq\mathbf{b}
$$
This expression computes the best allowed state: the state that minimizes the quadratic cost while satisfying every linear threshold condition.

The curved objective in the figure supplies a downhill direction toward its unconstrained minimum. The flat constraint boundaries cut away portions of the space, leaving their shared feasible region as the set of allowable states. While the downhill direction remains inside that region, the state can follow it directly. When the state reaches a boundary, the same downhill direction may point partly outside the allowed region. The boundary then changes the permitted motion: improvement must continue through directions that remain feasible. In the spiking interpretation, reaching such a threshold boundary is associated with a spike event, which changes the network state and allows the optimization process to continue within the constraints.

For a concrete two-dimensional example, take
$$
E(x_1,x_2)=\frac{1}{2}(x_1^2+x_2^2)-2x_1-x_2
$$
Here, $\mathbf{x}=(x_1,x_2)^{\mathsf T}$, $Q$ is the $2\times2$ identity matrix, and $\mathbf{c}=(-2,-1)^{\mathsf T}$. The unconstrained optimizer is
$$
\mathbf{x}^*=-Q^{-1}\mathbf{c}=(2,1)^{\mathsf T}
$$
Now impose the constraint
$$
X_1+x_2\leq 2
$$
The point $(2,1)$ lies beyond the boundary because $2+1=3$. The optimizer must therefore lie somewhere in the half-plane satisfying $x_1+x_2\leq2$. The objective still pulls toward $(2,1)$, while the constraint limits how far that pull can carry the state. The best feasible point occurs on the boundary, where the cost contours first touch the allowed region. Geometrically, the unconstrained bowl chooses the preferred direction, and the constraint geometry determines how much of that direction is available.

**Question.** Does every constraint boundary cause a spike at every moment?

**Answer.** A boundary becomes active when the evolving state reaches it. Constraints with remaining slack still permit motion without an event. If $\mathbf{a}_i^{\mathsf T}\mathbf{x}<b_i$, constraint $i$ has slack equal to $b_i-\mathbf{a}_i^{\mathsf T}\mathbf{x}$. As this slack approaches zero, the corresponding state approaches its threshold. Reaching zero marks contact with the boundary and provides the geometric condition associated with an event.

**Question.** Why use a quadratic objective instead of measuring distance with an ordinary straight line?

**Answer.** A quadratic objective gives a smooth bowl whose gradient specifies a direction of improvement at every state. The matrix $Q$ can weight different coordinates and represent couplings between them, while the linear term $\mathbf{c}$ locates the preferred operating point. These features turn the network state into a structured optimization problem whose motion can be interpreted continuously between discrete spike events.

The resulting mental model combines three objects. The quadratic objective is the landscape that pulls the state downhill, the linear inequalities form walls around the allowed states, and the feasible region is the space in which computation proceeds. Membrane voltage indicates how close the state is to one of those walls. A spike marks contact with a threshold boundary, after which the network continues its search under the same geometric restrictions. Spiking computation therefore becomes a trajectory through a constrained landscape: continuous movement reflects improvement of the objective, and discrete events occur when that movement encounters the limits defining feasible neural states.
