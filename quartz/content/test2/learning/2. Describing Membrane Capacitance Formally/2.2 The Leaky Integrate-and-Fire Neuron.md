## The Leaky Integrate-and-Fire Neuron

A spike train tells us *when* a neuron communicates, while weighted synaptic input tells us how strongly incoming spikes affect it. We now need a rule that connects those ideas through membrane voltage. Imagine several weak input spikes arriving at slightly different times. Each one nudges the membrane voltage upward, but the effect gradually fades. If enough input accumulates before fading away, the voltage reaches a firing threshold, the neuron emits a spike, and its membrane voltage resets so the process can begin again. The **leaky integrate-and-fire neuron**, often shortened to **LIF neuron**, turns this sequence into a compact mathematical model.

Begin with integration. At discrete timestep $t$, the neuron receives a total weighted input $I[t]$. This quantity combines the incoming spike values with their synaptic weights. A positive contribution tends to raise the membrane voltage, while a negative contribution tends to lower it. If the neuron simply accumulated every contribution forever, then even widely separated inputs could eventually force it to fire. A membrane instead has capacitance and loses stored charge over time, so earlier input should have less influence than recent input. The LIF model captures this fading memory through **leakage**.

Let $u[t]$ denote the membrane voltage at timestep $t$. Before considering spike emission, its update can be written as
$$
U[t] = \beta u[t-1] + I[t]
$$
Here:

- $u[t]$ is the updated membrane voltage at timestep $t$;
- $u[t-1]$ is the voltage retained from the previous timestep;
- $I[t]$ is the total weighted synaptic input arriving at timestep $t$;
- $\beta$ is the leakage factor, usually chosen between $0$ and $1$.

The term $\beta u[t-1]$ represents the part of the earlier voltage that survives into the current timestep. The term $I[t]$ adds the new input. Together, they let us compute the neuron's present voltage from its previous state and its current input.

The value of $\beta$ controls the neuron's memory. If $\beta=0.9$, then 90 percent of the previous voltage remains after one timestep. If no new input arrives, a voltage of $1.0$ becomes $0.9$, then $0.81$, then $0.729$. Each step multiplies the remaining voltage by the same retention factor. A larger $\beta$ produces slower decay, allowing inputs separated by several timesteps to combine more strongly. A smaller $\beta$ produces faster decay, so firing depends more heavily on recent input.

Consider a neuron with $\beta=0.8$, an initial voltage $u[0]=0$, and three consecutive inputs $I[1]=0.4$, $I[2]=0.4$, and $I[3]=0.4$. Its voltage evolves as
$$
U[1] = 0.8(0)+0.4=0.4
$$
$$
U[2] = 0.8(0.4)+0.4=0.72
$$
$$
U[3] = 0.8(0.72)+0.4=0.976
$$
The neuron integrates the repeated inputs, while leakage reduces the contribution carried from each earlier timestep. The first input still affects $u[3]$, though its influence has been multiplied by $\beta$ twice. The voltage therefore acts like a short, gradually fading memory of recent synaptic activity.

Integration and leakage describe the internal state, but a spiking neuron also needs a rule for communication. Introduce a **firing threshold** $\theta$, a voltage level that triggers an output spike. The output at timestep $t$ can be represented by a binary variable $s[t]$:
$$
S[t] =
\begin{cases}
1, & \text{if } u[t] \geq \theta,\\
0, & \text{if } u[t] < \theta.
\end{cases}
$$
Here, $s[t]=1$ means that the neuron emits a spike at timestep $t$, while $s[t]=0$ means that it remains silent. The threshold $\theta$ determines how much membrane voltage must accumulate before firing. Reading the equation requires two stages of thought: first compute the voltage from retained state and new input, then compare that voltage with the threshold.

Suppose the previous example uses $\theta=0.9$. The voltages $0.4$ and $0.72$ remain below threshold, so $s[1]=0$ and $s[2]=0$. At the third timestep, the voltage reaches $0.976$, which crosses the threshold, so $s[3]=1$. Several individually weak inputs have combined into one output event. The spike reports that the accumulated voltage has reached the firing condition; the precise subthreshold values remain internal to the neuron.

**Question.** Does an input spike immediately cause the LIF neuron to emit an output spike?

**Answer.** An input spike contributes to the membrane voltage through its synaptic weight. The neuron emits an output spike only when the updated voltage reaches or exceeds the firing threshold. A strong input may cause immediate firing, while several weaker inputs may need to accumulate across nearby timesteps. Leakage determines how much of each earlier contribution remains available during that accumulation.

After emission, the neuron needs a **membrane reset**. Without a reset, a voltage that has crossed threshold could remain high and repeatedly trigger spikes even before sufficient new input accumulates. Resetting begins a fresh integration cycle after each emitted spike. Using a reset voltage $u_{\mathrm{reset}}$, the complete dynamics can be expressed as a piecewise rule:
$$
U[t] =
\begin{cases}
\beta u[t-1] + I[t], & \text{if } \beta u[t-1] + I[t] < \theta,\\
U_{\mathrm{reset}}, & \text{if } \beta u[t-1] + I[t] \geq \theta,
\end{cases}
$$
With
$$
S[t] =
\begin{cases}
0, & \text{if } \beta u[t-1] + I[t] < \theta,\\
1, & \text{if } \beta u[t-1] + I[t] \geq \theta.
\end{cases}
$$
In these equations, $\beta u[t-1]+I[t]$ is the candidate voltage produced by leakage and integration, $\theta$ is the firing threshold, $s[t]$ records whether a spike occurs, and $u_{\mathrm{reset}}$ is the voltage assigned after firing. The two branches describe **threshold-reset dynamics**: subthreshold activity updates the stored voltage, while threshold crossing emits a spike and places the voltage at its reset level.

A clear way to execute the rule is to compute an intermediate candidate voltage,
$$
\tilde{u}[t]=\beta u[t-1]+I[t]
$$
Where $\tilde{u}[t]$ is the voltage reached before the firing decision. Compare $\tilde{u}[t]$ with $\theta$. If it remains below threshold, set $s[t]=0$ and store $u[t]=\tilde{u}[t]$. If it reaches threshold, set $s[t]=1$ and store $u[t]=u_{\mathrm{reset}}$. This intermediate quantity keeps the order of events explicit: leak, integrate, compare, emit, and reset.

For example, let $\beta=0.8$, $\theta=1.0$, $u_{\mathrm{reset}}=0$, and $u[t-1]=0.7$. If the current input is $I[t]=0.2$, then
$$
\tilde{u}[t]=0.8(0.7)+0.2=0.76
$$
The candidate voltage remains below threshold, so the neuron emits no spike and stores $u[t]=0.76$. If the current input is instead $I[t]=0.5$, then
$$
\tilde{u}[t]=0.8(0.7)+0.5=1.06
$$
The candidate voltage crosses threshold, so the neuron emits $s[t]=1$ and resets its stored voltage to $u[t]=0$.

**Question.** Where does the voltage that exceeded the threshold go after a spike?

**Answer.** The threshold crossing determines that a spike occurs, and the reset rule then assigns the membrane its post-spike value. In the piecewise form above, the stored state becomes $u_{\mathrm{reset}}$. The emitted spike carries the firing event forward through the spike train, while the reset prepares the neuron to integrate later inputs.

The LIF neuron can therefore be pictured as a leaky container with an event detector. Weighted input pours voltage into the container, leakage drains a fraction between timesteps, and the threshold marks the level at which the neuron emits a spike. Reset returns the container to its starting level after that event. This single cycle-retain part of the past, add present input, test the threshold, emit when reached, and reset-connects membrane capacitance to a discrete spike train through a manageable computational rule.
