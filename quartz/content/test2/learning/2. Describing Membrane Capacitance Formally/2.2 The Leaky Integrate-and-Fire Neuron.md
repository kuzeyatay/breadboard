## The Leaky Integrate-and-Fire Neuron

A membrane potential gives us a running measure of a neuron's electrical state, while an action potential turns that state into a discrete spike. The remaining question is how the neuron moves from one state to the next. Imagine that several incoming spikes arrive close together. Each arrival pushes the membrane potential upward. Meanwhile, the effect of earlier arrivals gradually fades. If the combined push becomes large enough, the neuron emits its own spike and returns to a lower voltage so that accumulation can begin again. The leaky integrate-and-fire neuron expresses this entire cycle with a small set of update rules.

Begin with integration. Here, *integration* means accumulating the effects of inputs over time. Suppose the membrane potential at the previous timestep is $V_{t-1}$, and an input arrives during the current timestep $t$. A perfectly persistent accumulator would carry all of $V_{t-1}$ forward before adding the new input. A leaky membrane retains only a fraction of that earlier voltage. We describe the retained amount as
$$
\lambda V_{t-1}
$$
Where $\lambda$ is the **leak factor**, a number between $0$ and $1$. A value of $\lambda$ close to $1$ means that voltage fades slowly, giving the neuron a longer memory of recent inputs. A smaller value means that voltage fades quickly, so inputs must arrive closer together to have a strong combined effect.

For example, let $V_{t-1}=0.6$ and $\lambda=0.8$. Before considering any new input, the neuron carries forward
$$
0.8 \times 0.6 = 0.48
$$
The stored voltage has fallen from $0.6$ to $0.48$ during one timestep. If no further input arrives, repeated updates produce $0.384$, then $0.3072$, and so on. The sequence traces an electrical memory that gradually decays.

The incoming contribution depends on both the input and the strength of its connection to the neuron. Let $X_t$ represent the input at timestep $t$, and let $W$ represent the corresponding synaptic weight. A weight controls how strongly the input changes the receiving neuron's voltage. Their product,
$$
WX_t
$$
Is the input-driven voltage change. For a neuron receiving several inputs, $X_t$ can be a vector containing all current input values, while $W$ can contain their weights. The product then combines the weighted contributions into the total drive received at that timestep.

Putting retained voltage and new input together gives the membrane update before spike handling:
$$
U_t=\lambda V_{t-1}+WX_t
$$
Here, $U_t$ is the candidate membrane potential at timestep $t$, $V_{t-1}$ is the membrane potential carried from the previous timestep, $\lambda$ is the leak factor, $X_t$ is the current input, and $W$ determines the input's influence. This formula computes how high the voltage reaches after leakage and input accumulation have both acted.

Consider a neuron with $\lambda=0.8$, $V_{t-1}=0.6$, $W=0.5$, and $X_t=1$. Its candidate voltage is
$$
U_t=(0.8)(0.6)+(0.5)(1)=0.48+0.5=0.98
$$
The two terms have distinct meanings. The value $0.48$ is the surviving electrical state from the recent past, while $0.5$ is the change caused by the present input. Their sum shows how the neuron combines memory with current evidence.

Accumulation becomes spike generation when the voltage reaches a **firing threshold**. Let $V_{\mathrm{th}}$ denote this threshold voltage. The neuron's spike at time $t$ is represented by the binary variable $S_t$:
$$
S_t=
\begin{cases}
1, & \text{if } U_t\geq V_{\mathrm{th}},\\
0, & \text{if } U_t<V_{\mathrm{th}}.
\end{cases}
$$
The value $S_t=1$ means that the neuron emits a spike, while $S_t=0$ means that it remains silent. The threshold converts a continuously changing membrane potential into a discrete event. In the numerical example, a threshold of $V_{\mathrm{th}}=1$ produces $S_t=0$ because $0.98<1$. One additional input on the following timestep may then push the neuron across the threshold, depending on how much voltage survives the leak.

**Question.** Why can several individually weak inputs produce a spike?

**Answer.** Each input adds to voltage that may still remain from earlier timesteps. If inputs arrive quickly enough, leakage removes only part of the accumulated voltage between arrivals. Their weighted effects can therefore build until the candidate potential $U_t$ reaches $V_{\mathrm{th}}$. Inputs spaced farther apart allow more decay, so the same set of input strengths may leave the neuron below threshold.

After a spike, the membrane must begin a new accumulation cycle. One compact update combines leakage, input, spike emission, and a threshold-sized reduction:
$$
V_t=\lambda V_{t-1}+WX_t-S_tV_{\mathrm{th}}
$$
Here, $V_t$ is the membrane potential stored for the current timestep; all other symbols keep their previous meanings. When $S_t=0$, the final term vanishes, leaving the ordinary leaky integration rule. When $S_t=1$, the quantity $V_{\mathrm{th}}$ is subtracted from the voltage. This form performs a **soft reset** because any voltage above one threshold remains available after the spike.

Suppose the candidate voltage is $U_t=1.3$ and the threshold is $V_{\mathrm{th}}=1$. The neuron emits a spike and stores
$$
V_t=1.3-1=0.3
$$
The remaining $0.3$ records the amount by which the integrated input exceeded the threshold. This is similar to filling a measuring cup to $1.3$ units, pouring out exactly $1$ unit when it becomes full, and retaining the extra $0.3$.

A second reset convention sends the voltage directly to a chosen reset potential $V_r$:
$$
V_t=
\begin{cases}
U_t, & \text{if } U_t<V_{\mathrm{th}},\\
V_r, & \text{if } U_t\geq V_{\mathrm{th}}.
\end{cases}
$$
The symbol $V_r$ is the membrane potential immediately after spiking. This rule performs a **hard reset**: crossing the threshold causes a spike and places the neuron at the specified reset state. If $V_r=0$, a candidate voltage of $1.3$ becomes $0$ after firing. Both reset forms enforce the same cycle of integration, threshold crossing, spike emission, and renewed accumulation, while preserving different amounts of the pre-spike voltage.

**Question.** Does the firing threshold describe the size of the outgoing spike?

**Answer.** The threshold decides whether a spike occurs. The output variable $S_t$ records the event as either $0$ or $1$, while the membrane potential carries the accumulated internal state. A larger candidate voltage can affect the reset state under a soft reset, but spike emission itself remains a binary event in this model.

The complete mental model is a leaky container checked at regular timesteps. Earlier voltage survives in the fraction $\lambda V_{t-1}$, current weighted input adds $WX_t$, and their sum produces the candidate voltage $U_t$. The threshold test converts that evolving state into a spike whenever $U_t$ reaches $V_{\mathrm{th}}$. Resetting then lowers the voltage, preparing the same neuron to integrate another sequence of inputs. A spike train emerges from repeating this stateful competition among incoming drive, fading memory, threshold crossing, and reset.
