## The Hodgkin-Huxley Membrane Equation

The phases of an action potential describe how membrane voltage rises, falls, and returns toward rest. To explain what drives those changes, imagine watching a tiny patch of neuronal membrane while current is injected into it. Some of that current briefly accumulates electrical charge across the membrane, while the rest flows through ion channels. The voltage changes according to how the incoming current is divided between those two destinations. The Hodgkin-Huxley model expresses this division as a membrane current balance.

A cell membrane separates charged fluid inside the neuron from charged fluid outside it. Because the membrane keeps these charges apart, it behaves like a capacitor: a component that stores separated charge. Its **membrane capacitance**, written $C_m$, measures how much charge must be moved to change the membrane voltage by a given amount. If $V(t)$ is the membrane voltage at time $t$, the current used to change the stored charge is
$$
I_C(t)=C_m\frac{dV}{dt}
$$
Here, $I_C(t)$ is the capacitive current, $C_m$ is the membrane capacitance, and $\frac{dV}{dt}$ is the rate at which membrane voltage changes with time. The formula lets us compute how much current is charging or discharging the membrane at any instant. A large positive value of $\frac{dV}{dt}$ means that the voltage is rising rapidly; a value near zero means that the voltage is nearly steady.

Capacitance also explains why membrane voltage cannot jump instantaneously in this model. Moving the voltage requires moving charge, and moving charge requires current over time. A membrane with a larger capacitance needs more current, or more time, to undergo the same voltage change. As an analogy, capacitance plays a role similar to the size of a storage tank: a larger tank changes level more slowly when supplied at the same flow rate.

At the same time, ion channels provide pathways through which charge crosses the membrane. The ease with which a pathway carries current is its **conductance**. Hodgkin-Huxley dynamics track three ionic contributions: a potassium current $I_K$, a sodium current $I_{Na}$, and a leak current $I_L$. The leak current collects the background flow through channels whose combined conductance is treated as approximately steady.

With an applied current $I(t)$, conservation of charge gives the membrane current balance
$$
I(t)
=
C_m\frac{dV}{dt}
+
I_K
+
I_{Na}
+
I_L
$$
Every unit of applied current must appear either as capacitive current that changes stored charge or as ionic current that crosses the membrane. Solving for the voltage-change rate gives
$$
C_m\frac{dV}{dt}
=
I(t)-I_K-I_{Na}-I_L
$$
And therefore
$$
\frac{dV}{dt}
=
\frac{I(t)-I_K-I_{Na}-I_L}{C_m}
$$
In these equations, $I(t)$ is the current delivered to the membrane, $C_m$ is its capacitance, $V$ is membrane voltage, and $I_K$, $I_{Na}$, and $I_L$ are the potassium, sodium, and leak currents. The final expression computes the instantaneous slope of the voltage. When the applied current exceeds the total outward ionic current, $\frac{dV}{dt}$ is positive and the voltage rises. When the ionic currents dominate, $\frac{dV}{dt}$ is negative and the voltage falls. When the two sides balance, the voltage remains momentarily steady.

The equivalent circuit turns each term in the equation into a physical pathway. The capacitor represents charge stored across the membrane and corresponds to $C_m\frac{dV}{dt}$. The potassium, sodium, and leak branches correspond to $I_K$, $I_{Na}$, and $I_L$. All branches share the same membrane voltage because they connect the same interior and exterior sides of the membrane. The applied current reaches their common junction and divides among them, exactly as the current-balance equation requires.

Each ionic branch contains a conductance together with a voltage source. The voltage source represents the ion's **reversal potential**, the membrane voltage at which that ionic pathway carries zero net current. For an ionic species $x$, the current can be written
$$
I_x=g_x(V-E_x)
$$
Where $I_x$ is the ionic current, $g_x$ is the pathway's conductance, $V$ is the current membrane voltage, and $E_x$ is the reversal potential. The difference $V-E_x$ is the electrical driving force. This formula computes ionic current by multiplying how open the pathway is, represented by conductance, by how strongly the present voltage drives charge relative to the pathway's reversal potential.

A concrete interpretation helps. Suppose $V$ lies close to $E_K$. Then $V-E_K$ is small, so the potassium current is small even when potassium conductance is substantial. If $V$ moves farther from $E_K$, the driving force grows and more potassium current flows through the same conductance. If $V$ reaches $E_K$, the driving force becomes zero and the net potassium current vanishes at that instant.

Substituting the three ionic currents into the balance produces
$$
C_m\frac{dV}{dt}
=
I(t)
-
G_K(V-E_K)
-
G_{Na}(V-E_{Na})
-
G_L(V-E_L)
$$
Here, $g_K$, $g_{Na}$, and $g_L$ are the potassium, sodium, and leak conductances, while $E_K$, $E_{Na}$, and $E_L$ are their reversal potentials. The leak conductance $g_L$ is treated as fixed. The potassium and sodium conductances change as voltage-sensitive channel gates open and close, allowing the same membrane voltage to produce different currents at different moments.

The Hodgkin-Huxley model represents those changing conductances as
$$
G_K=\bar g_K n^4,
\qquad
G_{Na}=\bar g_{Na}m^3h
$$
The symbols $\bar g_K$ and $\bar g_{Na}$ are the maximum potassium and sodium conductances. The variables $n$, $m$, and $h$ describe the states of channel gates: $n$ controls potassium activation, $m$ controls sodium activation, and $h$ controls sodium inactivation. Their powers combine several gating requirements into one effective conductance. As the gate variables evolve, they change the ionic currents, and those currents change $\frac{dV}{dt}$.

**Question.** Why can a large ionic conductance still produce only a small ionic current?

**Answer.** Ionic current depends on both conductance and driving force: $I_x=g_x(V-E_x)$. A large $g_x$ provides an easy pathway, but a membrane voltage close to $E_x$ gives that pathway little electrical drive. Current becomes small as $V$ approaches the reversal potential and reaches zero when $V=E_x$.

**Question.** What determines whether membrane voltage rises or falls at a particular instant?

**Answer.** The balance between applied current and the summed ionic currents determines the sign of $\frac{dV}{dt}$. Excess applied current charges the membrane capacitor and raises $V$. A larger combined potassium, sodium, and leak current discharges it and lowers $V$. Capacitance sets how quickly that imbalance changes the voltage.

The resulting mental model is an electrical junction with one storage branch and several ion-flow branches. Incoming current divides among them. The capacitor converts any leftover current into voltage change, while each ionic pathway draws current according to its conductance and its distance from reversal potential. Voltage alters the channel conductances, the altered conductances reshape the ionic currents, and the new current balance determines the voltage's next movement.
