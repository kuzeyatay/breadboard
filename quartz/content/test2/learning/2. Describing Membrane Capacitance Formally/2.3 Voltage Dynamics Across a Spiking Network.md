A single leaky integrate-and-fire neuron has a membrane voltage that changes as incoming current charges its membrane capacitance while leakage gradually removes charge. In a network, every neuron follows this same local principle, but its incoming current now depends partly on spikes emitted by other neurons. The network therefore evolves as a coupled system: each voltage influences future spikes, and those spikes influence future voltages throughout the network.

Suppose an SNN contains $N$ neurons. Instead of writing one voltage variable for each neuron in separate equations, collect all membrane voltages into a **network voltage state**
$$
\mathbf{v}_t =
\begin{bmatrix}
V_{1,t}\\
V_{2,t}\\
\vdots\\
V_{N,t}
\end{bmatrix}
$$
Here, $v_{i,t}$ is the membrane voltage of neuron $i$ at time $t$, and $\mathbf{v}_t$ is an $N$-component vector describing the electrical state of the whole network at that instant. Knowing this vector gives a snapshot of how close every neuron is to producing its next spike.

The role of membrane capacitance becomes clearer when time is divided into short intervals of duration $\Delta t$. During one interval, the change in voltage equals the net current multiplied by the interval length and divided by capacitance. For a network whose neurons share a membrane capacitance $C$, a useful voltage update is
$$
C\frac{\mathbf{v}_{t+\Delta t}-\mathbf{v}_t}{\Delta t}
=
-g_L\left(\mathbf{v}_t-v_{\mathrm{rest}}\mathbf{1}\right)
+\mathbf{W}\mathbf{r}_t
+\mathbf{B}\mathbf{x}_t
-\mathbf{R}\mathbf{z}_t
$$
The left-hand side describes capacitive charging. The vector $\mathbf{v}_{t+\Delta t}-\mathbf{v}_t$ is the voltage change during the interval, so dividing by $\Delta t$ gives the rate of change. Multiplying that rate by the capacitance $C$ gives the current required to produce it.

Each term on the right-hand side contributes current. The parameter $g_L$ is the leak conductance, which controls how strongly voltages drift toward the resting voltage $v_{\mathrm{rest}}$. The vector $\mathbf{1}$ contains $N$ ones, allowing the scalar resting voltage to be compared with every component of $\mathbf{v}_t$. The vector $\mathbf{x}_t$ contains signals arriving from outside the recurrent network, while the matrix $\mathbf{B}$ contains the weights that route those signals to particular neurons. The vector $\mathbf{r}_t$ represents synaptic activity produced by earlier spikes, and the matrix $\mathbf{W}$ contains the recurrent connection weights between neurons. Finally, $\mathbf{z}_t$ records which neurons spike during the current interval, and $\mathbf{R}\mathbf{z}_t$ represents the spike-dependent change applied to their voltage states. The matrix $\mathbf{R}$ determines the size and placement of that change.

Solving the equation for the future voltage produces an explicit update:
$$
\mathbf{v}_{t+\Delta t}
=
\mathbf{v}_t
+
\frac{\Delta t}{C}
\left[
-g_L\left(\mathbf{v}_t-v_{\mathrm{rest}}\mathbf{1}\right)
+\mathbf{W}\mathbf{r}_t
+\mathbf{B}\mathbf{x}_t
-\mathbf{R}\mathbf{z}_t
\right]
$$
This form lets us compute the next network voltage state from the present state, recurrent synaptic activity, external input, and current spikes. Capacitance controls the scale of the update. A larger $C$ produces a smaller voltage change from the same net current during the same interval, much as a larger container requires more added material to raise its level by a given amount.

The recurrent term $\mathbf{W}\mathbf{r}_t$ is where separate neuron equations become a coupled network equation. The entry $W_{ij}$ describes how synaptic activity from neuron $j$ affects neuron $i$. The recurrent current received by neuron $i$ is therefore
$$
\left(\mathbf{W}\mathbf{r}_t\right)_i
=
\sum_{j=1}^{N} W_{ij}r_{j,t}
$$
Where $r_{j,t}$ is the current synaptic activity associated with neuron $j$. This sum combines all recurrent contributions arriving at neuron $i$. A positive weight can raise its voltage through an excitatory contribution, while a negative weight can lower it through an inhibitory contribution.

Consider two neurons with
$$
\mathbf{W}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
0 & 0.4\\
0.7 & 0
\end{bmatrix},
\qquad
\mathbf{r}_t
=
\begin{bmatrix}
1\\
0
\end{bmatrix}
$$
The product is
$$
\mathbf{W}\mathbf{r}_t
=
\begin{bmatrix}
0\\
0.7
\end{bmatrix}
$$
Neuron 1 currently contributes synaptic activity, so neuron 2 receives a recurrent drive of $0.7$. Neuron 1 receives zero recurrent drive during this interval because neuron 2 contributes no synaptic activity. If that drive raises neuron 2 enough to spike later, neuron 2 can then send an effect back to neuron 1. Recurrent coupling creates this continuing chain in which earlier events reshape the conditions for later events.

The external-input term has the same matrix-vector structure. If $\mathbf{x}_t$ contains readings from sensors or activity from a preceding layer, then $\mathbf{B}\mathbf{x}_t$ converts those readings into currents for the $N$ neurons. Recurrent input carries the network's own recent activity forward, whereas external input injects information from outside the recurrent population. Both enter the same capacitive balance and jointly determine how the voltage vector moves.

**Question.** Why does recurrent coupling use a matrix rather than a single weight?

**Answer.** A network can contain a different connection strength for every ordered pair of neurons. The matrix entry $W_{ij}$ stores the effect of neuron $j$ on neuron $i$. Multiplying $\mathbf{W}$ by $\mathbf{r}_t$ computes all incoming recurrent contributions for all neurons at once.

A common confusion comes from treating $\mathbf{z}_t$ and $\mathbf{r}_t$ as interchangeable. The spike vector $\mathbf{z}_t$ records discrete firing events during a particular interval. Synaptic activity $\mathbf{r}_t$ carries the influence of spikes across time, allowing an event to produce a current whose effect can persist beyond the instant of firing. The voltage state, spike events, and synaptic state therefore play distinct roles in the dynamics.

**Question.** How can a brief spike affect network voltages at later times?

**Answer.** A spike updates the synaptic activity associated with the emitting neuron. That activity enters later voltage updates through $\mathbf{W}\mathbf{r}_t$, so the spike's influence can continue while the synaptic response evolves. A momentary event can therefore redirect the subsequent trajectory of the entire voltage vector.

The resulting mental model is a moving point in an $N$-dimensional voltage space. Leakage pulls that point toward rest, external input pushes it according to incoming signals, recurrent synaptic coupling redirects it according to previous network activity, and spike-dependent updates alter selected components when neurons fire. Membrane capacitance determines how rapidly all of these currents can move the point. An SNN's voltage dynamics are thus the coordinated evolution of one network state, shaped continuously by currents and punctuated by discrete spike events.
