## Deriving an SNN from Constrained Gradient Dynamics

Once a quadratic objective and its feasible region are in place, the remaining question is how a state can move toward the minimum while respecting every constraint along the way. Imagine a point $y(t)$ sliding downhill across the objective's curved surface. The slope tells it which direction lowers the objective, while the boundaries determine when its motion must be redirected. By expressing both effects as continuous-time dynamics, the moving optimization state can be reinterpreted as the evolving voltage of a spiking neural network.

Consider the quadratic objective
$$
E(y)=\frac{\lambda}{2}y^\top y+b^\top y
$$
Where $y\in\mathbb{R}^{M}$ is the optimization state, $\lambda>0$ controls the curvature of the quadratic bowl, $b\in\mathbb{R}^{M}$ supplies a linear tilt, and $y^\top y$ is the squared length of $y$. The gradient collects the partial derivative of $E$ with respect to every component of $y$:
$$
\frac{\partial E}{\partial y}=\lambda y+b
$$
This gradient points in the direction of steepest increase. To lower the objective, the state moves in the opposite direction. Writing that motion in continuous time gives
$$
\dot y=-\frac{\partial E}{\partial y}=-\lambda y-b
$$
Where $\dot y=dy/dt$ is the instantaneous rate of change of $y$. This equation lets us compute how the optimizer state should move at any moment. The term $-\lambda y$ pulls the state toward the center of the quadratic bowl, while $-b$ shifts that pull according to the bowl's tilt.

For a one-dimensional example, let $\lambda=2$, $b=-4$, and $y=1$. Then
$$
\dot y=-2(1)-(-4)=2
$$
The positive derivative means that $y$ initially moves toward larger values. At $y=2$, the derivative becomes zero:
$$
\dot y=-2(2)+4=0
$$
The motion therefore settles at $y=2$, which is the unconstrained minimum.

Constraints can interrupt this smooth descent. Suppose the permitted states satisfy
$$
Fx-Gy\leq T
$$
Where $x$ is an input vector, $F$ maps that input into the constraint space, $G$ maps the optimization state $y$ into the same space, and $T$ is a vector of boundary values. Each component of this inequality describes one boundary. Ordinary gradient motion can carry $y$ through such a boundary, so the dynamics need a correction that acts at the instant of contact.

Introduce a vector $s(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{N}$ of impulsive boundary events and a matrix $D\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times N}$ of correction directions. Each event in $s(t)$ marks a boundary encounter, and the corresponding column of $D$ determines the direction in which the state is pushed. The constrained dynamics become
$$
\dot y=-\lambda y-b+Ds(t)
$$
The added term $Ds(t)$ redirects the trajectory into the feasible region. It can be understood through the analogy of a ball descending inside a container: gravity supplies the downhill motion, while a brief impulse from a wall changes the ball's velocity when it reaches the container's edge. Choosing the columns of $D$ in directions proportional to the relevant columns of $G$ makes the correction orthogonal to the contacted boundary.

A spiking interpretation appears when the remaining distance to each constraint is represented as a voltage. Define the **constraint voltage**
$$
V=Fx-Gy
$$
Here $V\in\mathbb{R}^{N}$ is the vector of neuron voltages. The feasibility condition becomes
$$
V\leq T
$$
So each component $V_i$ is compared with its corresponding threshold $T_i$. As the optimization state moves, a voltage approaches its threshold. Contact with that threshold produces a boundary event in $s(t)$, and the event triggers the correction needed to redirect $y$. In this mapping, a spike records a precise event in the constrained optimization process.

To find the voltage dynamics, differentiate the voltage definition with respect to time:
$$
\dot V=F\dot x-G\dot y
$$
Where $\dot V=dV/dt$ and $\dot x=dx/dt$. This equation states that voltage changes arise from two sources: changes in the input $x$ and motion of the optimizer state $y$. Substituting the constrained gradient dynamics,
$$
\dot y=-\lambda y-b+Ds(t)
$$
Gives
$$
\begin{aligned}
\dot V
&=F\dot x-G\left(-\lambda y-b+Ds(t)\right)\\
&=F\dot x+\lambda Gy+Gb-GDs(t).
\end{aligned}
$$
Every term now has a direct role. The term $F\dot x$ carries changes in the input into voltage space. The term $\lambda Gy$ carries the quadratic restoring dynamics into that same space. The term $Gb$ represents the linear part of the objective, and $-GDs(t)$ applies the voltage effect of boundary-triggered corrections.

The expression still contains $y$, but the voltage definition provides a way to eliminate it. Rearranging
$$
V=Fx-Gy
$$
Produces
$$
Gy=Fx-V
$$
Substituting this identity into the voltage equation yields
$$
\begin{aligned}
\dot V
&=F\dot x+\lambda(Fx-V)+Gb-GDs(t)\\
&=-\lambda V+F(\lambda x+\dot x)+Gb-GDs(t).
\end{aligned}
$$
This is the expanded SNN optimizer dynamics. The term $-\lambda V$ produces voltage decay, $F(\lambda x+\dot x)$ drives voltage from the input and its changes, $Gb$ supplies a constant objective-dependent drive, and $-GDs(t)$ describes the effect of spike-triggered corrections. If $D$ is chosen proportional to $G$, the same matrix structure that defines the constraints also determines how spikes alter the network state.

**Question.** Why define voltage as $V=Fx-Gy$ instead of using $y$ itself as the voltage?

**Answer.** Spiking must occur when a constraint reaches its boundary. The quantity $Fx-Gy$ measures the current value of every constraint directly, so comparing $V_i$ with $T_i$ detects exactly when boundary $i$ is reached. Using $y$ alone would require an additional transformation before the network could determine which constraint had become active.

**Question.** What does a spike accomplish in these dynamics?

**Answer.** A spike contributes an event through $s(t)$. That event enters the optimizer as $Ds(t)$, redirecting $y$, and enters the voltage dynamics as $-GDs(t)$, changing the constraint voltages immediately. The spike therefore implements the boundary correction that keeps the optimization trajectory feasible.

The complete mental model is a downhill trajectory translated into electrical variables. The quadratic objective creates a continuous pull, the constraints turn distances to boundaries into voltages, and threshold encounters create discrete corrective events. Voltage accumulation tracks how close the optimizer is to violating a constraint; voltage decay reflects the quadratic restoring force; and spikes apply the corrections that preserve feasibility. Continuous gradient motion and discrete boundary events thereby become the membrane-voltage dynamics of an SNN acting as a constrained optimizer.
