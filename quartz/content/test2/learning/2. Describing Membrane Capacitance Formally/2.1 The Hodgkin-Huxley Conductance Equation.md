## The Hodgkin-Huxley Conductance Equation

An action potential depends on the membrane potential changing over time. To describe that change precisely, imagine a small patch of membrane separating charged fluid inside a neuron from charged fluid outside it. Suppose an electrode injects current into this patch. Some of the incoming charge accumulates along the membrane, while some flows through ion pathways crossing the membrane. Predicting the membrane potential therefore requires an accounting rule: at every instant, the applied current must be distributed between charge storage and ion flow.

The membrane can store separated charge because its inner and outer surfaces are conductive fluids divided by a thin insulating layer. This arrangement behaves like a capacitor, an electrical component that stores charge across a voltage difference. Its **membrane capacitance**, written $C_m$, measures how much charge must be separated to produce a given membrane voltage. If $Q$ is the stored charge and $V_m$ is the membrane potential, then
$$
Q=C_mV_m
$$
Here, $Q$ is the charge stored on the membrane, $C_m$ is the membrane capacitance, and $V_m$ is the voltage across the membrane. Current measures how quickly charge moves, so $I=dQ/dt$. When $C_m$ is treated as constant, differentiating the charge relation gives the capacitive current:
$$
I_C=C_m\frac{dV_m}{dt}
$$
The symbol $I_C$ denotes the current used to change the membrane's stored charge, and $dV_m/dt$ is the rate at which membrane potential changes with time. This formula gives a concrete interpretation of capacitance: for the same current, a larger $C_m$ produces a slower voltage change because more charge must accumulate for each unit of voltage increase. A smaller capacitance allows the same current to move the voltage more rapidly.

Charge can also cross the membrane through ion-specific pathways. The Hodgkin-Huxley model represents each pathway by a conductance. **Ionic conductance** measures how readily a particular ionic current can flow; a larger conductance corresponds to a more open electrical pathway. The current through such a pathway depends on both its conductance and the voltage pushing ions through it:
$$
I_{\text{ion}}=g_{\text{ion}}(V_m-V_{\text{ion}})
$$
In this expression, $I_{\text{ion}}$ is the current carried by one ion pathway, $g_{\text{ion}}$ is that pathway's conductance, $V_m$ is the current membrane potential, and $V_{\text{ion}}$ is the pathway's **reversal potential**. The difference $V_m-V_{\text{ion}}$ is the driving voltage. It measures how far the membrane potential lies from the voltage at which that ionic current would reverse direction.

For example, suppose an ion pathway has a reversal potential of $50\ \text{mV}$. At a membrane potential of $20\ \text{mV}$, the driving voltage is $20-50=-30\ \text{mV}$. With a positive conductance, the resulting current has a negative sign under this convention. If the membrane potential rises above $50\ \text{mV}$, the driving voltage becomes positive and the current changes direction. At exactly $50\ \text{mV}$, the driving voltage is zero, so the net current through that pathway is zero even when its conductance is large. Conductance controls how strongly the pathway responds, while the reversal potential determines the voltage toward which that pathway tends to pull the membrane.

The Hodgkin-Huxley conductance equation combines capacitive storage with sodium, potassium, and leakage currents:
$$
I
=
C_m\frac{dV_m}{dt}
+
G_K(V_m-V_K)
+
G_{\mathrm{Na}}(V_m-V_{\mathrm{Na}})
+
G_l(V_m-V_l)
$$
Here, $I$ is the total applied current; $C_m\,dV_m/dt$ is the capacitive current; $g_K$, $g_{\mathrm{Na}}$, and $g_l$ are the potassium, sodium, and leak conductances; and $V_K$, $V_{\mathrm{Na}}$, and $V_l$ are their corresponding reversal potentials. The leak branch collects background ionic flow into one effective pathway. Every term has units of current, so the equation is a current-conservation statement: applied current is divided among charging the membrane and moving ions through the three conductance branches.

The equivalent membrane circuit makes each equation term visible. The capacitor corresponds to $C_m\,dV_m/dt$, the part of the applied current that changes stored charge and therefore changes $V_m$. The parallel sodium, potassium, and leak branches correspond to $g_{\mathrm{Na}}(V_m-V_{\mathrm{Na}})$, $g_K(V_m-V_K)$, and $g_l(V_m-V_l)$. Each branch combines a conductance with a driving voltage set by its reversal potential. The applied-current connection corresponds to $I$, which supplies or removes current from the whole parallel arrangement. Because all branches share the same two sides of the membrane, they experience the same membrane potential $V_m$, while their distinct reversal potentials give them different driving voltages.

To calculate the direction and speed of membrane-voltage change, rearrange the equation so that the derivative stands alone:
$$
\frac{dV_m}{dt}
=
\frac{
I
-
G_K(V_m-V_K)
-
G_{\mathrm{Na}}(V_m-V_{\mathrm{Na}})
-
G_l(V_m-V_l)
}{C_m}
$$
The numerator is the current remaining after the ionic currents are subtracted from the applied current. A positive remainder makes $dV_m/dt$ positive, so the membrane potential rises. A negative remainder makes it fall. When the remainder is zero, the currents balance and the voltage is momentarily steady. Dividing by $C_m$ converts this net current into a rate of voltage change.

Consider an instant when the applied current is $10$ units and the sodium, potassium, and leak currents are $2$, $5$, and $1$ units in the equation's outward-current convention. The current available to charge the capacitor is $10-2-5-1=2$ units. If $C_m=4$ capacitance units, then $dV_m/dt=2/4=0.5$ voltage units per unit time. Increasing the potassium conductance could increase the potassium-current term, reduce the remaining capacitive current, and slow or reverse the voltage change. The effect depends on both $g_K$ and the current distance $V_m-V_K$.

**Question.** Why can opening an ion pathway produce little current?

**Answer.** An open pathway has a high conductance, but current also requires a driving voltage. When $V_m$ is close to that pathway's reversal potential, $V_m-V_{\text{ion}}$ is small, so $g_{\text{ion}}(V_m-V_{\text{ion}})$ remains small. At the reversal potential, the net current through the pathway is zero.

**Question.** What determines whether the membrane potential rises or falls?

**Answer.** The full current balance determines the direction. Subtract the sodium, potassium, and leak currents from the applied current. A positive remainder charges the membrane in the positive direction, while a negative remainder changes its charge in the opposite direction. Capacitance determines how quickly that imbalance changes the voltage.

The resulting mental model is a parallel electrical circuit whose shared voltage is $V_m$. Applied current enters the circuit, ion-specific conductances route charge across the membrane according to their reversal potentials, and the remaining current charges or discharges the membrane capacitor. Membrane voltage evolves because these current contributions continually rebalance, with conductances setting pathway strength, reversal potentials setting driving direction, and capacitance setting the pace of change.
