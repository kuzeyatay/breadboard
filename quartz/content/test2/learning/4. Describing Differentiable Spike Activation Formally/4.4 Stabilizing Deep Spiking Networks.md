## Stabilizing Deep Spiking Networks

Surrogate gradients give a spiking neuron a usable training signal near its firing threshold, but a deep spiking network must carry that signal through many layers and many time steps. Each layer receives spikes, updates membrane potentials, emits new spikes, and passes those events onward. Small distortions can therefore accumulate in two directions at once: through depth as information crosses layers, and through time as membrane state evolves. Stable training requires three parts of this chain to remain well behaved: gradients must reach early layers, membrane potentials must fluctuate in a range that produces useful activity, and activation statistics must remain manageable across time.

A residual connection supports the first requirement by creating a short route around a block of transformations. Imagine that a layer receives a spike representation $x$, while a deeper block transforms it into $F(x)$. A residual block combines the incoming representation with the transformation:
$$
Y = x + F(x)
$$
Here, $x$ is the block's input, $F(x)$ is the change computed by the spiking layers inside the block, and $y$ is the block's output. This expression lets the block preserve an existing signal while learning an adjustment to it.

That structure helps during the backward pass. If the loss is written as $L$, the gradient arriving at the input follows from differentiating the residual relation:
$$
\frac{\partial L}{\partial x}
=
\frac{\partial L}{\partial y}
\left(
1+\frac{\partial F(x)}{\partial x}
\right)
$$
The term $\partial L/\partial y$ is the gradient reaching the block's output, while $\partial F(x)/\partial x$ measures how the internal transformation responds to its input. The added $1$ comes from the direct path $x \rightarrow y$. It provides a route through which a gradient can pass even when the derivatives inside $F$ are small or irregular.

Consider a stack of spiking layers whose surrogate derivatives are weak during a particular training step because many membrane potentials lie far from threshold. Along the transformation path, multiplying several weak derivatives can greatly shrink the learning signal. The residual path carries part of that signal around the affected transformations. Earlier layers can then continue receiving information about how their activity influenced the final loss. Residual connections therefore address the depth component of deep-SNN stability by improving both forward signal preservation and backward gradient propagation.

**Question.** Does a residual connection guarantee that every layer will produce useful spikes?

**Answer.** A residual connection preserves routes for signals and gradients, but useful spiking still depends on the membrane potentials produced within each layer. If initialization places nearly every neuron far below threshold, the residual route may carry information through the network while the transformed branches contribute very little. Activity statistics must therefore be stabilized as well as gradient paths.

Fluctuation-driven initialization addresses the network's starting membrane-potential regime. A spiking neuron typically integrates many weighted inputs. Some contributions raise its membrane potential, others may lower it, and the combined input changes over time as presynaptic spikes arrive. The neuron fires when these fluctuations carry the membrane potential across its threshold. Initial weights therefore determine whether the network begins with meaningful threshold crossings, near-total silence, or excessive firing.

The central quantity is the distribution of membrane potentials relative to the firing threshold. If the distribution sits far below threshold with very little spread, spikes become rare. Sparse firing can be a desired operating property, but complete or near-complete silence leaves few neurons near the region where a surrogate gradient is informative. If the distribution repeatedly pushes a large fraction of neurons across threshold, activity becomes dense and the temporal dynamics can lose useful selectivity. Fluctuation-driven initialization chooses initial parameter scales by considering the variations created by incoming spike trains, aiming to place neurons in a regime where ordinary input fluctuations can produce threshold crossings.

An analogy is a buoy moving beneath a height marker. A buoy floating far below the marker never reaches it when small waves pass. A buoy already driven upward by every ripple crosses constantly. A useful starting position combines an appropriate average height with enough wave-driven variation that crossings occur selectively. In a spiking layer, the average membrane level plays the role of the buoy's resting height, the membrane-potential variance plays the role of wave size, and the firing threshold plays the role of the marker.

This perspective connects initialization directly to surrogate-gradient training. Surrogate derivatives usually concentrate learning sensitivity around the threshold region. Initialization that creates suitable membrane fluctuations places a workable fraction of neurons near that region during early training. The network begins with both observable spike activity and accessible gradient information, giving optimization a chance to shape deeper representations rather than first struggling to escape an inactive or saturated state.

Training then changes the weights, and recurrent membrane dynamics make activation statistics depend on time. A normalization rule that treats all time steps as one stationary population can blur those temporal differences. Early in a sequence, membrane potentials may reflect only a few incoming events. Later, they may reflect accumulated input, leakage, reset events, and activity propagated from earlier layers. Temporal batch normalization handles this changing regime by normalizing activations with statistics associated with their temporal position.

For an activation value $u_t$ at time step $t$, normalization is motivated by centering and scaling relative to the activity observed at that time:
$$
\widehat{u}_t
=
\frac{u_t-\mu_t}{\sqrt{\sigma_t^2+\varepsilon}}
$$
Here, $u_t$ is the activation or membrane-related quantity being normalized, $\mu_t$ is its batch mean at time $t$, $\sigma_t^2$ is its batch variance at that time, and $\varepsilon$ is a small positive constant that keeps the division numerically stable. The normalized value $\widehat{u}_t$ indicates where the activation lies relative to the distribution appropriate for that temporal step. Learnable scaling and shifting can then adapt the normalized activity to the firing dynamics required by the layer.

Suppose membrane potentials are tightly clustered near the beginning of a sequence and become more widely spread after several integration steps. Using one shared variance would scale both regimes identically even though their distributions differ. Temporal normalization can scale the narrow early distribution and the wider late distribution according to their own statistics. Threshold crossings then arise from more controlled activation ranges across the sequence, which supports consistent spike activity and more reliable gradient flow through time.

**Question.** How do these three methods divide the stabilization problem?

**Answer.** Residual connections provide short routes through depth, helping representations and gradients cross many layers. Fluctuation-driven initialization establishes a useful starting balance between membrane variation and firing threshold, helping neurons begin with trainable spike activity. Temporal batch normalization manages how activation statistics evolve across time, helping later optimization preserve workable dynamic ranges. Their roles overlap, yet each acts on a different part of the training chain.

A stable deep spiking network can therefore be pictured as a sequence of threshold-driven dynamical layers with protected routes, prepared starting conditions, and time-aware statistical control. Residual connections keep information moving through depth. Fluctuation-driven initialization places membrane potentials where input variability can create selective spikes. Temporal batch normalization keeps evolving activation distributions within manageable ranges. Together, these mechanisms support the path from membrane dynamics to spike generation to surrogate-gradient learning, allowing depth and time to contribute useful computation without progressively eroding activity or learning signals.
