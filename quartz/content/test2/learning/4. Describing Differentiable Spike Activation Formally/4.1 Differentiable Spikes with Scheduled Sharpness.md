A neuron's membrane value can already be compared with a firing threshold: values below the threshold correspond to silence, while crossing the threshold produces a spike. Training becomes difficult at that crossing because the discrete spike function changes abruptly. Its output jumps from $0$ to $1$, leaving no gradual slope that ordinary gradient-based learning can follow. A differentiable spike activation replaces the abrupt jump during training with a smooth transition, then progressively sharpens that transition as training advances.

Let $u$ denote the neuron's membrane value and let $\vartheta$ denote its firing threshold. The difference
$$
Z = u-\vartheta
$$
Measures how far the neuron is from firing. A negative $z$ means that the membrane value remains below threshold, $z=0$ marks the threshold itself, and a positive $z$ means that the membrane value has crossed the threshold. Writing the activation in terms of $z$ centers the entire transition at the firing boundary.

A smooth spike activation can then be defined with a sigmoid:
$$
\tilde{s}(u;\alpha)
=
\frac{1}{1+\exp\left[-\alpha(u-\vartheta)\right]}
$$
Here, $\tilde{s}$ is the differentiable spike value used during training, $u$ is the membrane value, $\vartheta$ is the firing threshold, $\alpha>0$ is the **sharpness parameter**, and $\exp(\cdot)$ is the exponential function. The formula computes a value between $0$ and $1$ that indicates how strongly the neuron is approaching the firing state.

Suppose $\vartheta=1$, $\alpha=2$, and the membrane value is $u=0.5$. Then $u-\vartheta=-0.5$, so
$$
\tilde{s}
=
\frac{1}{1+\exp(1)}
\approx 0.269
$$
The neuron is below threshold, so the smooth activation lies closer to $0$. If the membrane value rises to $u=1.5$, the threshold difference becomes $0.5$, giving
$$
\tilde{s}
=
\frac{1}{1+\exp(-1)}
\approx 0.731
$$
The same activation now lies closer to $1$. At exactly $u=\vartheta$, the exponent is zero and the activation equals $1/2$. The sigmoid therefore places its midpoint at the firing threshold and changes continuously around that point.

The parameter $\alpha$ controls how wide this transition region is. With a small value of $\alpha$, membrane values across a broad interval around the threshold produce intermediate outputs. With a large value, the activation stays close to $0$ below threshold, rises rapidly near $u=\vartheta$, and stays close to $1$ above threshold. Increasing $\alpha$ therefore makes the smooth curve resemble a discrete spike threshold more closely.

For example, take a membrane value just below threshold, with $u-\vartheta=-0.1$. When $\alpha=1$,
$$
\tilde{s}
=
\frac{1}{1+\exp(0.1)}
\approx 0.475
$$
When $\alpha=20$,
$$
\tilde{s}
=
\frac{1}{1+\exp(2)}
\approx 0.119
$$
Now take a value equally far above threshold, $u-\vartheta=0.1$. The activation is approximately $0.525$ when $\alpha=1$, but approximately $0.881$ when $\alpha=20$. The larger sharpness parameter pushes the two nearby membrane values toward opposite firing states even though both remain only $0.1$ away from the boundary.

Smoothness helps the learning process because the derivative supplies a gradient. Differentiating the activation with respect to the membrane value gives
$$
\frac{\partial \tilde{s}}{\partial u}
=
\alpha\,\tilde{s}(1-\tilde{s})
$$
This derivative tells the training procedure how a small change in membrane value changes the smooth spike output. Every symbol comes from the activation above: $\partial \tilde{s}/\partial u$ is the activation's slope with respect to $u$, $\alpha$ is its sharpness, and $\tilde{s}(1-\tilde{s})$ makes the slope largest near the threshold. At $u=\vartheta$, where $\tilde{s}=1/2$, the slope is $\alpha/4$. Increasing $\alpha$ makes the threshold region steeper while concentrating useful gradient information into a narrower range of membrane values.

Starting training with a very sharp transition can leave many neurons far from the narrow region where the derivative is appreciable. A smoother initial activation spreads the gradient across a wider range, allowing learning to adjust parameters even when membrane values have not yet gathered near the firing threshold. As training proceeds, the activation can sharpen so that its outputs increasingly resemble discrete firing decisions. This progression is a **smooth-to-sharp transition**.

To make that progression formal, let the sharpness depend on the training epoch:
$$
\alpha_e
=
\alpha_{\mathrm{start}}
+
\frac{e}{E}
\left(\alpha_{\mathrm{end}}-\alpha_{\mathrm{start}}\right)
$$
In this schedule, $e$ is the current epoch, $E$ is the total number of scheduled epochs, $\alpha_{\mathrm{start}}$ is the initial sharpness, and $\alpha_{\mathrm{end}}$ is the desired final sharpness. An epoch is one complete pass through the training data. The fraction $e/E$ measures training progress, so this formula increases sharpness linearly from its starting value toward its final value.

If training lasts $E=100$ epochs, with $\alpha_{\mathrm{start}}=1$ and $\alpha_{\mathrm{end}}=21$, then epoch $25$ uses
$$
\alpha_{25}
=
1+\frac{25}{100}(21-1)
=
6
$$
Epoch $50$ uses $\alpha_{50}=11$, and epoch $100$ uses $\alpha_{100}=21$. Substituting $\alpha_e$ into the activation gives the complete epoch-dependent form:
$$
\tilde{s}_e(u)
=
\frac{1}{1+\exp\left[-\alpha_e(u-\vartheta)\right]}
$$
This expression computes the training-time spike value at epoch $e$. Early epochs produce a broad, gently sloped threshold region; later epochs produce a narrow, steep threshold region.

**Question.** Why does the sharpness parameter change during training instead of remaining large from the beginning?

**Answer.** A smaller initial sharpness gives membrane values across a wider region a usable slope, which helps gradient-based updates move the model toward effective firing boundaries. A larger later sharpness concentrates the transition near the threshold and makes the activation behave more like a discrete firing decision. The schedule coordinates these two stages within one activation.

**Question.** Does a smooth activation mean that the neuron emits fractional physical spikes?

**Answer.** The smooth value provides a differentiable training signal around the firing threshold. The firing rule still represents spikes as discrete events when a hard threshold is applied. Think of $\tilde{s}_e$ as a temporary, trainable description of proximity to firing: its early softness guides parameter updates, and its increasing sharpness aligns that guidance with the eventual threshold decision.

The resulting mental model is a threshold viewed through an adjustable lens. Early in training, the lens spreads the boundary into a broad transition so gradients can guide learning from many nearby membrane values. Each epoch tightens the lens by increasing $\alpha_e$. By the later epochs, the transition clusters around $u=\vartheta$, and the differentiable activation closely tracks the discrete spike behavior that the neuron is meant to express.
