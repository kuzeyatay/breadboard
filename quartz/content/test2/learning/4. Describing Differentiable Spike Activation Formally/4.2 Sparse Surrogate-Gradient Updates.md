## Sparse Surrogate-Gradient Updates

A surrogate gradient gives the spike function a usable slope during learning. Once that slope has been introduced, a practical question follows: which neurons actually need that derivative calculation at a given time step? Consider a layer containing thousands of spiking neurons. Each neuron has a membrane potential, yet only a small fraction may sit close enough to the firing threshold for a slight voltage change to affect whether it spikes. Sparse surrogate-gradient updates concentrate the backward calculation on that fraction.

<!-- learning-unit:U16:after-introduction -->

Let $u_i[t]$ denote the membrane potential of neuron $i$ at time step $t$, and let $\theta$ denote its firing threshold. The neuron's spike can be written as
$$
S_i[t] = H\!\left(u_i[t]-\theta\right)
$$
Where $s_i[t]$ is the emitted spike and $H$ is the step function. The quantity $u_i[t]-\theta$ measures the neuron's signed position relative to threshold. A positive value places the voltage above threshold, while a negative value places it below threshold.

For sparse updates, the useful quantity is the size of the gap rather than its sign:
$$
D_i[t] = \left|u_i[t]-\theta\right|
$$
Here, $d_i[t]$ is the voltage-to-threshold distance. A distance of zero means that the membrane potential lies exactly at threshold. A small distance means that the neuron is close to changing its spike state. A large distance means that its voltage would require a larger change before crossing the threshold.

Suppose $\theta=1.0$. A neuron with $u_i[t]=0.97$ has distance $d_i[t]=|0.97-1.0|=0.03$. Another neuron with $u_j[t]=0.30$ has distance $d_j[t]=|0.30-1.0|=0.70$. The first neuron lies near the spike boundary, so a small parameter update could move it across that boundary. The second lies much farther away, so its current spike decision is less sensitive to a small voltage change.

Training can turn this idea into a precise selection rule by choosing a surrogate-region width $\delta>0$. A neuron belongs to the **surrogate active set** when its membrane potential falls within $\delta$ of the threshold:
$$
\mathcal{A}[t]
=
\left\{
I:\left|u_i[t]-\theta\right|\leq\delta
\right\}
$$
The symbol $\mathcal{A}[t]$ denotes the set of active neurons at time $t$, $i$ identifies a neuron, and $\delta$ controls how wide the active surrogate region extends around the threshold. This formula lets us determine exactly which neurons participate in the surrogate-derivative calculation.

The same condition can be represented by an **activity mask**:
$$
M_i[t]
=
\begin{cases}
1, & \left|u_i[t]-\theta\right|\leq\delta,\\
0, & \left|u_i[t]-\theta\right|>\delta.
\end{cases}
$$
The mask $m_i[t]$ records membership in the active set. A value of $1$ includes the neuron in the sparse gradient evaluation, while a value of $0$ gives it no local surrogate derivative at that time step. The set and mask express the same decision in two forms: $\mathcal{A}[t]$ is convenient for describing a selected group, and $m_i[t]$ is convenient for writing the derivative algebraically.

Let $g_i[t]$ be the surrogate slope assigned to neuron $i$ when it lies near threshold. The sparse surrogate derivative can then be written as the piecewise rule
$$
\frac{\widetilde{\partial s_i[t]}}{\partial u_i[t]}
=
\begin{cases}
G_i[t], & \left|u_i[t]-\theta\right|\leq\delta,\\
0, & \left|u_i[t]-\theta\right|>\delta.
\end{cases}
$$
The tilde indicates that this is a surrogate derivative used for learning. The numerator $s_i[t]$ is the neuron's spike, the denominator $u_i[t]$ is its membrane potential, and $g_i[t]$ supplies the chosen local slope inside the active region. The upper branch gives active neurons a usable gradient. The lower branch assigns zero to inactive neurons, allowing their local derivative calculations to be skipped.

Using the activity mask, the same rule becomes
$$
\frac{\widetilde{\partial s_i[t]}}{\partial u_i[t]}
=
M_i[t]\,g_i[t]
$$
This compact form shows how sparsity enters the gradient. When $m_i[t]=1$, multiplication preserves the surrogate slope $g_i[t]$. When $m_i[t]=0$, multiplication produces zero. The threshold-proximity test therefore acts as a gate on the backward signal.

Imagine four neurons with threshold $\theta=1.0$, active-region width $\delta=0.1$, and membrane potentials
$$
U[t]=(0.94,\;0.25,\;1.07,\;1.40)
$$
Their voltage-to-threshold distances are
$$
D[t]=(0.06,\;0.75,\;0.07,\;0.40)
$$
Only the first and third distances are at most $0.1$, so
$$
\mathcal{A}[t]=\{1,3\},
\qquad
M[t]=(1,0,1,0)
$$
If the active surrogate slope is $g_i[t]=0.5$ for each selected neuron, the sparse derivative vector is
$$
\frac{\widetilde{\partial s[t]}}{\partial u[t]}
=
(0.5,\;0,\;0.5,\;0)
$$
The backward pass evaluates meaningful local slopes for two neurons rather than carrying the same derivative work across all four. In a larger layer, the amount of local surrogate-gradient work follows the size of the active set, $|\mathcal{A}[t]|$, rather than automatically following the total number of neurons.

**Question.** Does an active neuron have to emit a spike at that time step?

**Answer.** Activity here refers to the surrogate derivative, rather than spike emission. A neuron slightly below threshold can belong to $\mathcal{A}[t]$ because its voltage is close to changing the spike decision. A neuron slightly above threshold can also belong to the set. Threshold proximity determines gradient activity, while the sign of $u_i[t]-\theta$ determines the current spike state.

The width $\delta$ controls how selective the calculation becomes. A smaller $\delta$ admits fewer voltages, producing a smaller active set and a sparser derivative vector. A larger $\delta$ admits more voltages, allowing gradient information to pass through a wider neighborhood around threshold. The choice therefore balances concentration of computation against the range over which the surrogate supplies a learning signal.

**Question.** Why use distance from threshold instead of membrane-potential magnitude?

**Answer.** The spike decision changes at $\theta$, so sensitivity is organized around that boundary. A voltage can have a large numerical magnitude while remaining far from its firing threshold, or a modest magnitude while lying extremely close to it. The distance $|u_i[t]-\theta|$ directly measures proximity to the decision boundary that learning is trying to influence.

The complete mental model is a narrow learning window centered on the firing threshold. Each neuron's membrane potential determines its distance from that window's center. The activity mask opens the backward path for neurons inside the window and closes it for neurons outside. The resulting surrogate active set identifies where spike decisions are locally sensitive, turning threshold proximity into a sparse gradient calculation while preserving the piecewise neuron update used in the forward dynamics.
