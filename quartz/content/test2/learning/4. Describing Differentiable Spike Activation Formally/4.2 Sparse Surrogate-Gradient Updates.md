## Sparse Surrogate-Gradient Updates

Earlier, surrogate gradients gave each spike-generating neuron a usable local slope during learning. Once that slope exists, another practical question appears: should the backward pass evaluate it for every neuron at every time step? Imagine a layer containing many neurons whose membrane voltages remain far below their firing thresholds. Their states have little immediate influence on whether a spike appears, yet a dense backward pass still processes them all. Sparse surrogate-gradient updates concentrate that work on neurons whose activity or voltage indicates that a useful change may occur.

Consider one neuron with membrane voltage $u_i$, firing threshold $\theta$, and spike output $s_i$. The index $i$ identifies the neuron at a particular time step. Its spike is described by the threshold rule
$$
S_i = H(u_i-\theta)
$$
Where $H$ is the step function: $H(x)=1$ when $x\geq 0$ and $H(x)=0$ when $x<0$. The difference $u_i-\theta$ tells us how far the neuron is above or below threshold. A positive difference produces a spike, while a negative difference produces silence.

During backpropagation, a weight $w$ affecting this neuron receives a gradient through the chain
$$
\frac{\partial L}{\partial w}
=
\frac{\partial L}{\partial s_i}
\frac{\partial s_i}{\partial u_i}
\frac{\partial u_i}{\partial w}
$$
Where $L$ is the training loss, $\partial L/\partial s_i$ measures how the spike influences that loss, $\partial s_i/\partial u_i$ measures how the spike responds to voltage, and $\partial u_i/\partial w$ measures how the weight changes the voltage. A surrogate function supplies an approximate value for the middle factor. Let that surrogate derivative be $g(u_i-\theta)$, with its largest useful values usually concentrated around the firing threshold.

A sparse update adds an eligibility decision before retaining this derivative. An eligibility mask $m_i$ records whether neuron $i$ participates in the current gradient calculation:
$$
M_i =
\begin{cases}
1, & \text{if neuron } i \text{ is eligible},\\
0, & \text{if neuron } i \text{ is ineligible}.
\end{cases}
$$
The sparse surrogate derivative is then
$$
\widetilde{\frac{\partial s_i}{\partial u_i}}
=
M_i\,g(u_i-\theta)
=
\begin{cases}
G(u_i-\theta), & m_i=1,\\
0, & m_i=0.
\end{cases}
$$
Here, the tilde marks an approximate derivative, $g$ is the chosen surrogate slope, and $m_i$ controls whether that slope is retained. An eligible neuron passes gradient information through the spike operation. An ineligible neuron contributes zero through this local derivative, allowing its associated gradient work to be suppressed.

One way to define eligibility is through observed activity. Let the spike itself determine the mask:
$$
M_i^{\mathrm{act}} =
\begin{cases}
1, & s_i=1,\\
0, & s_i=0.
\end{cases}
$$
The symbol $m_i^{\mathrm{act}}$ is the activity-based mask. A neuron that fired receives an active-neuron gradient, while a silent neuron receives zero through the masked surrogate derivative. For example, suppose four neurons produce the spike pattern
$$
(s_1,s_2,s_3,s_4)=(0,1,0,1)
$$
Their activity masks are
$$
(m_1^{\mathrm{act}},m_2^{\mathrm{act}},m_3^{\mathrm{act}},m_4^{\mathrm{act}})
=(0,1,0,1)
$$
Only neurons 2 and 4 retain their surrogate derivatives. The backward computation therefore follows the two neurons that generated events instead of treating all four neurons equally.

Activity provides a clear eligibility signal, but voltage contains additional information. A silent neuron may sit just below threshold, where a small weight change could make it fire. Near-threshold eligibility captures this situation by selecting neurons according to their voltage distance from threshold:
$$
M_i^{\mathrm{thr}} =
\begin{cases}
1, & |u_i-\theta|\leq \delta,\\
0, & |u_i-\theta|>\delta.
\end{cases}
$$
The symbol $m_i^{\mathrm{thr}}$ is the near-threshold mask, $|u_i-\theta|$ is the absolute distance between voltage and threshold, and $\delta$ is a chosen eligibility width. This condition retains gradient computation inside a voltage band extending $\delta$ below and above the threshold. Voltages outside that band receive a zero masked derivative.

Suppose $\theta=1.0$ and $\delta=0.1$. Neurons with voltages $0.94$, $1.02$, and $0.30$ have threshold distances $0.06$, $0.02$, and $0.70$. The first two neurons are eligible because their distances lie within the $0.1$ band. The third neuron is ineligible because its voltage is far from a spike transition. The silent neuron at $0.94$ still receives a gradient: its voltage places it close enough to threshold for a small parameter change to affect its firing state.

**Question.** Why include a silent neuron when the method is supposed to produce sparse updates?

**Answer.** Sparsity selects informative neurons rather than selecting spikes alone. A neuron just below threshold has produced no event, yet its firing decision is sensitive to a small voltage change. Near-threshold eligibility retains that useful sensitivity while excluding silent neurons whose voltages lie far from the decision boundary.

The width $\delta$ controls a training-efficiency trade-off. A narrow band admits fewer neurons, creating sparser gradient evaluation and reducing the amount of backward work. It also discards more possible learning signals. A wider band admits more neurons and preserves a broader region of surrogate-gradient information, while making the backward pass denser. The threshold band therefore expresses a direct choice about where computational effort should be spent.

For sparse evaluation to save work, the mask must guide which local derivatives and associated gradient paths are evaluated. Computing every surrogate derivative first and multiplying most of them by zero afterward produces the same masked values, yet much of the dense calculation has already occurred. Eligibility becomes computationally useful when the training procedure identifies active or near-threshold neurons and restricts gradient processing to those selected entries.

**Question.** Does a zero masked derivative mean that an ineligible neuron can never learn?

**Answer.** Eligibility is evaluated from the neuron's current activity or voltage at each relevant step. A neuron excluded at one moment can become eligible later as incoming spikes, membrane dynamics, or updated weights move its voltage toward threshold. The mask describes where the current backward pass carries information; it does not permanently remove the neuron from training.

A useful mental model is a backward-pass spotlight moving across the network. Spikes identify neurons that have just crossed a decision boundary, while near-threshold voltages identify neurons poised to cross it. The eligibility mask places the spotlight on those neurons, the surrogate derivative supplies a usable slope inside the illuminated region, and the remaining neurons contribute zeros for that update. Sparse surrogate-gradient training emerges from this combination: a formal local derivative, a piecewise eligibility condition, and a deliberate balance between preserving learning signals and limiting gradient computation.
