## Differentiable Spikes with Evolving Sharpness

A hard spike threshold gives a neuron a clear decision rule: emit a spike when its internal state reaches the threshold. The previous discussion of surrogate gradients showed how training can proceed despite the abruptness of that decision. A closely related approach smooths the spike activation itself during learning, then gradually sharpens it as the training epochs pass. Early training receives broad, usable gradient information; later training increasingly resembles the discrete spike behavior the network will ultimately use.

Consider a neuron with membrane potential $u$ and firing threshold $\theta$. Their difference,
$$
Z = u-\theta
$$
Measures how close the neuron is to firing. Here, $z$ is the **threshold margin**. A negative margin means the membrane potential lies below threshold, a zero margin means it has reached threshold, and a positive margin means it has crossed threshold. The hard spike activation converts this margin into a binary output:
$$
H(z)=
\begin{cases}
0, & z<0,\\
1, & z\geq 0.
\end{cases}
$$
The symbol $H$ denotes the hard threshold function. Its output changes instantly from $0$ to $1$ at $z=0$. That instant jump creates the familiar training difficulty: away from the threshold, the derivative is zero, while at the threshold the ordinary derivative is undefined. Gradient-based learning therefore receives little guidance about how a slightly subthreshold membrane potential should move.

A differentiable spike activation replaces the jump during training with a smooth transition. One useful form is
$$
S(z;\alpha)=\frac{1}{1+\exp(-\alpha z)}
$$
The quantity $s$ is the differentiable spike activation, $z=u-\theta$ is the threshold margin, $\exp$ is the exponential function, and $\alpha>0$ is the **sharpness parameter**. The formula computes a continuous value between $0$ and $1$. Far below threshold, $s$ approaches $0$; far above threshold, it approaches $1$; at the threshold, where $z=0$, it equals $1/2$.

The sharpness parameter controls how quickly the output changes near the threshold. With a small $\alpha$, the transition stretches across a wide range of membrane potentials. A neuron that is moderately below threshold can still produce a value noticeably above zero, and a neuron moderately above threshold can produce a value noticeably below one. With a large $\alpha$, the transition is concentrated into a narrow neighborhood around $z=0$. Most outputs then lie close to the binary values $0$ and $1$.

For example, suppose $z=0.2$. When $\alpha=1$,
$$
S(0.2;1)=\frac{1}{1+\exp(-0.2)}\approx 0.55
$$
The activation treats this small threshold crossing cautiously. When $\alpha=20$,
$$
S(0.2;20)=\frac{1}{1+\exp(-4)}\approx 0.98
$$
The same membrane potential now produces an output very close to a spike. For $z=-0.2$, the corresponding values are approximately $0.45$ and $0.018$. Increasing $\alpha$ therefore pushes positive margins toward $1$ and negative margins toward $0$, making the smooth activation approximate the hard threshold more closely.

Differentiability gives the neuron a usable derivative:
$$
\frac{\partial s}{\partial z}
=
\alpha s(1-s)
$$
This expression follows by differentiating the exponential form of the activation. Every symbol refers to a quantity already present: $s$ is the current smooth spike value, $z$ is the threshold margin, and $\alpha$ is the sharpness. The derivative tells training how strongly a small change in membrane potential changes the activation.

Near threshold, $s\approx 1/2$, so the derivative reaches
$$
\left.\frac{\partial s}{\partial z}\right|_{z=0}
=
\alpha\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)\left(1-\frac{1}{2}\right)
=
\frac{\alpha}{4}
$$
A larger $\alpha$ creates a taller derivative near the threshold, while also compressing that derivative into a narrower interval of $z$. A smaller $\alpha$ spreads gradient information across a broader interval. Early in training, that broad interval helps many near-threshold states receive guidance. Later, the narrow interval encourages behavior that closely follows the firing boundary.

**Question.** Why begin training with a smoother activation instead of using the sharpest available activation immediately?

**Answer.** At the beginning of training, membrane potentials may lie far from useful firing boundaries. A very sharp activation gives substantial gradient information only within a narrow region around the threshold, so many neuron states receive weak updates. A smoother activation has a wider transition region and can guide a larger set of membrane potentials toward useful values. Once those states have moved into better positions, sharpening the activation aligns the learned behavior more closely with discrete spikes.

The activation can evolve by making $\alpha$ depend on the training epoch. Let $e$ denote the current epoch and $E$ the total number of scheduled epochs. A simple gradual schedule is
$$
\alpha(e)
=
\alpha_{\mathrm{start}}
+
\frac{e}{E}
\left(
\alpha_{\mathrm{end}}-\alpha_{\mathrm{start}}
\right)
$$
Here, $\alpha(e)$ is the sharpness used at epoch $e$, $\alpha_{\mathrm{start}}$ is the initial sharpness, and $\alpha_{\mathrm{end}}$ is the final sharpness. The fraction $e/E$ measures training progress. At $e=0$, the formula gives $\alpha(0)=\alpha_{\mathrm{start}}$. At $e=E$, it gives $\alpha(E)=\alpha_{\mathrm{end}}$. Between those endpoints, sharpness increases steadily.

Suppose training lasts $100$ epochs, with $\alpha_{\mathrm{start}}=1$ and $\alpha_{\mathrm{end}}=21$. At epoch $25$,
$$
\alpha(25)=1+\frac{25}{100}(21-1)=6
$$
At epoch $50$, the sharpness is $11$, and at epoch $100$, it reaches $21$. The neuron therefore begins with a broad transition, passes through progressively steeper transitions, and finishes with an activation concentrated closely around the threshold. This schedule illustrates the principle of an evolving activation; the appropriate endpoints and rate depend on the training setup rather than on a single universal choice.

The gradual change is a form of **continuation from smooth to sharp behavior**. Training starts with an easier, smoother optimization landscape, then continuously transforms the activation toward the desired spike-like response. Because each epoch changes the activation only partway, the parameters learned under one sharpness remain useful as the transition becomes steeper.

**Question.** Does increasing sharpness simply make every gradient larger?

**Answer.** Increasing $\alpha$ makes the derivative taller at the threshold and narrower around it. Membrane potentials very close to $\theta$ can receive stronger gradients, while potentials farther away receive smaller gradients because the activation has already approached $0$ or $1$. Sharpness changes both the strength and the range of the learning signal.

The complete mental model begins with the threshold margin $u-\theta$, which states how close a neuron is to firing. A smooth activation turns that margin into a differentiable value, and its sharpness parameter determines how closely the value resembles a binary spike. An epoch-dependent schedule raises the sharpness as learning proceeds, allowing broad guidance early and precise threshold behavior later. The evolving spike activation therefore connects trainability and discreteness through one controlled progression: learn with a forgiving boundary, then gradually concentrate that boundary into a spike.
