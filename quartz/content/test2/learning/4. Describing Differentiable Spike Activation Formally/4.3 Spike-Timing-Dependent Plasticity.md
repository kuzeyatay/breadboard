## Spike-Timing-Dependent Plasticity

Once pre-synaptic and post-synaptic spikes have been assigned precise times, their temporal relationship can guide learning at the synapse connecting the two neurons. Imagine a pre-synaptic neuron emitting a spike shortly before the post-synaptic neuron fires. The close ordering suggests that activity arriving through this synapse contributed to the post-synaptic spike. The connection can therefore be strengthened. If the post-synaptic spike occurs first and the pre-synaptic spike follows, the ordering suggests a weaker causal relationship in that direction, so the connection can be weakened. Spike-timing-dependent plasticity, usually abbreviated **STDP**, turns this timing intuition into a rule for changing the synaptic weight.

A synaptic weight measures how strongly a spike from one neuron influences another neuron. Let the current weight be $w$, and let $\Delta w$ denote the update produced by a pair of spikes. After learning, the weight becomes
$$
W_{\text{new}} = w_{\text{old}} + \Delta w
$$
Here, $w_{\text{old}}$ is the weight before the update, $w_{\text{new}}$ is the weight afterward, and $\Delta w$ is the signed amount of change. A positive $\Delta w$ strengthens the synapse, a process called **synaptic potentiation**. A negative $\Delta w$ weakens the synapse, a process called **synaptic depression**.

The update depends on relative spike timing rather than on the two absolute clock readings. Let $t_{\text{pre}}$ be the time of the pre-synaptic spike and $t_{\text{post}}$ be the time of the post-synaptic spike. Define their time difference as
$$
\Delta t = t_{\text{post}} - t_{\text{pre}}
$$
This sign convention gives $\Delta t>0$ when the pre-synaptic spike occurs first. For example, if the pre-synaptic neuron fires at $12\ \text{ms}$ and the post-synaptic neuron fires at $15\ \text{ms}$, then $\Delta t=15-12=3\ \text{ms}$. The positive value records a pre-before-post ordering. If the post-synaptic neuron fires at $15\ \text{ms}$ and the pre-synaptic neuron fires at $19\ \text{ms}$, then $\Delta t=15-19=-4\ \text{ms}$. The negative value records a post-before-pre ordering.

Ordering determines the direction of the update, while temporal separation determines its magnitude. Spikes separated by only a short interval produce a relatively large change because their timing is closely related. As the interval grows, the update becomes smaller. A common STDP rule expresses both ideas with a piecewise exponential function:
$$
\Delta w =
\begin{cases}
A_{+}\exp\left(-\dfrac{\Delta t}{\tau_{+}}\right),
& \Delta t > 0, \\[8pt]
-A_{-}\exp\left(\dfrac{\Delta t}{\tau_{-}}\right),
& \Delta t < 0.
\end{cases}
$$
The two branches correspond to the two possible spike orders. In the upper branch, $\Delta t>0$, so the pre-synaptic spike arrives before the post-synaptic spike. The update is positive and produces timing-dependent potentiation. In the lower branch, $\Delta t<0$, so the post-synaptic spike occurs before the pre-synaptic spike. The minus sign makes the update negative and produces timing-dependent depression.

Every term controls a specific part of the learning rule. The quantity $\Delta w$ is the resulting weight change. The constants $A_{+}>0$ and $A_{-}>0$ set the largest potentiating and depressing update magnitudes. The constants $\tau_{+}>0$ and $\tau_{-}>0$ are time scales that determine how quickly the influence of a spike pair fades as the spikes move farther apart. The exponential function $\exp(x)$, meaning $e^x$, creates a smooth decay away from nearly coincident spikes.

Consider the potentiation branch with $A_{+}=0.10$ and $\tau_{+}=20\ \text{ms}$. A pre-synaptic spike followed $5\ \text{ms}$ later by a post-synaptic spike gives
$$
\Delta w
=0.10\exp\left(-\frac{5}{20}\right)
\approx 0.10(0.779)
\approx 0.078
$$
The weight increases by about $0.078$. If the separation grows to $40\ \text{ms}$, the same rule gives
$$
\Delta w
=0.10\exp\left(-\frac{40}{20}\right)
\approx 0.10(0.135)
\approx 0.014
$$
Both spike pairs have the same ordering, so both strengthen the synapse. The more tightly timed pair produces the larger update.

The depression branch follows the same distance-sensitive pattern. Suppose $A_{-}=0.12$, $\tau_{-}=20\ \text{ms}$, and the post-synaptic spike occurs $5\ \text{ms}$ before the pre-synaptic spike. Then $\Delta t=-5\ \text{ms}$, giving
$$
\Delta w
=-0.12\exp\left(\frac{-5}{20}\right)
\approx -0.12(0.779)
\approx -0.093
$$
The negative result decreases the synaptic weight by about $0.093$. The formula therefore reads as a timing curve centered around $\Delta t=0$: positive values on the pre-before-post side, negative values on the post-before-pre side, and magnitudes that shrink toward zero as temporal separation increases. The parameters allow potentiation and depression to have different peak strengths and different temporal windows.

**Question.** Why does a larger positive $\Delta t$ produce less potentiation even though the spike order remains favorable?

**Answer.** A larger positive $\Delta t$ means more time passed between the pre-synaptic and post-synaptic spikes. In the potentiation branch, the exponent is $-\Delta t/\tau_{+}$. Increasing $\Delta t$ makes this exponent more negative, which makes the exponential factor smaller. The update stays positive because the ordering remains pre-before-post, while its magnitude decreases because the temporal relationship is looser.

The value $\Delta t=0$ requires an explicit convention in a concrete learning system because the piecewise expression assigns branches only for positive and negative differences. A system may choose a special update for simultaneous spikes or resolve equality through its event-ordering procedure. The essential STDP pattern lies on either side of zero, where the sign of the interval identifies the order and the distance from zero measures temporal separation.

STDP is called a **local learning rule** because the update can be computed from information available around one synapse: the synaptic weight and the relevant pre- and post-synaptic spike times. Each synapse responds to its own timing relationships. The rule links an event pair directly to a weight change without requiring a network-wide error signal for that update.

**Question.** Does a positive time difference always mean potentiation?

**Answer.** It does under the convention $\Delta t=t_{\text{post}}-t_{\text{pre}}$ used here. Some descriptions define the difference in the reverse order, which reverses the signs on the horizontal axis. Reading an STDP formula therefore begins by checking how $\Delta t$ is defined. The actual event sequence-pre before post or post before pre-determines the learning direction.

A useful mental model is a timing-sensitive adjustment window surrounding each pair of spikes. Place the pre-synaptic spike and post-synaptic spike on a shared timeline, subtract their times to obtain $\Delta t$, use its sign to select potentiation or depression, and use its magnitude to determine how strongly the weight changes. Close pre-before-post events strengthen the pathway strongly, distant pre-before-post events strengthen it gently, close post-before-pre events weaken it strongly, and distant post-before-pre events weaken it gently. In this way, relative timing becomes a compact local signal for shaping synaptic influence.
