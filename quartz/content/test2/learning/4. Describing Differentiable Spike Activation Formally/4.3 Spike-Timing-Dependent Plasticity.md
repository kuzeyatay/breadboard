## Spike-Timing-Dependent Plasticity

A synaptic weight determines how strongly an incoming spike influences a receiving neuron. Once spike timing and synaptic transmission are in place, a natural learning question appears: when should that weight increase, and when should it decrease? Imagine a presynaptic neuron sending a spike through a synapse, followed shortly afterward by a spike from the postsynaptic neuron. The ordering suggests that the incoming event contributed to the receiving neuron's response. If the receiving neuron spikes before the incoming event arrives, the same causal interpretation is weaker. Spike-timing-dependent plasticity, usually abbreviated **STDP**, turns this temporal relationship into a local rule for changing the synaptic weight.

The rule begins with two event times. Let $t_{\mathrm{pre}}$ be the time of the presynaptic spike and $t_{\mathrm{post}}$ be the time of the postsynaptic spike. Their difference defines the **relative spike time**
$$
\Delta t = t_{\mathrm{post}} - t_{\mathrm{pre}}
$$
Here, $\Delta t$ measures how far apart the spikes occur and records their order through its sign. A positive value means the presynaptic spike occurred first because $t_{\mathrm{post}}>t_{\mathrm{pre}}$. A negative value means the postsynaptic spike occurred first because $t_{\mathrm{post}}<t_{\mathrm{pre}}$. For example, if the presynaptic neuron spikes at $20\ \text{ms}$ and the postsynaptic neuron spikes at $25\ \text{ms}$, then
$$
\Delta t = 25\ \text{ms}-20\ \text{ms}=5\ \text{ms}
$$
The positive result records a pre-before-post ordering. If the two times are reversed, then $\Delta t=-5\ \text{ms}$, recording a post-before-pre ordering.

STDP maps this signed time difference to a weight change $\Delta w$. A common formal description uses a piecewise function:
$$
\Delta w =
\begin{cases}
A_{+}\exp\left(-\dfrac{\Delta t}{\tau_{+}}\right), & \Delta t>0,\\[8pt]
-A_{-}\exp\left(\dfrac{\Delta t}{\tau_{-}}\right), & \Delta t<0.
\end{cases}
$$
The symbol $w$ denotes the current synaptic weight, and $\Delta w$ denotes the change applied to it. The constants $A_{+}>0$ and $A_{-}>0$ set the largest magnitudes of strengthening and weakening. The constants $\tau_{+}>0$ and $\tau_{-}>0$ are time scales controlling how rapidly the effect fades as the spikes move farther apart. The exponential function $\exp(x)$ makes nearby spike pairs produce larger changes than widely separated pairs. After computing the change, the updated weight is
$$
W_{\mathrm{new}}=w_{\mathrm{old}}+\Delta w
$$
Where $w_{\mathrm{old}}$ is the weight before learning and $w_{\mathrm{new}}$ is the weight afterward.

The upper branch applies when $\Delta t>0$, so the presynaptic spike arrives before the postsynaptic spike. Every factor in that branch is positive, which gives $\Delta w>0$. Adding a positive change increases the weight. This strengthening is called **timing-dependent potentiation**. The interval also controls the amount of strengthening. When the postsynaptic spike follows almost immediately, $\Delta t$ is small, the exponential factor is close to $1$, and the update approaches $A_{+}$. As the delay grows, the exponential factor shrinks toward zero, so the strengthening becomes smaller.

Suppose $A_{+}=0.02$, $\tau_{+}=10\ \text{ms}$, and the presynaptic spike occurs $5\ \text{ms}$ before the postsynaptic spike. Then $\Delta t=5\ \text{ms}$, giving
$$
\Delta w
=0.02\exp\left(-\frac{5}{10}\right)
\approx 0.02(0.607)
\approx 0.012
$$
A synapse with weight $0.40$ would therefore become approximately $0.412$. If the delay were $20\ \text{ms}$, the exponential factor would be much smaller, and the same spike ordering would cause only a slight increase. The rule therefore treats temporal proximity as evidence about how strongly the two events are related.

The lower branch applies when $\Delta t<0$, meaning the postsynaptic neuron spikes before the presynaptic event. The minus sign in front of $A_{-}$ gives $\Delta w<0$. Adding this negative change reduces the synaptic weight. This weakening is called **timing-dependent depression**. Because $\Delta t$ is already negative, the ratio $\Delta t/\tau_{-}$ is negative, and its exponential decreases as the magnitude of the time gap grows.

For example, let $A_{-}=0.018$, $\tau_{-}=10\ \text{ms}$, and let the postsynaptic spike occur $5\ \text{ms}$ before the presynaptic spike. Then $\Delta t=-5\ \text{ms}$, so
$$
\Delta w
=-0.018\exp\left(\frac{-5}{10}\right)
\approx -0.018(0.607)
\approx -0.011
$$
A weight of $0.40$ would become approximately $0.389$. The ordering determines the direction of learning, while the separation determines its magnitude.

**Question.** Why does a positive relative spike time strengthen the synapse?

**Answer.** With $\Delta t=t_{\mathrm{post}}-t_{\mathrm{pre}}$, a positive value means the presynaptic spike occurred first. The postsynaptic spike then followed within the relevant time window, so the rule assigns a positive weight change. The strength of that change decays as the delay increases because a widely separated pair receives a smaller exponential factor.

A common beginner confusion comes from treating "positive timing" as an intrinsic property of the spike pair. Its meaning depends on how the difference is defined. Here, subtracting presynaptic time from postsynaptic time makes pre-before-post positive. A convention that reverses the subtraction would reverse the signs and would require the piecewise branches to be relabeled accordingly. The reliable procedure is to write the definition of $\Delta t$, determine the spike ordering from that definition, and then select the matching branch.

**Question.** How can the same pair of neurons sometimes strengthen their synapse and sometimes weaken it?

**Answer.** STDP evaluates individual temporal relationships rather than assigning one permanent update direction to a neuron pair. A pre-before-post pairing produces potentiation, while a post-before-pre pairing produces depression. Repeated activity can therefore generate many positive and negative updates, and the synaptic weight reflects their accumulated effect.

STDP is a **local plasticity rule** because the update can be determined from information available around one synapse: the presynaptic spike time, the postsynaptic spike time, and the current connection being modified. The full mental model is a timing-sensitive window centered on a spike pair. One side of the window corresponds to pre-before-post events and raises the weight; the other corresponds to post-before-pre events and lowers it. Near-coincident spikes create the largest updates, and the influence fades as their separation grows. Relative spike timing therefore supplies both pieces of information needed for learning: its sign chooses strengthening or weakening, and its magnitude sets how strongly the synapse changes.
