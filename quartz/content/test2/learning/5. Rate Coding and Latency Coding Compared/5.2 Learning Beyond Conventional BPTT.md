## Learning Beyond Conventional BPTT

Once spike timing has been encoded and surrogate gradients have made threshold crossings trainable, a practical question remains: how should learning move through time? Conventional backpropagation through time, or BPTT, answers by unfolding the spiking network across a sequence of time steps. Each step contributes neuron states and spike events, and the backward pass follows those stored dependencies from later steps toward earlier ones. This gives learning access to temporal cause and effect, but it also ties training to a recorded history of network activity. Long sequences therefore create larger state-storage and backward-computation demands.

The alternatives can be understood by imagining a spiking sensor processing a continuous stream. Most moments may contain little activity, while a few moments contain informative spikes. BPTT organizes learning around the entire unfolded sequence. Event-driven optimization organizes learning around the moments when events occur. Online training organizes learning around the arrival of new information. Implicit differentiation organizes learning around the relationship satisfied by the network's resulting state. Biologically motivated rules such as spike-timing-dependent plasticity organize learning around locally available spike times. Each approach chooses a different answer to the same question: what information should be retained so that a useful parameter update can be computed?

Event-driven learning begins with the sparse nature of spike communication. A neuron emits a discrete event when its internal state reaches a firing condition, and silence carries the information that no firing event occurred. An event-driven optimizer can focus computation on active neurons, active connections, or relevant spike times. Consider a stream containing one spike at time step 20 and another at time step 80. A dense time-stepped procedure still advances through all intermediate steps. An event-driven procedure concentrates its work around the two events and the state changes they trigger. The benefit grows when spikes are sparse because fewer events create fewer opportunities for learning computations.

This approach fits latency coding especially naturally. In latency coding, information is carried by when a spike occurs, such as an earlier spike representing a stronger or more urgent signal. Learning must therefore preserve precise event times. Rate coding places more emphasis on the number of spikes observed over a window. An event-driven method can support either code, but its bookkeeping changes: latency-based learning follows individual event times closely, while rate-based learning may accumulate events across a period before interpreting their combined count. The coding choice therefore shapes which temporal details the optimizer must retain.

Event-driven computation also creates a tradeoff. Focusing on actual spikes preserves the efficiency of sparse activity, while temporal dependencies may extend through quiet intervals because neuron states continue to evolve between spikes. A learning method must still account for the influence of earlier inputs on later firing. Sparse gradient approximations address this tension by restricting parts of the backward computation to the most relevant activity. Their appeal comes from reducing work during the backward pass; their approximation comes from using less of the complete unfolded dependency structure.

Online SNN training changes the timing of parameter updates. Instead of waiting for an entire sequence to finish, an online learner processes information as the stream arrives and updates using the state currently available. Imagine a neuron receiving spikes one moment at a time. After each moment, the learner can carry forward a compact description of the neuron's state and the recent influence of its parameters. When an error or teaching signal becomes available, that carried information supports an immediate update.

The practical attraction is a smaller dependence on storing a complete temporal history. The learner can adapt during an ongoing stream, which aligns with systems that receive events continuously. The central tradeoff concerns temporal credit assignment: a current error may have been influenced by activity many steps earlier. BPTT can revisit those stored steps directly. An online method must preserve enough information as it moves forward to represent the influence of the past. Compact traces reduce storage, while compressed history may provide a less complete account of long-range dependencies.

**Question.** Does online learning mean that each spike immediately changes every synaptic weight?

**Answer.** Online learning means that computation and updates can proceed incrementally as data arrive. The exact update schedule can still depend on when a learning signal is available and which parameters contributed to the current state. A spike may update a local trace immediately, while a weight change may occur when that trace is combined with an error or teaching signal.

Biologically motivated learning pushes locality further. Spike-timing-dependent plasticity uses the relative timing of pre-synaptic and post-synaptic spikes to guide a synaptic change. Its information requirements are local: the synapse needs relevant nearby spike times rather than a globally unfolded backward graph. That locality makes the rule compatible with event-driven operation. Its learning signal, however, differs from the global objective information propagated by BPTT. The resulting tradeoff is between local, timing-based adaptation and direct optimization of a network-level objective. The useful mental connection is locality: event-driven learning decides *when* computation happens, while timing-based plasticity helps decide *which locally observed events* can drive a change.

Implicit differentiation starts from a different view of temporal processing. Suppose repeated SNN dynamics bring the network to a state that satisfies a stable relationship among inputs, neuron states, spikes, and parameters. Instead of explicitly tracing every transition that produced that state, implicit differentiation studies how the satisfied relationship changes when a parameter changes. The network's state is treated as the solution of its dynamics, and the gradient is obtained from the conditions that define that solution.

A simple analogy is adjusting a support under a balanced surface. One approach records every wobble until the surface settles and then retraces the wobbling process. The implicit approach examines the balance condition at the settled position and asks how that condition shifts when the support moves. For an SNN, this can reduce dependence on storing a long sequence of intermediate states. Its demands move elsewhere: the method needs a suitable representation of the network's defining state relationship and a way to solve the associated gradient calculation.

**Question.** Which alternative is the best replacement for BPTT?

**Answer.** The choice follows the learning setting. Sparse event streams favor event-driven computation; continuous adaptation favors online updates; locally available spike timing favors biologically motivated rules; and a suitable settled-state formulation creates an opening for implicit differentiation. Their objectives, approximations, memory needs, and temporal credit mechanisms differ, so a universal ranking would require controlled evidence for a specific task and implementation.

A useful mental model is to picture temporal learning as managing a record of influence. BPTT keeps an explicit record across unfolded time. Event-driven optimization concentrates that record around spikes. Online learning compresses it into information carried forward with the stream. Timing-based local rules store influence near the synapse through spike relationships. Implicit differentiation replaces the recorded trajectory with the relationship satisfied by the resulting state. Rate and latency codes then determine whether the learner must preserve accumulated spike counts, precise event times, or both.
