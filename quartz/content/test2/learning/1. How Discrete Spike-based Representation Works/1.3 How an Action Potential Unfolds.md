## How an Action Potential Unfolds

Once a spike has been recognized as a discrete event in a spike train, the natural question is how a neuron produces that event. Begin with the neuron's membrane voltage: the electrical difference between the inside and outside of the cell. At rest, this voltage remains near a stable negative level. The membrane contains ion channels-protein pathways that allow particular charged particles, called ions, to cross. Some channels respond to the membrane voltage itself. These **voltage-gated ion channels** change state as the voltage changes, and their coordinated opening, closing, and temporary inactivation generates an action potential.

Imagine the membrane voltage receiving a small upward push from incoming signals. A weak push causes only a small voltage change, followed by a return toward rest. A sufficiently strong push reaches a triggering level called the **threshold**. At threshold, many voltage-gated sodium channels begin opening rapidly. Sodium ions then flow through the membrane in a direction that drives the inside of the cell toward a more positive voltage.

This upward movement of membrane voltage is called **depolarization**. The word describes a reduction and eventual reversal of the electrical separation across the membrane. Depolarization also creates a reinforcing process: opening sodium channels raises the voltage, and the higher voltage encourages more voltage-gated sodium channels to open. The membrane voltage therefore rises much more sharply than it did during the initial input. A gradual approach to threshold becomes the steep rising edge of a spike.

A useful analogy is a row of spring-loaded gates. A small disturbance moves one gate slightly, while reaching the release point allows many gates to swing open in quick succession. In the membrane, the "release point" is the threshold, the gates are voltage-gated sodium channels, and the resulting sodium movement produces the rapid voltage rise. This regenerative opening helps make the action potential a clear, brief event rather than a vague continuation of the initial input.

The rising phase cannot continue indefinitely. Voltage-gated sodium channels pass through different functional states. Before the spike, a channel is closed but available to open. During depolarization, it opens and conducts sodium. Soon afterward, it becomes **inactivated**, a temporary state in which sodium flow through that channel stops. Inactivation interrupts the reinforcing sodium current near the top of the action potential.

At about the same time, voltage-gated potassium channels are opening. Their response develops more slowly than the rapid opening of sodium channels. Potassium movement drives the membrane voltage back toward its resting negative level. The combination of reduced sodium entry and increased potassium flow turns the voltage trajectory downward. This falling phase is called **repolarization** because the membrane is recovering its earlier electrical separation.

*Action potential phases and voltage-gated sodium and potassium channel states* *(p. 2)*

Read the figure from the resting state through depolarization, repolarization, and recovery. During rest, the voltage-gated sodium and potassium channels are prepared for a future event. As depolarization begins, sodium channels open quickly, matching the steep rise in voltage. Near the peak, sodium channels enter their inactivated state while potassium channels provide the dominant open pathway. The voltage then falls during repolarization. During recovery, potassium channels close and sodium channels return from inactivation to a closed, available state. The shapes of the spike and the channel states are therefore two views of one mechanism: the voltage changes because the available pathways for ion movement change over time.

**Question.** Why does the voltage begin falling even though depolarization has just opened many sodium channels?

**Answer.** Open sodium channels do not remain conductive throughout the entire spike. They soon become inactivated, reducing the sodium movement that produced the rise. Meanwhile, voltage-gated potassium channels have had time to open. Their effect drives the voltage downward. The falling phase begins when this combination-sodium-channel inactivation and potassium-channel opening-reverses the direction of the membrane-voltage change.

Potassium channels can remain open briefly as the voltage approaches its resting level. Their continued influence may carry the membrane voltage below its usual resting value, producing a short **after-hyperpolarization**, or undershoot. As the potassium channels close, the membrane voltage returns toward resting behavior. Sodium channels also recover from inactivation, becoming closed and ready to open during a later action potential.

This recovery gives the spike a direction in time. Immediately after sodium channels become inactivated, the membrane has fewer sodium channels available to support another rapid rising phase. As those channels recover, the neuron gradually regains its ability to produce another spike. The channel-state cycle therefore helps separate successive action potentials into distinct events.

After connecting each phase to its channels, the complete voltage trajectory becomes easier to interpret:

The graph traces one transient electrical event. The voltage begins near its resting level and then enters a rapid rise as sodium channels open. The peak marks the transition created by sodium-channel inactivation and the growing influence of open potassium channels. The descending portion records repolarization, while the lower recovery region reflects potassium channels remaining open briefly before the membrane returns toward rest. The entire curve represents one action potential unfolding through time, with each change in slope reflecting a change in which voltage-gated channels are available and conducting.

**Question.** Does the peak of the action potential mean that all ion movement has stopped?

**Answer.** The peak marks a change in the balance of channel activity. Sodium-driven depolarization is weakening as sodium channels become inactivated, while potassium-driven repolarization is strengthening as potassium channels open. The voltage stops rising at the turning point because these changing influences shift the membrane from an upward trajectory to a downward one.

The action potential can therefore be understood as a coordinated channel-state cycle. A sufficient voltage increase opens sodium channels, sodium movement produces rapid depolarization, sodium-channel inactivation limits the rise, potassium-channel opening produces repolarization, and the slower closing and resetting of channels completes recovery. The visible spike is the electrical trace of that hidden sequence. Its sharp rise, peak, fall, and return toward rest form a single discrete event because voltage-gated sodium and potassium channels change state in a reliable temporal order.
