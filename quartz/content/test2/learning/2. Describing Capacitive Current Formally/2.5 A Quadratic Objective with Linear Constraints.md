## A Quadratic Objective with Linear Constraints

A network voltage state gives one number for each neuron, so the voltages at a particular moment can be collected into a vector. Once the state is written this way, its evolution can be interpreted geometrically: the state moves through a multidimensional space, with one coordinate axis for each neuron. To connect that motion to optimization, imagine placing a smooth landscape over this state space. Every possible state receives a numerical cost, and lower points on the landscape represent states that better satisfy the network's computational goal. Constraints then mark which parts of the landscape the state is allowed to occupy.

The simplest useful smooth landscape is a **quadratic objective**. For an optimization variable $\mathbf{x}$, write the problem as
$$
\begin{aligned}
\underset{\mathbf{x}}{\operatorname{minimize}}
\quad & f(\mathbf{x})
= \frac{1}{2}\mathbf{x}^{\mathsf T}Q\mathbf{x}
+ \mathbf{c}^{\mathsf T}\mathbf{x} \\
\text{subject to}
\quad & A\mathbf{x}\leq \mathbf{b}.
\end{aligned}
$$
Here, $\mathbf{x}$ is the **optimization variable**, meaning the vector whose value may change while the cost is reduced. In a network interpretation, its components can represent quantities associated with the neurons' collective state. The matrix $Q$ controls the curvature of the cost landscape. The vector $\mathbf{c}$ tilts that landscape, favoring movement in some directions over others. The transpose symbol ${\mathsf T}$ turns a column vector into a row vector so that $\mathbf{x}^{\mathsf T}Q\mathbf{x}$ evaluates to a single number. The factor $1/2$ makes later differentiation cleaner. The matrix $A$ and vector $\mathbf{b}$ describe the linear constraints that determine which values of $\mathbf{x}$ are permitted.

The quadratic term can be understood by beginning with one variable. If
$$
F(x)=\frac{1}{2}qx^2+cx
$$
Then $q$ controls how sharply the curve bends, while $c$ controls its tilt. With $q>0$, the graph has a bowl shape. Moving downhill decreases the objective until the bottom is reached. In several dimensions, $Q$ generalizes this curvature: it determines how rapidly the objective rises as $\mathbf{x}$ moves away from low-cost states and whether some combinations of coordinates are coupled. An off-diagonal entry of $Q$, for example, makes the cost depend on two components together rather than treating each component independently.

The direction of steepest increase is found by taking the gradient:
$$
\nabla f(\mathbf{x})=Q\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{c}
$$
When $Q$ is symmetric. The gradient $\nabla f(\mathbf{x})$ is a vector containing the rate at which the objective changes along each coordinate. Its negative, $-\nabla f(\mathbf{x})$, points downhill. Without constraints, a stationary point satisfies
$$
Q\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{c}=\mathbf{0}
$$
If $Q$ is positive definite, the landscape curves upward in every direction, so this stationary point is the unique minimum. Solving $Q\mathbf{x}=-\mathbf{c}$ then identifies the bottom of the bowl.

Linear constraints can prevent the state from reaching that unconstrained minimum. Consider one row of the matrix inequality,
$$
\mathbf{a}_i^{\mathsf T}\mathbf{x}\leq b_i
$$
The vector $\mathbf{a}_i$ contains the coefficients of constraint $i$, and $b_i$ is its boundary value. The equation $\mathbf{a}_i^{\mathsf T}\mathbf{x}=b_i$ forms a line in two dimensions or a plane in three dimensions. The inequality selects one side of that boundary. Every constraint selects a permitted half-space, and the overlap of all permitted half-spaces forms the **feasible region**: the complete set of states satisfying every constraint simultaneously.

The curved surface in the figure represents the quadratic cost, while the flat boundaries represent linear constraints. Each boundary removes a collection of otherwise available states. Their intersection leaves a smaller feasible region in which the optimizer must remain. A downhill direction that crosses one of the boundary planes cannot be followed indefinitely. Once the state reaches that boundary, its permissible motion is confined to directions that remain inside the feasible region. If the bowl's unconstrained bottom lies outside the allowed region, the constrained solution appears where the lowest reachable part of the quadratic surface touches the feasible set, often on a boundary or at the intersection of several boundaries.

For a concrete two-variable example, suppose the objective is
$$
F(x_1,x_2)=\frac{1}{2}(x_1^2+x_2^2)-2x_1-x_2
$$
With the constraint
$$
X_1+x_2\leq 1
$$
The gradient is
$$
\nabla f(x_1,x_2)=
\begin{bmatrix}
X_1-2\\
X_2-1
\end{bmatrix}
$$
Setting the gradient to zero gives the unconstrained minimum $(x_1,x_2)=(2,1)$. That point violates the constraint because $2+1>1$. The optimizer therefore moves toward lower cost until it reaches the boundary $x_1+x_2=1$, then searches along that boundary for the lowest feasible point. The constraint changes the destination by removing the descent directions that would carry the state into the forbidden side of the line.

**Question.** Does a linear constraint make the objective function linear?

**Answer.** The objective remains quadratic because its value still comes from $\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{x}^{\mathsf T}Q\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{c}^{\mathsf T}\mathbf{x}$. "Linear" describes the shape of each constraint boundary. A constrained quadratic problem therefore combines a curved cost landscape with flat boundaries that carve out the feasible region.

This geometry provides the starting point for interpreting voltage dynamics as optimization dynamics. The quadratic objective supplies a continuous pressure toward lower-cost states through the gradient $Q\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{c}$. The linear inequalities define admissible states and block motion that would violate a constraint. Within this picture, voltage-like quantities can track how close the evolving network is to constraint boundaries, while discrete events can participate in correcting the state when those boundaries become active.

**Question.** Why can the constrained solution lie on a boundary even though the gradient there may be nonzero?

**Answer.** A nonzero gradient may point partly outside the feasible region. At a boundary, only directions that preserve all inequalities are available. The solution is reached when every feasible direction would keep the cost unchanged or increase it, even if an unavailable direction could decrease it. The boundary therefore balances the downhill pull of the quadratic objective against the requirement to remain feasible.

The resulting mental model is a state moving downhill across a curved landscape while flat constraint surfaces guide and confine its path. The vector $\mathbf{x}$ locates the state, $Q$ shapes the landscape, $\mathbf{c}$ tilts it, and $A\mathbf{x}\leq\mathbf{b}$ carves out the region the state may occupy. Constrained quadratic optimization joins these pieces into a formal problem whose dynamics can later be related to the evolving voltages and discrete events of a spiking network.
