## Voltage Dynamics Across a Spiking Network

A leaky integrate-and-fire neuron already gives us a compact picture of one membrane: incoming current raises its voltage, leak pulls the voltage toward rest, and crossing a threshold produces a spike followed by a reset. A network forms when many such neurons exchange spikes. Each membrane voltage then evolves as part of a larger, coupled system: external signals drive the network, emitted spikes travel through weighted connections, and those arriving spikes alter other neurons' future voltages.

Imagine three neurons whose membrane voltages are changing at the same time. An external spike raises the voltage of neuron 1. Neuron 1 reaches threshold and fires, sending events to neurons 2 and 3. The effect on each receiving neuron depends on the relevant **synaptic weight**, which represents the strength and sign of that connection. Neuron 2 may receive a strong positive contribution and approach threshold, while neuron 3 may receive a weaker contribution or one that lowers its voltage. Their responses can generate further spikes, so a single external event may initiate a sequence of interactions across the network.

To describe this process formally, assign neuron $i$ a membrane voltage $V_i(t)$ at time $t$. Collecting all neuron voltages gives the **network voltage state**
$$
\mathbf{V}(t)
=
\begin{bmatrix}
V_1(t)\\
V_2(t)\\
\vdots\\
V_N(t)
\end{bmatrix}
$$
Where $N$ is the number of neurons and $\mathbf{V}(t)$ is a vector containing one voltage per neuron. This vector acts like a snapshot of the network's internal electrical condition. Knowing it tells us which neurons are near threshold, which are near their resting voltage, and how strongly the network is currently poised to produce spikes.

A voltage state changes because charge accumulates on each neuron's membrane. The membrane acts like a capacitor: adding charge changes its voltage, while ion flow through the membrane dissipates stored charge. For neuron $i$, this balance can be written as
$$
C_i\frac{dV_i(t)}{dt}
=
-g_{L,i}\bigl(V_i(t)-E_{L,i}\bigr)
+
I_i^{\mathrm{ext}}(t)
+
\sum_{j=1}^{N} w_{ij}s_j(t)
$$
Here, $C_i$ is the membrane capacitance of neuron $i$, and $dV_i(t)/dt$ is the rate at which its voltage changes. Their product is the capacitive current associated with changing the membrane voltage. The parameter $g_{L,i}$ is the leak conductance, while $E_{L,i}$ is the resting or leak-reversal voltage toward which the membrane relaxes. The function $I_i^{\mathrm{ext}}(t)$ is the **external input drive**, meaning current supplied from outside the recurrent network. The weight $w_{ij}$ gives the influence of neuron $j$ on neuron $i$, and $s_j(t)$ is neuron $j$'s spike train.

A spike train records the times at which a neuron emits discrete events. It can be represented as
$$
S_j(t)=\sum_k \delta\bigl(t-t_j^{(k)}\bigr)
$$
Where $t_j^{(k)}$ is the time of neuron $j$'s $k$-th spike and $\delta$ denotes a sharply localized event at that time. The expression $\sum_j w_{ij}s_j(t)$ therefore computes the total recurrent spike influence arriving at neuron $i$. Each presynaptic spike is scaled by its connection weight before contributing to the receiving neuron's dynamics.

The voltage equation can be read as a running balance. The leak term pulls $V_i(t)$ toward $E_{L,i}$. The external-drive term pushes the neuron according to incoming signals. The recurrent term adds the effects of spikes generated within the network. The remaining current changes the voltage at a rate controlled by the membrane capacitance. A larger capacitance means that the same net current produces a slower voltage change because more charge is required to move the voltage by the same amount.

Consider neuron 2 at a moment when its voltage lies slightly below threshold. Its leak is pulling it gently toward rest, while its external input is small. Neuron 1 then fires through a connection with a positive weight $w_{21}$. The recurrent term for neuron 2 receives a positive event, its membrane voltage rises, and it may cross threshold. If it fires, neuron 2 contributes its own spike to every neuron connected to it. The network consequently evolves through alternating continuous voltage changes and discrete spike events.

The sign of a synaptic weight determines the direction of the immediate influence. A positive $w_{ij}$ raises the receiving neuron's drive when neuron $j$ spikes, making threshold crossing more likely. A negative $w_{ij}$ lowers that drive, moving the receiving neuron away from threshold. The magnitude $\lvert w_{ij}\rvert$ determines how strongly the spike affects the receiver. The full set of weights can be collected into a matrix $\mathbf{W}$, where entry $w_{ij}$ describes the connection from neuron $j$ to neuron $i$.

Using vectors, the interacting dynamics can be written compactly as
$$
\mathbf{C}\frac{d\mathbf{V}(t)}{dt}
=
-\mathbf{G}_L\bigl(\mathbf{V}(t)-\mathbf{E}_L\bigr)
+
\mathbf{I}^{\mathrm{ext}}(t)
+
\mathbf{W}\mathbf{s}(t)
$$
The vector $\mathbf{s}(t)$ contains every neuron's spike train, $\mathbf{I}^{\mathrm{ext}}(t)$ contains their external drives, and $\mathbf{E}_L$ contains their resting voltages. The matrices $\mathbf{C}$ and $\mathbf{G}_L$ contain the membrane capacitances and leak conductances, commonly along their diagonals. Multiplying $\mathbf{W}\mathbf{s}(t)$ routes emitted spikes through the network: each row calculates the weighted recurrent input received by one neuron.

Threshold and reset complete these coupled dynamics. When $V_i(t)$ reaches neuron $i$'s threshold, the neuron emits a spike into $s_i(t)$, and its voltage is reset according to the neuron model. That spike immediately becomes part of the recurrent drive seen by connected neurons. Their resulting spikes may later feed back to neuron $i$. This circular dependence is **recurrent spike interaction**: voltages produce spikes, and spikes reshape the future evolution of voltages.

**Question.** Does the network voltage state include the spikes themselves?

**Answer.** The voltage state $\mathbf{V}(t)$ contains the membrane voltages at a given time. Spike trains are discrete events generated when those voltages reach threshold. The two are linked by the threshold-and-reset rule: voltages determine when spikes occur, while arriving spikes contribute current that changes later voltages.

**Question.** What is the difference between external input drive and recurrent input?

**Answer.** External input drive enters from outside the interacting population, such as an encoded input spike train or another upstream population. Recurrent input is generated by neurons within the network and routed back through the weight matrix. Both alter membrane voltage, but their origins differ.

The resulting mental model is a network of leaky electrical accumulators joined by event-carrying connections. External drive starts or sustains activity, leak continuously removes accumulated influence, and the weight matrix determines how each emitted spike is distributed. Threshold crossings convert evolving voltages into new events, while resets begin another accumulation cycle. SNN voltage dynamics therefore emerge from one repeating loop across the whole population: currents change the network voltage state, the voltage state generates spikes, and those spikes become currents that shape the state's future trajectory.
