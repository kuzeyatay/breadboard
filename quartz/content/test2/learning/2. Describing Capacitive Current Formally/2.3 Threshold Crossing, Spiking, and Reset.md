## Threshold Crossing, Spiking, and Reset

The membrane leak described earlier gives a leaky integrate-and-fire neuron a fading memory: recent inputs raise its membrane voltage, while older contributions gradually lose influence. That continuous accumulation still leaves one practical question unanswered. When does the neuron turn its internal voltage into an event that can be sent to other neurons? The answer comes from adding a firing threshold and a reset rule.

<!-- learning-unit:U6:after-introduction -->

Imagine that a neuron receives several small input pulses close together. Each pulse nudges the membrane voltage upward. Between pulses, leakage pulls the voltage back toward its resting level. A single weak pulse may fade away, while a rapid sequence can accumulate faster than it leaks. Once the accumulated voltage reaches a chosen boundary, the neuron emits a spike. The threshold therefore acts as a decision boundary between continued accumulation and event generation.

Let $u[t]$ denote the membrane voltage at discrete time step $t$. Before checking whether the neuron fires, we compute a candidate voltage:
$$
\tilde{u}[t] = \lambda u[t-1] + I[t]
$$
Here, $\tilde{u}[t]$ is the candidate membrane voltage produced during the current step, $u[t-1]$ is the voltage retained from the previous step, $\lambda$ is the leak factor, and $I[t]$ is the total incoming current at time $t$. The leak factor usually lies between $0$ and $1$. A value near $1$ preserves voltage for longer, while a smaller value makes stored voltage fade more quickly. This equation lets us compute how far the neuron has progressed toward firing before applying the threshold rule.

Suppose the firing threshold is $\vartheta$. The neuron compares its candidate voltage with this threshold and produces a binary spike:
$$
S[t] =
\begin{cases}
1, & \tilde{u}[t] \geq \vartheta,\\
0, & \tilde{u}[t] < \vartheta.
\end{cases}
$$
The symbol $s[t]$ is the neuron's spike output at time $t$. The value $1$ means that a spike occurs during the current step, and the value $0$ means that the neuron remains silent. The symbol $\vartheta$ is the firing threshold. Because $s[t]$ has only two possible values, it records the occurrence of an event rather than the full membrane voltage. Information can still be carried through the times at which the ones appear: a sequence such as $0,0,1,0,1$ contains spikes at the third and fifth time steps.

Consider a neuron with threshold $\vartheta=1.0$, leak factor $\lambda=0.8$, previous voltage $u[t-1]=0.7$, and incoming current $I[t]=0.5$. Its candidate voltage is
$$
\tilde{u}[t]=(0.8)(0.7)+0.5=1.06
$$
Since $1.06\geq1.0$, the neuron emits $s[t]=1$. If the incoming current had instead been $0.3$, the candidate voltage would have been $0.86$, giving $s[t]=0$. The same stored voltage can therefore lead to different outcomes depending on the current input, and the same input can lead to different outcomes depending on the voltage left by earlier inputs.

Spike emission must also change the neuron's internal state. If the voltage remained above threshold after firing, the neuron could emit another spike at every following step without receiving enough new input. A reset rule begins a fresh accumulation cycle by assigning a reset potential $u_{\mathrm{reset}}$ after a spike:
$$
U[t] =
\begin{cases}
U_{\mathrm{reset}}, & \tilde{u}[t] \geq \vartheta,\\
\tilde{u}[t], & \tilde{u}[t] < \vartheta.
\end{cases}
$$
The symbol $u[t]$ now represents the voltage carried into the next time step, and $u_{\mathrm{reset}}$ is the voltage assigned immediately after firing. When the candidate voltage stays below threshold, the neuron preserves that candidate voltage and continues integrating. When the candidate voltage reaches threshold, the neuron emits a spike and replaces the candidate voltage with the reset potential.

The complete update is therefore piecewise: one branch governs subthreshold evolution, while the other governs threshold-triggered emission and reset. "Piecewise" means that the update follows different rules under different conditions. Below threshold, voltage behaves as a continuously changing internal state shaped by integration and leak. At threshold, the output changes abruptly from $0$ to $1$, and the state returns to $u_{\mathrm{reset}}$. The abrupt switch is what converts gradual voltage accumulation into a discrete spike event.

For a concrete sequence, take $\lambda=0.9$, $\vartheta=1.0$, $u_{\mathrm{reset}}=0$, and initial voltage $u[0]=0$. Let the neuron receive currents $I[1]=0.6$, $I[2]=0.5$, and $I[3]=0.4$. At the first step,
$$
\tilde{u}[1]=(0.9)(0)+0.6=0.6
$$
So the output is $s[1]=0$ and the stored voltage becomes $u[1]=0.6$. At the second step,
$$
\tilde{u}[2]=(0.9)(0.6)+0.5=1.04
$$
The threshold is crossed, so $s[2]=1$ and $u[2]=0$. At the third step,
$$
\tilde{u}[3]=(0.9)(0)+0.4=0.4
$$
Giving $s[3]=0$ and $u[3]=0.4$. The second-step spike ends the earlier accumulation cycle. Consequently, the third input begins from the reset potential rather than inheriting the threshold-crossing voltage of $1.04$.

**Question.** Does a binary spike mean that every emitted spike carries exactly the same information?

**Answer.** Each spike has the same binary event value, $s[t]=1$, but the spike time depends on the history of inputs and membrane voltage. A neuron receiving strong or closely spaced inputs may reach threshold sooner or more often. The informative pattern lies in when spikes occur across time and which neurons emit them.

A common confusion arises around the candidate voltage $\tilde{u}[t]$ and the stored voltage $u[t]$. The candidate voltage is the temporary result obtained after integration and leak. It is the value tested against the threshold. The stored voltage is the state retained after the threshold decision and possible reset. During a non-spiking step, these values are equal. During a spiking step, the candidate may lie at or above $\vartheta$, while the stored voltage becomes $u_{\mathrm{reset}}$.

**Question.** What happens when the candidate voltage equals the threshold exactly?

**Answer.** With the condition $\tilde{u}[t]\geq\vartheta$, equality triggers a spike. The neuron emits $s[t]=1$ and sets its stored voltage to $u_{\mathrm{reset}}$. A model must use one consistent boundary convention so that the same voltage always produces the same decision.

The leaky integrate-and-fire neuron can now be pictured as a repeating cycle. Incoming current raises a candidate voltage, leakage controls how much earlier voltage survives, and the threshold tests whether enough evidence has accumulated. Subthreshold steps preserve the evolving voltage. A threshold crossing produces a binary spike and sends the voltage to its reset potential. Through this piecewise update, a continuously changing internal state becomes a timed sequence of discrete events.
