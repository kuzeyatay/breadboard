## The Hodgkin-Huxley Membrane Equation

With membrane potential, voltage-gated ion channels, capacitance, and conductance already in place, we can combine them into one quantitative description of a membrane. Imagine injecting a small electrical current into a patch of neuronal membrane. Some of that current changes the membrane potential by accumulating charge on the membrane surfaces. The rest passes through ion channels carried mainly by sodium and potassium ions, together with a smaller background pathway. To predict how the membrane potential changes, the Hodgkin-Huxley model keeps an account of all these current pathways at every moment.

The membrane's ability to store separated charge makes it behave like a capacitor. For a capacitor, stored charge $Q$ is related to voltage $V$ by
$$
Q = C_m V
$$
Where $Q$ is the electrical charge stored across the membrane, $C_m$ is the membrane capacitance, and $V$ is the membrane potential. Current measures how quickly charge changes, so $I=dQ/dt$. If the capacitance remains constant, differentiating the charge equation gives the **capacitive current**
$$
I_C = C_m\frac{dV}{dt}
$$
Here, $I_C$ is the capacitive current, $C_m$ is the membrane capacitance, $V$ is the membrane potential, $t$ is time, and $dV/dt$ is the rate at which membrane potential changes. This equation lets us compute how much current is being used to change the voltage across the membrane. A rapidly rising membrane potential has a large positive $dV/dt$, so it requires a large capacitive current. A steady membrane potential has $dV/dt=0$, so the capacitor draws no current even though it can still hold separated charge.

The remaining current crosses the membrane through ion-selective pathways. Each pathway behaves like a conductance connected to a voltage source. The conductance describes how easily ions can cross, while the voltage source represents the ion's **reversal potential**: the membrane potential at which that ion's net current is zero. For an ionic pathway $x$, the current takes the form
$$
I_x = g_x(V-E_x)
$$
Where $I_x$ is the ionic current, $g_x$ is the pathway's conductance, $V$ is the current membrane potential, and $E_x$ is its reversal potential. The difference $V-E_x$ is the electrical driving force. The conductance determines the size of the available pathway, while the driving force determines how strongly ions are pushed through it and in which direction under the chosen current convention.

For example, suppose a channel has a conductance of $2\ \text{mS}$, the membrane potential is $-40\ \text{mV}$, and the relevant reversal potential is $+50\ \text{mV}$. The driving force is
$$
V-E_x=-40-50=-90\ \text{mV}
$$
Multiplying conductance by driving force gives a negative current under the usual outward-positive convention. The sign indicates an inward current. This worked interpretation reveals why an open channel alone does not determine current: the separation between $V$ and $E_x$ supplies the driving force.

The Hodgkin-Huxley membrane equation combines the capacitor with sodium, potassium, and leak pathways:
$$
I_m
=
C_m\frac{dV}{dt}
+
G_{\mathrm{Na}}(V-E_{\mathrm{Na}})
+
G_{\mathrm{K}}(V-E_{\mathrm{K}})
+
G_{\mathrm{L}}(V-E_{\mathrm{L}})
$$
In this equation, $I_m$ is the total membrane current supplied to the membrane patch. The term $C_m\,dV/dt$ is the capacitive current. The conductance $g_{\mathrm{Na}}$ and reversal potential $E_{\mathrm{Na}}$ determine the sodium current $I_{\mathrm{Na}}$. The conductance $g_{\mathrm{K}}$ and reversal potential $E_{\mathrm{K}}$ determine the potassium current $I_{\mathrm{K}}$. The conductance $g_{\mathrm{L}}$ and reversal potential $E_{\mathrm{L}}$ determine the leak current $I_{\mathrm{L}}$, a background current representing membrane pathways that remain available outside the main voltage-gated sodium and potassium responses.

The same equation can be rearranged to calculate the rate of voltage change:
$$
\frac{dV}{dt}
=
\frac{
I_m-I_{\mathrm{Na}}-I_{\mathrm{K}}-I_{\mathrm{L}}
}{C_m}
$$
This form says that the membrane potential changes when the supplied current differs from the total ionic current crossing the membrane. A larger capacitance produces a slower voltage change for the same remaining current because more charge must accumulate to move the voltage by a given amount. A smaller capacitance allows the same current imbalance to change the voltage more quickly.

All four branches lie in parallel, so each experiences the same membrane potential $V$. The capacitor branch stores charge. The sodium branch pairs $g_{\mathrm{Na}}$ with $E_{\mathrm{Na}}$, the potassium branch pairs $g_{\mathrm{K}}$ with $E_{\mathrm{K}}$, and the leak branch pairs $g_{\mathrm{L}}$ with $E_{\mathrm{L}}$. Kirchhoff's current law then supplies the governing rule: the total current entering the circuit equals the sum of the currents through these parallel branches. The diagram therefore maps directly onto the four terms of the membrane equation rather than serving as a loose analogy.

The sodium and potassium conductances can change over time because voltage-gated channels open and close in response to membrane conditions. Their current contributions can therefore grow or shrink even while the associated reversal potentials remain fixed over the short interval being modeled. The leak conductance is treated as a simpler background pathway. At each instant, the model evaluates the available conductance of every pathway, multiplies it by its driving force, adds the resulting ionic currents, and uses the remaining current to determine $dV/dt$.

**Question.** If sodium channels open, does the membrane potential immediately become equal to the sodium reversal potential?

**Answer.** Opening sodium channels increases $g_{\mathrm{Na}}$, which increases the sodium current for a given driving force $V-E_{\mathrm{Na}}$. That current changes the charge stored by the membrane capacitor, so the membrane potential moves over time. Potassium current, leak current, capacitance, and any supplied current all participate in the same current balance. The sodium reversal potential indicates the voltage toward which sodium current drives the membrane; the rate and actual trajectory come from the complete equation.

**Question.** Why does the capacitive term use $dV/dt$ while the ionic terms use $V-E_x$?

**Answer.** A capacitor carries current when its stored charge changes, and stored charge is proportional to membrane voltage, giving $I_C=C_m\,dV/dt$. An ion channel carries current when ions have both an available pathway and an electrical driving force, giving $I_x=g_x(V-E_x)$. The two mathematical forms describe two distinct physical roles joined in one circuit.

The Hodgkin-Huxley membrane can therefore be pictured as a charge-storing capacitor surrounded by parallel ionic pathways. Incoming membrane current is continually divided among changing the stored charge, carrying sodium ions, carrying potassium ions, and passing through the leak pathway. Their balance determines the slope of the membrane potential at each moment, and following that slope through time produces the membrane's evolving voltage.
