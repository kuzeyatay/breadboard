## Leaky Integration of Synaptic Input

Membrane potential gives a neuron a short-lived memory of earlier synaptic input, while spike encoding turns each incoming event into a precisely timed contribution to that memory. Suppose several presynaptic neurons send spikes toward one receiving neuron. A single incoming spike may be too weak to produce a large change, yet several nearby spikes can build on one another. The receiving neuron therefore needs a state that carries recent input forward through time. At the same time, old input should gradually lose influence so that events from the distant past do not remain stored forever. A **leaky integrate-and-fire neuron** captures these two behaviors with one evolving quantity: its membrane potential.

Begin with integration. Here, **temporal integration** means combining inputs that arrive at different times. Imagine that an incoming spike raises the membrane potential by two units. If another spike arrives soon afterward and contributes three more units, the neuron can carry part of the earlier two-unit contribution forward and combine it with the new three-unit contribution. The membrane potential acts like a running total with memory.

That memory fades through **membrane leakage**. During a time step with no incoming spikes, the stored membrane potential moves closer to its baseline value. A useful analogy is water held in a container with a small drain. Incoming spikes add water, while the drain steadily removes a fraction of what is already present. Water added recently remains available to combine with another pour; water added much earlier has had more time to drain away. In the same way, closely spaced spikes can reinforce one another more strongly than widely separated spikes.

To describe this process, divide time into equal steps labeled $t=0,1,2,\ldots$. Let $u_i[t]$ be the membrane potential of neuron $i$ at time step $t$. Before adding new input, leakage leaves only a fraction of this stored value. Introduce a **decay factor** $\lambda$, with $0 \leq \lambda \leq 1$. After one step, the retained part of the previous potential is
$$
\lambda u_i[t]
$$
Here, $u_i[t]$ is the potential already stored in neuron $i$, and $\lambda$ is the fraction that survives one time step. A value of $\lambda=0.9$ means that 90 percent of the stored potential carries forward. Repeated leakage compounds over time: after two silent steps, the retained amount is $\lambda^2u_i[t]$; after $k$ silent steps, it is $\lambda^k u_i[t]$. The exponent $k$ counts elapsed time steps, so this expression lets us compute how strongly an earlier state still influences the neuron after a delay.

The new synaptic input must then be added. Let $s_j[t]$ represent whether presynaptic neuron $j$ sends a spike during time step $t$. In a simple discrete spike representation, $s_j[t]=1$ when a spike arrives and $s_j[t]=0$ when none arrives. Let $w_{ij}$ be the synaptic weight from neuron $j$ to neuron $i$. The product
$$
W_{ij}s_j[t]
$$
Is the contribution made by that connection during the current step. When a spike arrives, multiplying by $1$ includes the weight; during silence, multiplying by $0$ contributes nothing. With several presynaptic neurons, their contributions add:
$$
\sum_j w_{ij}s_j[t]
$$
The symbol $\sum_j$ means "add over all presynaptic neurons $j$ connected to neuron $i$." Each $w_{ij}$ determines the size and direction of one connection's influence, and each $s_j[t]$ determines whether that influence is active at time $t$.

Combining retained voltage with incoming synaptic contributions gives the discrete leaky integration rule
$$
U_i[t+1]
=
\lambda u_i[t]
+
\sum_j w_{ij}s_j[t]
$$
The left side, $u_i[t+1]$, is the membrane potential at the next time step. The first term on the right, $\lambda u_i[t]$, is the decayed portion of the previous membrane potential. The second term, $\sum_j w_{ij}s_j[t]$, is the total weighted input carried by spikes arriving during the current step. The equation computes the neuron's next internal state from its fading memory and its newest events.

Consider a neuron with current potential $u_i[t]=4$ and decay factor $\lambda=0.8$. Leakage retains
$$
0.8 \times 4 = 3.2
$$
Now suppose three presynaptic neurons have weights $1.5$, $2.0$, and $0.5$, while their spike states are $1$, $0$, and $1$. Their total contribution is
$$
(1.5)(1)+(2.0)(0)+(0.5)(1)=2.0
$$
The updated potential is therefore
$$
U_i[t+1]=3.2+2.0=5.2
$$
This value has a clear interpretation: $3.2$ units come from the neuron's fading memory, and $2.0$ units come from current synaptic events. If the same neuron receives no spikes during the following step, its potential becomes $0.8\times5.2=4.16$. Silence still produces a meaningful update because leakage continues to reshape the stored state.

**Question.** How can two individually small spikes produce a larger response when they arrive close together?

**Answer.** The first spike raises the membrane potential, and the decay factor preserves part of that increase into the following step. When the second spike arrives, its weighted contribution is added to the retained potential. For example, with $\lambda=0.9$, a two-unit contribution leaves $1.8$ units after one silent update. A second two-unit contribution then produces $1.8+2=3.8$. A longer delay applies the decay factor more times, leaving less of the first contribution available for combination.

The decay factor also determines the neuron's effective memory span. When $\lambda$ is close to $1$, stored potential fades slowly, allowing inputs separated by several steps to combine. When $\lambda$ is smaller, the potential fades quickly, emphasizing events that occur close together. The size of a time step is therefore part of the interpretation: $\lambda$ describes retention per step, so changing the duration represented by one step changes the physical time associated with the decay.

**Question.** Does temporal integration mean simply counting incoming spikes?

**Answer.** Temporal integration combines weighted spikes with a decaying internal state. Two spikes can contribute different amounts because their synaptic weights can differ, and their arrival times affect how much earlier input remains. A spike arriving through a weight of $2$ adds twice the contribution of a spike arriving through a weight of $1$. Likewise, two spikes separated by one step overlap more strongly than two spikes separated by many steps because the earlier contribution undergoes less decay.

The complete mental model is a leaky accumulator advancing one time step at a time. Its membrane potential stores the recent history of synaptic events, the decay factor weakens that history as it ages, and the weighted spike sum adds what has just arrived. Each update blends memory with new evidence, allowing nearby events to cooperate while letting older events gradually fade.
