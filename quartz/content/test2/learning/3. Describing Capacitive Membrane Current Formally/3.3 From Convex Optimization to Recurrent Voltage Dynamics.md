## From Convex Optimization to Recurrent Voltage Dynamics

The feasible region developed earlier tells us which values of an optimization variable are allowed. Now imagine that the variable is moving downhill toward the minimum of an objective, while several boundaries surround its path. Ordinary gradient descent supplies the downhill motion, but a spiking network also needs a mechanism that reacts whenever the motion reaches a boundary. By interpreting each boundary value as a membrane voltage and each boundary correction as a spike-triggered event, the optimization process becomes a recurrent voltage dynamics.

Let $y\in\mathbb{R}^{M}$ be the variable the system must choose. A useful objective assigns a cost to every possible $y$:
$$
E(y)=\frac{\lambda}{2}y^{\mathsf T}y+b^{\mathsf T}y,
\qquad
\text{subject to }
Fx-Gy\leq T
$$
Here, $E(y)$ is the scalar cost to minimize; $y^{\mathsf T}y$ is the squared length of $y$; $\lambda>0$ controls how strongly large values of $y$ are penalized; and $b\in\mathbb{R}^{M}$ supplies a linear preference over directions. The vector $x$ represents an input, $F$ maps that input into the constraint space, $G$ maps the optimization variable into the same space, and $T$ contains the boundary values. The inequality is applied component by component, so every row of $Fx-Gy\leq T$ defines one constraint.

The objective is quadratic because $y^{\mathsf T}y$ contains squared components. In two dimensions, its graph has the shape of a bowl. The linear term $b^{\mathsf T}y$ tilts that bowl, shifting the location of its lowest point. The parameter $\lambda$ controls its curvature: a larger $\lambda$ makes movement away from the minimum more costly.

To turn this static optimization problem into motion, let $y$ change continuously with time. Gradient flow points the velocity in the direction of steepest decrease:
$$
\dot y=-\frac{\partial E}{\partial y}
$$
The dot in $\dot y$ means $dy/dt$, the rate at which $y$ changes. Differentiating the quadratic term gives $\lambda y$, while differentiating the linear term gives $b$. The resulting dynamics are
$$
\dot y=-\lambda y-b
$$
This equation lets us compute the instantaneous velocity of $y$ from its current value. For a one-dimensional example, take $\lambda=2$ and $b=-6$. Then $\dot y=-2y+6$. At $y=1$, the velocity is $4$, so $y$ moves upward. At $y=5$, the velocity is $-4$, so $y$ moves downward. At $y=3$, the velocity is zero. The motion therefore settles at the minimum $y=3$.

The constraints add event-like corrections to this smooth flow. When $y$ reaches a boundary, a brief restoring event redirects it toward the feasible region. Write these events as a vector $s(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{N}$, with one component for each of the $N$ constraints. Each event is represented by a delta function: it is concentrated at a particular time and has a finite integrated effect even though its duration is idealized as instantaneous. A matrix $D\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times N}$ converts the active boundary events into corrections of $y$:
$$
\dot y=-\lambda y-b+Ds(t)
$$
Each column of $D$ gives the direction in which $y$ is pushed when the corresponding constraint is reached. Choosing these directions from the constraint normals produces an orthogonal reflection away from the touched boundary. Since the rows of $G$ define how $y$ approaches each boundary, the matching correction directions are aligned with those rows, expressed as columns under the matrix convention used for $D$.

A simple analogy is a ball rolling downhill inside a walled region. The slope continuously accelerates the ball toward lower cost, while contact with a wall produces a short impulse. The trajectory combines smooth motion between contacts with abrupt corrections at contact times. In the optimization dynamics, $s(t)$ records those contact times.

**Question.** Why use a delta function for a boundary correction rather than a large ordinary force?

**Answer.** The delta function captures an idealized event whose effect is concentrated at one instant. Its amplitude represents an impulse, so integrating the dynamics across that instant produces a finite change in $y$. This separates the continuous downhill flow from discrete boundary events and creates the same continuous-between-events structure used in spiking dynamics.

The bridge to membrane voltage appears when the constraint quantity itself is given a voltage interpretation. Define
$$
V=Fx-Gy,\qquad V\leq T
$$
The vector $V\in\mathbb{R}^{N}$ contains neuron-like voltages, and $T\in\mathbb{R}^{N}$ contains their thresholds. A component $V_i$ reaching $T_i$ means that its associated constraint boundary has been reached. Differentiating the voltage definition gives
$$
\dot V=F\dot x-G\dot y
$$
Where $\dot x$ is the input's rate of change. Substituting the boundary-corrected gradient flow yields
$$
\dot V
=F\dot x+\lambda Gy+Gb-GDs(t)
$$
Every term now has a dynamical interpretation. The term $F\dot x$ carries changes in the external input into voltage space. The term $\lambda Gy$ arises from the quadratic objective's restoring gradient. The constant term $Gb$ carries the objective's linear preference into the voltage dynamics. The event term $-GDs(t)$ changes voltages whenever a boundary event occurs.

The remaining $y$ can be eliminated using $V=Fx-Gy$, which implies $Gy=Fx-V$. Substitution produces
$$
\dot V
=-\lambda V+F(\lambda x+\dot x)+Gb-GDs(t)
$$
This is the recurrent voltage formulation. The term $-\lambda V$ makes voltage decay continuously, much like a leak. The transformed input $F(\lambda x+\dot x)$ drives the neurons from the current input and its changes. The bias $Gb$ reflects the linear part of the objective. The matrix $-GD$ maps the event vector $s(t)$ back into voltage changes, so one boundary event can alter multiple voltages. That event-mediated coupling supplies the recurrent interaction.

**Question.** How can a boundary event be interpreted as a spike?

**Answer.** Each voltage component measures proximity to one constraint threshold. When $V_i$ reaches $T_i$, the corresponding component $s_i(t)$ emits an event. Through $Ds(t)$, that event corrects the optimization variable; through $-GDs(t)$, it simultaneously changes the network's voltages. A spike therefore marks boundary contact and performs the correction needed to continue the constrained descent.

The full mental model is a quadratic bowl enclosed by constraint boundaries. Gradient flow moves $y$ down the bowl, threshold voltages track its distance from the boundaries, and spikes occur at boundary contacts. Each spike applies a restoring impulse and propagates through recurrent connections, allowing continuous voltage evolution and discrete events to implement one constrained optimization process.
