## The Leaky Integrate-and-Fire Neuron

The capacitive view of a membrane gives us a useful starting point: incoming current changes the membrane potential by adding or removing electrical charge. To describe a spiking neuron over discrete time steps, imagine checking that membrane potential at regular intervals. During each interval, incoming spikes push the voltage upward or downward, some previously stored voltage fades away, and the neuron emits a spike if the resulting voltage reaches a chosen threshold. The leaky integrate-and-fire neuron turns this sequence into a compact mathematical rule.

Consider a neuron receiving signals from several other neurons. Each incoming spike has an associated **weight**, which describes the strength and direction of its effect. A positive weight raises the receiving neuron's membrane potential, while a negative weight lowers it. If several incoming spikes arrive during the same time step, their weighted effects are added together. This accumulation is called **voltage integration** because the neuron combines input arriving over both connections and time.

Let $X_t$ represent the incoming spike pattern at time step $t$. Each element of $X_t$ indicates whether a particular input neuron spiked during that step. Let $W$ contain the corresponding connection weights. The product
$$
WX_t
$$
Computes the total weighted input received at time $t$. For a single neuron with three inputs, suppose the weights are $0.4$, $0.7$, and $-0.2$, while the incoming spike pattern is $X_t=(1,1,0)$. The weighted input is
$$
WX_t=(0.4)(1)+(0.7)(1)+(-0.2)(0)=1.1
$$
The two active inputs therefore raise the membrane potential by $1.1$ voltage units during that step. The inactive input contributes nothing because its spike value is zero.

A membrane also gradually loses stored voltage. The leaky integrate-and-fire model represents this **membrane leak** by retaining only a fraction of the previous membrane potential. If $V_{t-1}$ is the voltage from the preceding time step, then
$$
\lambda V_{t-1}
$$
Is the voltage that remains after leakage. Here, $\lambda$ is the membrane leakage constant, with $0<\lambda<1$. A value of $\lambda$ close to $1$ means voltage fades slowly, so inputs can accumulate across many time steps. A smaller value means voltage fades quickly, so spikes must arrive closer together to produce a strong combined effect.

For example, if $V_{t-1}=0.8$ and $\lambda=0.9$, the retained voltage is
$$
\lambda V_{t-1}=(0.9)(0.8)=0.72
$$
Before any new input arrives, $0.08$ voltage units have leaked away. If the neuron then receives weighted input $WX_t=0.3$, its updated voltage becomes $0.72+0.3=1.02$. Leakage therefore gives timing a practical role: closely spaced inputs can build on one another, while widely separated inputs may fade before they can combine.

The integration and leakage processes come together in the subthreshold update
$$
\widetilde{V}_t=\lambda V_{t-1}+WX_t
$$
The symbol $\widetilde{V}_t$ denotes the candidate membrane potential after retaining part of the previous voltage and adding the current weighted input. The symbol $V_{t-1}$ is the membrane potential at the preceding time step, $\lambda$ is the retained fraction of that voltage, $W$ contains synaptic weights, and $X_t$ contains the incoming spikes or external input at the current step. This formula lets us compute how strongly the neuron is activated before deciding whether it fires.

The firing decision uses a **threshold voltage**, written $V_{\mathrm{th}}$. The neuron's spike at time $t$ is represented by a binary variable $S_t$:
$$
S_t=
\begin{cases}
1, & \widetilde{V}_t\geq V_{\mathrm{th}},\\
0, & \widetilde{V}_t<V_{\mathrm{th}}.
\end{cases}
$$
Here, $S_t=1$ means the neuron emits a spike, while $S_t=0$ means it remains silent. The threshold converts a continuously changing membrane potential into a discrete event. For instance, with $V_{\mathrm{th}}=1$, the candidate voltage $\widetilde{V}_t=1.02$ produces $S_t=1$. A candidate voltage of $0.96$ produces $S_t=0$, and the remaining voltage can continue contributing to later time steps.

After a spike, the membrane potential must be reset so the same accumulated voltage does not trigger the firing decision repeatedly. One compact form combines integration, spike generation, and a threshold-sized reset:
$$
V_t=\lambda V_{t-1}+WX_t-S_tV_{\mathrm{th}}
$$
In this equation, $V_t$ is the membrane potential carried into the following step. The term $S_tV_{\mathrm{th}}$ applies the reset conditionally. When $S_t=0$, the reset term is zero and the candidate voltage is retained. When $S_t=1$, one threshold unit is subtracted from the voltage. This is often called a threshold reset or subtractive reset.

Suppose $\widetilde{V}_t=1.15$, $V_{\mathrm{th}}=1$, and therefore $S_t=1$. The updated voltage becomes
$$
V_t=1.15-(1)(1)=0.15
$$
The neuron emits a spike and carries the remaining $0.15$ voltage units forward. If $\widetilde{V}_t=0.85$, then $S_t=0$, giving $V_t=0.85$. The neuron stays silent and preserves its subthreshold activation, subject to leakage during the following step.

**Question.** Does the neuron lose all memory of earlier inputs whenever a time step passes?

**Answer.** The leakage factor determines how much memory survives. At each step, the previous voltage is multiplied by $\lambda$. With $\lambda=0.9$, a voltage contribution retained without further input becomes $90\%$ of its previous value after one step, $81\%$ after two steps, and $72.9\%$ after three steps. Earlier inputs therefore leave a gradually fading trace that can combine with later spikes.

A related LIF description uses a fixed resting potential $V_r$ after firing. Its dynamics can be written as a piecewise rule:
$$
V_t=
\begin{cases}
F(V_{t-1},X_t), & F(V_{t-1},X_t)<V_{\mathrm{th}},\\
V_r, & F(V_{t-1},X_t)\geq V_{\mathrm{th}}.
\end{cases}
$$
Here, $F(V_{t-1},X_t)$ is the voltage update produced by leakage and incoming input, $V_{\mathrm{th}}$ is the firing threshold, and $V_r$ is the reset or resting potential. When the updated voltage remains below threshold, the neuron continues integrating. When it reaches threshold, the neuron emits a spike and sets its voltage to $V_r$. For example, if $F(V_{t-1},X_t)=1.2$, $V_{\mathrm{th}}=1$, and $V_r=0$, the neuron spikes and begins the following integration cycle from zero.

**Question.** Why can the reset rule be written either as subtraction or as a jump to $V_r$?

**Answer.** Both forms express spike-triggered reset dynamics. Subtracting $V_{\mathrm{th}}$ preserves any voltage above the threshold, while assigning $V_r$ starts the neuron from a specified resting value. A model chooses one rule and applies it consistently during the discrete-time update.

The complete mental model is a leaky accumulator followed by an event decision. At every time step, part of the old membrane potential survives, weighted incoming spikes modify it, the threshold converts sufficient accumulated voltage into a spike, and the reset prepares the neuron to integrate again. Repeating that cycle turns a stream of discrete inputs into a timed sequence of output spikes.
