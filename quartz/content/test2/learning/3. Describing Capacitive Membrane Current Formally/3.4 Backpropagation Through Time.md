## Backpropagation Through Time

A membrane potential already gives a spiking neuron a memory of its recent inputs. Charge accumulated during earlier timesteps remains in the membrane, usually with some decay, and influences whether the neuron spikes later. Training therefore has to follow two paths at once: signals move forward through the network's layers, while membrane states carry information forward through time. When a spike at a late timestep contributes to an error, the weight update may depend on inputs and membrane potentials from many earlier timesteps.

Imagine a neuron receiving a weak input at each of three successive moments. No individual input is large enough to produce a spike, but their accumulated effect raises the membrane potential until the third input crosses the firing threshold. If the resulting spike increases the loss, assigning responsibility only to the third input would miss the accumulation that made the spike possible. The earlier inputs affected earlier membrane states, those states affected the final membrane potential, and the final potential determined the spike. Training needs a chain of derivatives that follows this entire temporal route.

Backpropagation through time, abbreviated **BPTT**, exposes that route by **temporally unrolling** the neuron. The same neuron is represented as a sequence of connected copies, one for each simulation timestep. These copies share the same trainable weights, but each holds the membrane state reached at its own time. If $u[t]$ denotes the membrane potential at timestep $t$, a general recurrent update can be written as
$$
U[t] = F\bigl(u[t-1], x[t], W\bigr)
$$
Where $F$ is the membrane-update rule, $u[t-1]$ is the previous membrane potential, $x[t]$ is the input arriving at the current timestep, and $W$ is a trainable weight. This formula computes the current membrane state from the state carried out of the past, the new input, and the parameter being learned.

For a simple interpretation, suppose $W$ scales the incoming signal. A larger $W$ makes the same input $x[t]$ inject more current into the neuron. That change immediately affects $u[t]$, then persists through the recurrence and can affect $u[t+1]$, $u[t+2]$, and later states. A single shared weight consequently has a direct effect at every timestep and an indirect effect through every state that carries its influence forward.

Training begins with a loss that measures the discrepancy between the desired and produced behavior. Because an SNN generates activity over a sequence, the total loss can accumulate contributions from multiple timesteps:
$$
\mathcal{L} = \sum_{t=1}^{T} \mathcal{L}[t]
$$
Here, $\mathcal{L}$ is the total loss over the simulation, $\mathcal{L}[t]$ is the loss contribution at timestep $t$, and $T$ is the number of simulated timesteps. Differentiating this sum with respect to a shared weight gives
$$
\frac{d\mathcal{L}}{dW}
=
\sum_{t=1}^{T}
\frac{d\mathcal{L}[t]}{dW}
$$
This is temporal loss accumulation: the gradient for $W$ collects the weight's contribution to the loss at every timestep. A weight that produces a small helpful effect at several times can receive a substantial accumulated update, while harmful and helpful contributions at different times can partially offset one another.

Suppose a four-timestep simulation has weight-gradient contributions $0.2$, $0.1$, $-0.4$, and $0.3$. The total gradient is $0.2+0.1-0.4+0.3=0.2$. Training uses the combined value because the same weight generated all four temporal effects. Updating it from only the final contribution, $0.3$, would discard information about how it shaped the earlier sequence.

The dependence inside each term can be exposed with the chain rule. Let $s[t]$ denote the neuron's spike output at timestep $t$, produced from its membrane potential $u[t]$. Then a loss contribution that depends on the spike has the gradient
$$
\frac{d\mathcal{L}[t]}{dW}
=
\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}[t]}{\partial s[t]}
\frac{\partial s[t]}{\partial u[t]}
\frac{d u[t]}{dW}
$$
The factor $\partial \mathcal{L}[t]/\partial s[t]$ measures how a change in the spike output would change the loss at time $t$. The factor $\partial s[t]/\partial u[t]$ connects the spike to the membrane potential that produced it. The factor $d u[t]/dW$ measures how the shared weight influences that potential. Together, the factors carry responsibility from the loss, through the emitted spike, into the neuron's internal state, and finally to the weight.

The state derivative $d u[t]/dW$ contains both a current dependency and a prior-state dependency. Differentiating the recurrent update gives
$$
\frac{d u[t]}{dW}
=
\frac{\partial u[t]}{\partial W}
+
\frac{\partial u[t]}{\partial u[t-1]}
\frac{d u[t-1]}{dW}
$$
The direct term $\partial u[t]/\partial W$ measures how $W$ affects the current update at timestep $t$. The recurrent term multiplies $\partial u[t]/\partial u[t-1]$, which measures how strongly the current membrane state depends on the preceding state, by $d u[t-1]/dW$, which already contains the weight's earlier effects. This recursive expression lets the gradient travel backward from the current state into progressively earlier states.

Expanding it once makes the temporal structure visible:
$$
\frac{d u[t]}{dW}
=
\frac{\partial u[t]}{\partial W}
+
\frac{\partial u[t]}{\partial u[t-1]}
\left(
\frac{\partial u[t-1]}{\partial W}
+
\frac{\partial u[t-1]}{\partial u[t-2]}
\frac{d u[t-2]}{dW}
\right)
$$
Now the weight can influence $u[t]$ directly, through $u[t-1]$, or through the longer path from $u[t-2]$ to $u[t-1]$ and then to $u[t]$. Continuing the expansion reaches every earlier state connected to the current one. For an earlier timestep $k<t$, the strength of the temporal path can be expressed as
$$
\frac{\partial u[t]}{\partial u[k]}
=
\prod_{j=k+1}^{t}
\frac{\partial u[j]}{\partial u[j-1]}
$$
Here, $k$ identifies an earlier timestep, $j$ indexes each transition along the path, and the product multiplies the recurrent-state derivatives from time $k$ through time $t$. This quantity computes how much a small change in the earlier membrane potential $u[k]$ would affect the later potential $u[t]$.

**Question.** Why is the same weight included in gradient calculations at many timesteps?

**Answer.** The neuron reuses $W$ during every state update. Its use at time $t$ changes the current membrane potential directly, while its earlier uses remain embedded in recurrent states. The correct gradient adds all of these temporal contributions before updating the single shared parameter.

**Question.** What does "backward through time" mean physically for the membrane state?

**Answer.** The membrane potential evolves forward during simulation. During training, the chain rule traces causal dependencies in reverse: a later loss points to a later spike, the spike points to its membrane potential, and that potential points to preceding membrane states and earlier uses of the weight.

The resulting mental model is an unrolled chain of membrane states joined by recurrence. Forward simulation moves input and stored charge along that chain until spikes and losses are produced. BPTT starts from those losses, gathers contributions across time, and follows each contribution backward through the spike and the connected membrane states. The final weight gradient therefore records both what the weight did at the current timestep and what its earlier effects became after being carried through the neuron's temporal memory.
