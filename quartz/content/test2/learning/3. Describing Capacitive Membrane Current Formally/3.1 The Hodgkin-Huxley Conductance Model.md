## The Hodgkin-Huxley Conductance Model

The changing membrane potential developed in the previous section can now be described as an electrical accounting problem. Imagine pausing at one instant while current reaches a neuron. Some charge accumulates across the membrane, while some flows through sodium channels, potassium channels, and small leakage pathways. The Hodgkin-Huxley conductance model tracks these four destinations and uses their balance to determine how quickly the membrane voltage changes.

The membrane can store separated charge because its inner and outer surfaces are divided by a thin insulating layer. Electrically, this arrangement behaves like a **capacitor**, a component that stores charge when a voltage exists across it. The relevant voltage is the membrane potential $V_m$, which measures the electrical potential inside the neuron relative to the outside. The membrane capacitance $C_m$ measures how much charge must be separated to produce a given change in that voltage.

A capacitor draws current whenever its voltage changes. Its current is
$$
I_C = C_m\frac{dV_m}{dt}
$$
Where $I_C$ is the capacitive membrane current, $C_m$ is the membrane capacitance, $V_m$ is the membrane potential, $t$ is time, and $\frac{dV_m}{dt}$ is the rate at which the membrane potential changes. This expression lets us compute how much current is being used to change the stored charge across the membrane. A large capacitance requires more current to produce the same rate of voltage change. Equivalently, if two membranes receive the same capacitive current, the membrane with the larger capacitance changes voltage more slowly.

Current also crosses the membrane through ion pathways. Hodgkin-Huxley represents each pathway using a conductance multiplied by a voltage difference. Conductance describes how readily current can flow: a high conductance corresponds to an open, effective pathway, while a low conductance corresponds to little flow. For an ion pathway $i$, the current has the form
$$
I_i = g_i(V_m-V_i)
$$
Where $I_i$ is the current through pathway $i$, $g_i$ is that pathway's conductance, and $V_i$ is its **reversal potential**. The difference $V_m-V_i$ supplies the electrical driving force. At $V_m=V_i$, this driving force is zero. As $V_m$ moves to either side of $V_i$, the sign of the difference changes, representing a reversal in the direction of current flow.

For example, suppose a pathway has a reversal potential of $-70\ \text{mV}$. At a membrane potential of $-50\ \text{mV}$, the driving difference is
$$
V_m-V_i=(-50)-(-70)=20\ \text{mV}
$$
At $-80\ \text{mV}$, the difference becomes
$$
V_m-V_i=(-80)-(-70)=-10\ \text{mV}
$$
The changed sign records the changed current direction under the equation's sign convention. The magnitude of the current also depends on $g_i$: doubling the conductance doubles the current for the same driving difference.

The full membrane-current balance combines the capacitive contribution with sodium, potassium, and leak currents:
$$
I
=
C_m\frac{dV_m}{dt}
+
G_K(V_m-V_K)
+
G_{Na}(V_m-V_{Na})
+
G_l(V_m-V_l)
$$
Here, $I$ is the total current associated with the membrane; $C_m\frac{dV_m}{dt}$ is the capacitive current; $g_K$, $g_{Na}$, and $g_l$ are the potassium, sodium, and leak conductances; and $V_K$, $V_{Na}$, and $V_l$ are their corresponding reversal potentials. The subscripts $K$, $Na$, and $l$ identify potassium, sodium, and leakage pathways. Rearranging the balance isolates the voltage-change rate:
$$
\frac{dV_m}{dt}
=
\frac{
I
-
G_K(V_m-V_K)
-
G_{Na}(V_m-V_{Na})
-
G_l(V_m-V_l)
}{C_m}
$$
This form computes how rapidly the membrane potential changes from the current available after accounting for flow through the three conductive pathways. The capacitance scales that change: the same remaining current produces a slower voltage movement when $C_m$ is larger.

The equivalent circuit makes the current balance visible. The capacitor branch corresponds to $C_m\frac{dV_m}{dt}$, where current changes the charge stored across the membrane. The sodium branch corresponds to $g_{Na}(V_m-V_{Na})$, the potassium branch to $g_K(V_m-V_K)$, and the leak branch to $g_l(V_m-V_l)$. Because these branches are arranged in parallel, they all experience the same membrane potential $V_m$, while each conductive branch has its own conductance and reversal potential. The total current is therefore divided among parallel contributions: charge storage, sodium flow, potassium flow, and leakage.

This circuit picture also clarifies how channel behavior shapes voltage. Increasing sodium conductance strengthens the sodium-current branch at the present driving force. Increasing potassium conductance strengthens the potassium-current branch. The leak conductance provides an additional pathway associated with ordinary membrane leakage. As conductances and driving forces change, the division of current changes, and the capacitive branch receives the portion that determines $\frac{dV_m}{dt}$.

**Question.** If sodium conductance is large, does the sodium current always have a large magnitude?

**Answer.** The magnitude depends on both factors in $I_{Na}=g_{Na}(V_m-V_{Na})$. A large $g_{Na}$ creates a highly conductive pathway, yet the current becomes small when $V_m$ lies close to $V_{Na}$ because the driving difference is small. Conductance tells us how readily current can flow, while $V_m-V_{Na}$ tells us how strongly the present voltage drives that flow.

**Question.** Why does capacitance appear in the equation when sodium, potassium, and leak already account for membrane current?

**Answer.** Ion currents describe charge crossing through conductive pathways, while capacitive current describes charge accumulating on the membrane's two surfaces. That accumulation changes $V_m$. The term $C_m\frac{dV_m}{dt}$ therefore links the current balance to voltage dynamics and tells us how quickly the electrical state of the membrane evolves.

A useful mental model is a shared voltage connected to four parallel routes. Three routes carry current through sodium, potassium, and leak conductances, with each route guided by its own reversal potential. The remaining route stores charge in the membrane capacitance. At every moment, the total current is distributed across these routes, and the capacitive share sets the rate of membrane-voltage change. The Hodgkin-Huxley model turns membrane dynamics into this continuously updated balance between charge storage and ion flow.
