A surrogate gradient gives us a way to train through a spike, but applying that gradient to every neuron at every simulated time step can still require a large amount of computation. The membrane potential offers a useful clue for reducing this work. A neuron whose voltage lies close to its firing threshold is poised to change its spiking decision: a small change in its inputs or weights could move it across the threshold. A neuron whose voltage lies far below threshold is much less sensitive at that moment. Sparse surrogate-gradient computation uses this difference to concentrate training on the neurons near threshold.

Consider a neuron with membrane potential $V_i^l(t)$, where $i$ identifies the neuron, $l$ identifies its layer, and $t$ identifies the time step. The neuron emits a spike when its membrane potential reaches the firing threshold $V_{\mathrm{th}}$. Its spike can be written as
$$
S_i^l(t)=f\!\left(V_i^l(t)\right)
$$
Where $S_i^l(t)$ is either $0$ or $1$, and $f$ is the threshold function. A value of $1$ means that the neuron spikes at time $t$; a value of $0$ means that it remains silent.

Training needs the derivative of the spike $S_i^l(t)$ with respect to the membrane potential $V_i^l(t)$. The hard threshold creates an abrupt jump from $0$ to $1$, so a smooth surrogate derivative is used in its place. One example is the SuperSpike surrogate gradient
$$
G(V)=\frac{1}{\left(\beta\left|V-V_{\mathrm{th}}\right|+1\right)^2}
$$
Here, $V$ is the membrane potential being evaluated, $V_{\mathrm{th}}$ is the firing threshold, $\left|V-V_{\mathrm{th}}\right|$ is the distance from that threshold, and $\beta$ controls how quickly the surrogate gradient decreases as the distance grows. The formula computes a large gradient near the threshold and progressively smaller gradients farther away.

For example, when $V=V_{\mathrm{th}}$, the distance term is zero, so $g(V)=1$. If the voltage moves away from the threshold, the denominator grows and the gradient shrinks. With $\beta=10$ and $\left|V-V_{\mathrm{th}}\right|=0.1$, the gradient is
$$
G(V)=\frac{1}{(10\cdot 0.1+1)^2}
=\frac{1}{4}
$$
At a distance of $0.5$, the same calculation gives $1/36$. The rapidly decreasing values express a practical idea: the spiking decision is most sensitive to voltage changes near its boundary.

Sparse surrogate-gradient computation turns that gradual decline into an explicit selection rule. Choose a threshold-proximity width $B_{\mathrm{th}}>0$. A neuron is treated as active for gradient computation when its membrane potential lies within the selected region around the firing threshold:
$$
A_i^l(t)=
\begin{cases}
1, & \left|V_i^l(t)-V_{\mathrm{th}}\right|\leq B_{\mathrm{th}},\\
0, & \left|V_i^l(t)-V_{\mathrm{th}}\right|>B_{\mathrm{th}}.
\end{cases}
$$
The quantity $A_i^l(t)$ is the active-neuron mask. It records whether neuron $i$ in layer $l$ participates in surrogate-gradient computation at time $t$. The parameter $B_{\mathrm{th}}$ sets the permitted distance from the firing threshold. A mask value of $1$ selects the neuron, while a mask value of $0$ removes its local spike derivative from the calculation.

The masked surrogate-gradient rule is then
$$
\frac{\partial S_i^l(t)}{\partial V_i^l(t)}
\approx
A_i^l(t)\,g\!\left(V_i^l(t)\right)
$$
Or, equivalently,
$$
\frac{\partial S_i^l(t)}{\partial V_i^l(t)}
\approx
\begin{cases}
G\!\left(V_i^l(t)\right),
& \left|V_i^l(t)-V_{\mathrm{th}}\right|\leq B_{\mathrm{th}},\\
0,
& \left|V_i^l(t)-V_{\mathrm{th}}\right|>B_{\mathrm{th}}.
\end{cases}
$$
This formula computes the approximate spike derivative for each neuron. A near-threshold neuron receives its usual surrogate gradient. An inactive neuron receives a local gradient of zero and can be omitted from the corresponding sparse gradient computation.

Suppose $V_{\mathrm{th}}=1.0$ and $B_{\mathrm{th}}=0.1$. Three neurons have membrane potentials $0.95$, $0.72$, and $1.04$. Their distances from threshold are $0.05$, $0.28$, and $0.04$, so their mask values are $1$, $0$, and $1$. Training evaluates the surrogate derivative for the neurons at $0.95$ and $1.04$. The neuron at $0.72$ contributes an inactive-neuron gradient of zero at that time step. If its membrane potential later rises into the selected window, its mask changes to $1$ and it rejoins the gradient calculation.

**Question.** Does an active-neuron mask select only neurons that emitted spikes?

**Answer.** The mask selects neurons by membrane-potential proximity to the firing threshold. A neuron can remain just below threshold, emit no spike, and still be active for gradient computation because a small voltage change could alter its decision. Likewise, activity must be evaluated at each time step because membrane potentials evolve over time. Training sparsity therefore depends on the number of near-threshold states, rather than simply on the number of emitted spikes.

The width $B_{\mathrm{th}}$ controls how selective the computation is. A narrow window activates fewer neurons and produces a sparser gradient calculation. A wider window includes more neurons and retains surrogate-gradient contributions over a larger voltage range. The window therefore expresses an approximation choice: it decides how far from threshold a neuron may be before its local spike sensitivity is treated as negligible.

**Question.** Why keep the smooth surrogate $g(V)$ after introducing a binary mask?

**Answer.** The mask answers whether a neuron participates, while $g(V)$ determines the strength of its participation. Two neurons can both lie inside the active window yet sit at different distances from the threshold. The neuron closer to $V_{\mathrm{th}}$ receives the larger surrogate derivative, preserving the graded sensitivity encoded by $g(V)$.

The complete mental model is a voltage-sensitive gate around the firing threshold. Each neuron's membrane potential moves through time, the mask opens when that potential enters the near-threshold region, and the surrogate function assigns a gradient according to the remaining distance from threshold. Closed gates produce inactive-neuron gradients of zero; open gates define the sparse subset used for learning. Sparse surrogate-gradient computation therefore follows the states most capable of changing their spiking decisions while avoiding repeated derivative work for states far from that decision boundary.
