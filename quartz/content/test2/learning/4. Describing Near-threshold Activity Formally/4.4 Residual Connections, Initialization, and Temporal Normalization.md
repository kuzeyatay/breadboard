## Residual Connections, Initialization, and Temporal Normalization

Surrogate gradients make spike-based learning possible by providing a usable training signal near the firing threshold. In a deep spiking neural network, however, that signal must travel through many layers and many simulation steps while membrane potentials continually integrate inputs, leak, cross thresholds, and reset. A neuron whose membrane potential stays far below threshold rarely enters the region where the surrogate gradient is useful. A neuron driven far above threshold may spike too readily and provide little sensitivity to small input changes. Deep SNN training therefore depends on keeping two related quantities well behaved: the gradients carrying learning information and the activity determining how often neurons approach or cross threshold.

Imagine stacking dozens of spiking layers. Each layer transforms incoming spike trains into weighted input, updates its membrane potentials over time, and emits new spikes. During learning, the forward pass carries activity toward the output, while the backward pass carries gradient information toward earlier layers. Repeated transformations can shrink a gradient until early layers receive almost no useful update, a condition called a **vanishing gradient**. They can also amplify it until updates become unstable, producing an **exploding gradient**. SNNs add a further difficulty because their internal state evolves through time: the learning signal passes through both the depth of the network and the sequence of membrane-potential updates.

A **residual connection** gives information a shorter route through this deep computation. Instead of asking a block of layers to construct its entire output from scratch, the block learns a change to an input that is also carried along a direct path. If the input activity is called $x$ and the transformation performed by the spiking block is called $F(x)$, the residual form can be written as
$$
Y = x + F(x)
$$
Here, $x$ is the activity entering the block, $F(x)$ is the modification learned by the block, and $y$ is the combined output. The equation computes a new representation by preserving the incoming representation and adding a learned correction.

Suppose a useful spike pattern already reaches a deep block. A purely sequential block must reproduce that pattern through every internal transformation. A residual block can carry the pattern through $x$ while using $F(x)$ to add only the changes needed for the task. When no change is useful, the block can approach the identity function by making $F(x)$ small, so that $y$ approaches $x$. This identity route also gives gradients a more direct path during learning. Element-wise residual designs adapted to spiking activity preserve this property and support the training of SNNs with more than one hundred layers by mitigating vanishing and exploding gradients.

Residual connections primarily organize how signals move through depth. They do not determine whether the neurons begin training in a useful activity regime. That responsibility begins with **fluctuation-driven initialization**, a method for choosing initial weight scales so that membrane potentials exhibit a targeted amount of variation.

At initialization, random weights turn incoming spikes into fluctuating membrane potentials. Very small weights produce weak fluctuations, leaving many potentials far below the firing threshold. Very large weights produce excessive fluctuations and can drive activity too strongly. A useful initial state places a meaningful share of membrane-potential values near the threshold, where small changes in synaptic input can affect whether a spike occurs and where the surrogate gradient can provide an informative learning signal.

The relevant statistic is the **variance** of the membrane potential. Variance measures how widely potential values spread around their mean. If membrane potentials are $V_1,V_2,\ldots,V_N$ with mean $\bar V$, their variance is
$$
\operatorname{Var}(V)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(V_i-\bar V)^2
$$
In this expression, $V_i$ is one observed membrane potential, $\bar V$ is the average of the observed potentials, $N$ is the number of observations, and $\operatorname{Var}(V)$ is their average squared distance from the mean. The formula lets us quantify the scale of neuronal fluctuations rather than describing activity only as "high" or "low."

For example, imagine a threshold of $1$ and membrane potentials clustered tightly around $0.1$. Their variance is small, and ordinary input fluctuations seldom bring a neuron close to firing. A wider distribution extending toward $1$ places more neurons near threshold. Fluctuation-driven initialization selects the initial weight scale to target such a membrane-potential variance. This extends variance-based initialization ideas used in conventional deep networks to the state variable that directly governs spiking. Targeting membrane-potential variance has been reported to improve initialization for deep SNNs beyond seven layers compared with applying He initialization directly.

**Question.** Why does fluctuation-driven initialization target membrane potentials rather than spike counts alone?

**Answer.** Spike counts record completed threshold crossings, while membrane potentials reveal how close silent neurons came to firing. Two layers can emit similarly few spikes even though one contains many neurons just below threshold and the other contains neurons far from threshold. The first layer is more responsive to small input or weight changes. Membrane-potential variance therefore describes the near-threshold conditions that shape both future activity and useful surrogate-gradient signals.

Initialization establishes an appropriate starting regime, but learning continually changes the weights. Activity statistics can then drift across layers and across simulation steps. **Temporal normalization** controls this drift by extending normalization to the time dimension of an SNN.

Traditional batch normalization standardizes pre-activations using statistics collected from a batch. In an SNN, the distribution at one timestep may differ from the distribution at another because membrane state accumulates, leaks, spikes, and resets. Treating every timestep as though it had the same activity statistics can overlook this temporal structure. Batch normalization through time addresses the additional dimension by allowing normalization to track activity over the simulation sequence. Threshold-dependent batch normalization further links normalized pre-activation variance to the firing threshold $V_{\mathrm{th}}$, the membrane-potential level at which a neuron emits a spike.

This threshold dependence gives normalization a natural scale. A pre-activation variance has different functional meaning when compared with a low threshold than when compared with a high one. Relating the normalized variance to $V_{\mathrm{th}}$ helps maintain activity in a regime compatible with threshold crossing across deep layers. In practical terms, temporal normalization performs **activity-statistic control**: it limits harmful changes in the distributions driving neuronal states. Stable activity distributions then support **gradient-statistic control**, because the backward signal depends on where membrane potentials lie relative to the threshold-sensitive surrogate region.

**Question.** Do residual connections, fluctuation-driven initialization, and temporal normalization solve the same training problem?

**Answer.** They act at different points in the training process. Residual connections provide direct routes for forward activity and backward gradients through depth. Fluctuation-driven initialization chooses the starting weight scale by targeting membrane-potential variation. Temporal normalization regulates evolving activity statistics across layers and timesteps as training proceeds. Together they create a coherent training regime: signals have viable paths, neurons begin near a responsive operating range, and that range remains controlled as the network changes.

A useful mental model is a deep, time-evolving chain of threshold devices. Residual connections keep routes through the chain open, fluctuation-driven initialization sets the initial motion of each device near a responsive range, and temporal normalization prevents that motion from drifting unpredictably as time and learning unfold. Deep SNN training becomes manageable when gradient flow and near-threshold activity are controlled as complementary parts of the same dynamical system.
