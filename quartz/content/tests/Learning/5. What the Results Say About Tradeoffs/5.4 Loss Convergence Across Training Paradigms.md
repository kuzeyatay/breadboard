### Loss Convergence Across Training Paradigms

Imagine training three spiking systems to recognize patterns, and after each pass through the training data you ask the same simple question: "How wrong is the system now?" That wrongness is measured by **training loss**. A high loss means the model's current behavior differs strongly from the desired answers. A lower loss means the model's internal parameters have moved toward producing better outputs. Watching loss across repeated passes through the data, called **epochs**, gives a practical view of how easily each training paradigm improves.

An **epoch** is one full round of training over the available examples. If a model's loss drops sharply in early epochs, the model is finding useful parameter changes quickly. If the loss drops slowly, the model is learning, but each round gives a smaller improvement. If the loss flattens while still high, the model has reached a plateau: more training in the same style gives limited additional error reduction. The shape of the loss curve therefore tells a story about optimization behavior, not just final performance.

A spiking neural network adds a special difficulty to this story because its signals are discrete spike events. In a conventional artificial neural network, values change smoothly, so gradient-based learning can directly estimate how a small parameter change affects the loss. In a spiking network, a neuron often stays silent until its internal state crosses a threshold, then emits a spike. That threshold event makes learning more delicate because a tiny parameter change may leave the spike pattern unchanged, or may shift a spike in time. Different training paradigms handle this difficulty in different ways, and their loss curves reveal how well each one turns repeated epochs into improvement.

A **surrogate gradient SNN** handles the threshold difficulty by using a smooth training approximation while preserving spiking behavior during computation. The word **surrogate** means "standing in for something hard to use directly." During learning, the sharp spike threshold is treated with an approximate gradient so the training algorithm can still decide how to adjust weights. This gives the optimizer a usable direction: if a weight change would tend to reduce the loss, the algorithm can reinforce that direction across epochs.

That mechanism appears in the loss trajectory. The surrogate gradient SNN reduces training loss from about **0.90** to **0.44** over 20 epochs. The drop is both steep and consistent, which means the model keeps finding useful updates rather than improving only at the beginning. By the 20th epoch, the loss has stabilized at a relatively low value among the spiking variants. A beginner-friendly way to picture this is a hiker descending a hill with a reliable map: each step may be small, but the map keeps pointing downhill, so progress continues.

A **converted SNN** begins from a trained artificial neural network and translates it into a spiking form. The advantage is that the system inherits much of the structure learned by the original ANN. The challenge is that continuous activations must be represented through spikes and time windows. A value that was once a smooth number now has to be expressed through event activity, often requiring more spikes or longer simulation time to approximate the original behavior.

Its training-loss behavior sits between the other two spiking approaches. The converted SNN reaches about **0.60** loss by epoch 20. That is meaningful improvement, but the decline is slower than the surrogate gradient case. The curve suggests that conversion gives the model a useful starting structure, yet the spiking representation creates friction during optimization. The system can improve, but it does not reduce error as rapidly as the model trained with surrogate gradients. An analogy is translating a well-written paragraph into a language with different grammar: the meaning carries over, but some precision has to be recovered through adjustment.

An **STDP-based SNN** uses **Spike-Timing Dependent Plasticity**, a learning rule based on the relative timing of spikes. If one neuron tends to fire just before another, the connection between them can strengthen; if the timing relationship differs, the connection can weaken. This rule is attractive because it is local and biologically inspired. Each connection can update using nearby spike timing rather than relying on a full supervised error signal flowing backward through the network.

That local timing rule produces a different loss pattern. The STDP-based SNN converges the slowest and stabilizes around **0.75** loss. The plateau at a higher value shows that timing-based unsupervised adaptation can organize useful spike patterns, but it has less direct pressure toward minimizing classification error. The model becomes efficient and adaptive, yet its loss curve shows shallower error reduction over the same 20 epochs. If surrogate-gradient learning resembles following a map toward a target, STDP resembles forming paths by repeated foot traffic: structure emerges from experience, but the path may lead toward useful regularities rather than the exact labeled destination.

**Question.** If STDP-based learning is energy-efficient, why does its training loss stay higher?

**Answer.** STDP changes connections using spike timing, so it can adapt with low activity and local updates. Training loss for classification measures how closely the model's outputs match desired labels. A local timing rule can discover patterns without using a strong global error signal, so it often saves energy while reducing classification error more slowly. The higher plateau around 0.75 reflects that tradeoff.

The comparison across epochs separates three meanings of "good training." Fast convergence means the model reduces loss quickly. Low final loss means the model reaches a better fit to the training objective. Stable optimization means the curve continues improving without erratic behavior or early stagnation. The surrogate gradient SNN performs strongly on all three within the 20-epoch window: it falls from 0.90 to 0.44, stabilizes by the end, and supports competitive accuracy with moderate spike counts and sub-15 ms latency. Converted SNNs show moderate convergence, landing near 0.60, which fits their role as a bridge from ANN performance into spiking computation. STDP-based SNNs converge slowly and settle near 0.75, matching their strength in low-energy unsupervised adaptation rather than precise supervised classification.

The epoch dynamics also help explain why a single metric gives an incomplete picture. A model with very low energy use may still need more epochs or may settle at a higher loss. A model with faster convergence may spend more computation during training but become more suitable for real-time learning tasks. A converted model may preserve much of an ANN's capability but pay for that translation through spike count and simulation-window demands. Training loss acts as the first lens because it shows whether the learning process itself is moving efficiently before accuracy, latency, and energy are considered together.

**Question.** Does the lowest training loss automatically mean the best spiking system?

**Answer.** The lowest training loss means the model best minimized the training objective among the compared systems. For the 20-epoch results, that points to the surrogate gradient SNN. Choosing a spiking system also depends on the deployment goal. Real-time classification favors fast convergence and low latency. Ultra-low-energy adaptation may favor STDP even with higher loss. ANN-to-SNN conversion may fit situations where an existing ANN must be moved into a spiking format.

The mental model is a race down three different learning paths. Surrogate gradients give spiking networks a usable downhill signal, so loss falls quickly and settles low. Conversion starts with useful ANN-shaped knowledge, then works through the cost of expressing smooth activations as spikes, so loss falls at a moderate pace. STDP learns from spike timing itself, producing efficient local adaptation while reaching a higher loss plateau. Reading the loss curves across epochs turns the training paradigms into visible behaviors: one optimizes directly and quickly, one translates and adjusts, and one adapts through timing with an energy-centered profile.
