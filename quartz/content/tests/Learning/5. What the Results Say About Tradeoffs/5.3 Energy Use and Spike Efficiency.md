### Energy Use and Spike Efficiency

Imagine a small camera on a battery-powered drone watching a hallway. Most of the time, the scene barely changes: the wall stays still, the floor stays still, and only an occasional person moves through the frame. A conventional neural network still processes dense layers of numbers every time it makes a decision. Even quiet pixels become part of a large stream of arithmetic. A spiking neural network uses a different assumption: silence carries information. When there is little change, few neurons need to fire. When something meaningful happens, neurons send brief events called **spikes**, and the timing and number of those spikes become part of the computation.

Energy use becomes easier to understand once computation is tied to activity. A dense artificial neural network, such as a CNN, uses continuous-valued operations. Each layer passes many numerical activations forward, and each inference requires many multiply-and-accumulate operations. In the comparison here, the ANN CNN uses about **200 millijoules per inference**. One inference means one complete pass from input to output, such as classifying a gesture or recognizing a sound pattern. A millijoule is a unit of energy; lower millijoules per inference means the model can make more decisions from the same battery.

A spiking model can reduce this cost because neurons communicate through sparse events. A useful way to connect spikes to energy is to think in terms of event-driven work:

\[
E_{\text{inference}} \approx N_{\text{spikes}} \times E_{\text{spike}} + E_{\text{overhead}}
\]

Here, \(E_{\text{inference}}\) is the total energy used to make one prediction, \(N_{\text{spikes}}\) is the number of spikes generated during that prediction, \(E_{\text{spike}}\) is the average energy cost of handling one spike on the hardware, and \(E_{\text{overhead}}\) is the remaining cost of running the system, such as memory access and control logic. This formula lets you estimate why fewer spikes usually mean lower energy: if the hardware cost per spike stays roughly similar, cutting the spike count cuts a large part of the event-processing workload.

A converted SNN shows both the promise and the cost of this approach. ANN-to-SNN conversion starts with a trained conventional network and replaces continuous activations with spike rates. A high activation becomes many spikes over a simulation window; a low activation becomes fewer spikes. This can reduce energy sharply because the model now uses spike-based communication, but the network may need many spikes to approximate the original continuous values accurately. In the comparison, the converted SNN drops energy from about **200 mJ** to about **20 mJ per inference**, which is a tenfold reduction. Its spike count remains high at roughly **20,000 spikes per inference**, because rate coding often needs repeated spikes to represent activation strength.

A simple analogy helps: imagine replacing a spoken sentence with hand claps. If every word must be represented by a number of claps, a strong or detailed message may require many claps. The message becomes event-based, but it can still be busy. Converted SNNs work in a similar way: they gain energy efficiency from spikes, while their rate-coded representation can keep spike counts relatively large.

Surrogate gradient SNNs reduce this activity by learning spiking behavior directly. The difficulty is that a spike is a threshold event: a neuron fires when its internal voltage crosses a firing threshold. Ordinary gradient descent expects smooth changes, while a spike appears as an abrupt jump. Surrogate gradient training solves this by using a smooth approximation during learning, allowing the network to adjust its weights toward useful spike timing and useful spike placement. The result is a model that can keep many of the accuracy benefits of gradient-based learning while producing fewer unnecessary spikes.

In the comparison, the surrogate gradient SNN uses about **15 mJ per inference** and about **12,000 spikes per inference**. Its energy is lower than the converted SNN, and its spike count is also lower. This means the model is learning a more economical spiking pattern rather than simply imitating dense ANN activations through repeated firing. It also keeps strong task performance, with accuracy close to ANN levels, often within about one to two percentage points in the broader comparison. For applications that need a balanced design, such as fast recognition on edge devices, this balance is attractive: the model responds quickly, uses much less energy than the ANN, and avoids the heaviest spike traffic of conversion-based approaches.

**Question.** If spikes cost energy, why use spikes at all instead of continuous values?

**Answer.** Spikes allow the system to compute mainly when events occur. Continuous-valued networks usually move dense numerical activity through every layer during every inference. A spiking network can stay mostly quiet when the input is quiet, and quiet neurons consume far less event-processing energy. The advantage comes from sparsity: useful information is carried by a small number of timed events instead of a constant stream of dense values.

STDP-based SNNs push this sparse idea the furthest. STDP stands for **Spike-Timing Dependent Plasticity**. It is a learning rule based on the relative timing of spikes. If one neuron tends to fire shortly before another, the connection between them can strengthen; if the timing relationship is weaker or reversed, the connection can weaken. The model gradually shapes its connections around repeated temporal patterns. This kind of learning fits naturally with event-based data because timing is part of the signal.

The energy result is striking: the STDP-based SNN reaches about **5 mJ per inference** with about **4,000 spikes per inference**. Compared with the ANN CNN at about 200 mJ, that is a reduction of roughly **97.5%**:

\[
\frac{200 - 5}{200} \times 100\% = 97.5\%
\]

In this calculation, \(200\) is the ANN energy in millijoules per inference, \(5\) is the STDP SNN energy in millijoules per inference, and the result gives the percentage reduction in energy. The spike count tells the same story from the activity side. Moving from about 20,000 spikes in the converted SNN to about 4,000 spikes in the STDP-based SNN means the STDP model uses only one fifth as many spikes:

\[
\frac{4{,}000}{20{,}000} = 0.2
\]

Here, \(4{,}000\) is the STDP spike count, \(20{,}000\) is the converted SNN spike count, and \(0.2\) means 20% as many spikes. Fewer spikes mean fewer event transmissions and fewer neuron updates, which explains why STDP sits in the lowest-power region of the comparison.

The tradeoff is that energy efficiency is only one axis of performance. STDP-based models can have slightly reduced accuracy, around **95.5%** in the integrated comparison, and can converge more slowly because their learning depends on gradual timing-based adaptation. Surrogate gradient SNNs use more energy, around **15 mJ**, but can reach higher accuracy around **97.8%** while maintaining low latency near **10 ms**. Converted SNNs can preserve competitive performance from trained ANNs, but their longer simulation windows and higher spike counts make them less efficient than directly trained spiking models. The ANN CNN remains strong in raw accuracy, while its dense continuous computation makes it the most energy-hungry option.

**Question.** Does the lowest spike count always mean the best model?

**Answer.** The lowest spike count means the model is very efficient in event activity, which usually helps energy use. Model choice also depends on accuracy, latency, convergence, and the learning setting. An STDP-based SNN is well suited to ultra-low-power or unsupervised edge scenarios. A surrogate gradient SNN may be better when the task needs high accuracy and fast responses while still saving large amounts of energy compared with an ANN.

A useful mental model is to picture each model as spending an energy budget to send evidence through a network. The ANN spends heavily because it pushes dense values everywhere. The converted SNN spends much less, but it still sends many spikes to mimic continuous activations. The surrogate gradient SNN learns to spend its spikes more selectively, keeping accuracy high while reducing activity. The STDP-based SNN spends the least by letting timing-based local learning produce sparse firing patterns. Energy use and spike efficiency therefore describe the same underlying behavior from two angles: millijoules measure the cost paid by the hardware, and spike count measures how much event activity the network asked the hardware to perform.
