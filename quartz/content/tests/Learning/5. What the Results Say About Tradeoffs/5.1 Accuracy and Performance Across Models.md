### Accuracy and Performance Across Models

Imagine choosing a neural network for a small camera device that must recognize simple images while running on a tiny battery. If the task is image classification, accuracy is the most obvious number to ask for: how often does the model name the image correctly? But the device also has to finish quickly, avoid draining power, and avoid producing unnecessary internal activity. A model that wins by a tiny accuracy margin may be a poor fit if each decision costs far more energy. A spiking neural network makes this comparison more interesting because it changes the basic unit of computation. Instead of pushing continuous numerical activations through every layer at every step, it sends brief events called spikes. A spike is a compact signal that says, in effect, "something worth transmitting happened now."

On image benchmarks such as MNIST and CIFAR-10, the comparison usually begins with an artificial neural network baseline, often a convolutional neural network for vision. MNIST is the simpler setting: handwritten digits are centered, grayscale, and relatively clean. CIFAR-10 is harder: the images are small color photographs spread across ten object classes, with more variation in background, texture, and shape. These datasets give two useful checkpoints. MNIST tests whether a method can learn a clean visual mapping without wasting computation. CIFAR-10 tests whether the same training approach remains useful when the visual patterns are less tidy.

The ANN baseline gives the reference point for raw accuracy. A conventional convolutional ANN computes with dense continuous values, so every inference involves many multiply-and-accumulate operations. That style is powerful for classification, and it explains why ANNs tend to dominate the top accuracy comparison. In the reported results, the strongest spiking methods come close rather than surpassing the ANN baseline: surrogate-gradient SNNs approach ANN accuracy within roughly 1-2%, with representative accuracy around 97.8% in the integrated comparison. That small gap matters differently depending on the setting. For a cloud model where energy is secondary and the goal is squeezing out the highest possible score, the ANN may still be favored. For an edge device that must make many decisions under strict power limits, a 1-2% accuracy loss can be an acceptable price for a large reduction in energy.

A converted SNN starts from a trained ANN and translates its activity into spikes. The intuition is straightforward: if an ANN unit has a large activation, the corresponding spiking unit can fire more often over a simulation window. This is called rate coding because information is carried by the rate or count of spikes. The advantage is that the converted model inherits much of the ANN's learned decision structure, so its accuracy can remain competitive on datasets such as MNIST and CIFAR-10. The cost is that rate coding often needs many spikes and a longer observation window to represent a value reliably. If a neuron must fire many times before its signal is clear, the model spends time and energy creating a dense stream of events.

A surrogate-gradient SNN trains the spiking model more directly. The obstacle is that a spike is a sudden event: below threshold the neuron stays silent, and above threshold it fires. That sharp threshold makes ordinary gradient learning difficult because the derivative is not useful at the jump. A surrogate gradient solves this by using a smooth approximation during training while preserving spiking behavior during operation. The practical result is a spiking model that can learn efficient spike patterns rather than merely copying dense ANN activations into spike rates. This is why surrogate-gradient SNNs sit near the strongest accuracy results while also improving latency and energy. Reported latency can be as low as about 10 milliseconds, with energy around 15 millijoules per inference and a spike count near 12,000.

The energy comparison becomes clear when the models are placed side by side.

The pattern in the graph is the central tradeoff. The ANN sits at the high-energy end, around 200 millijoules per inference, because dense continuous computation keeps many units active. The converted SNN drops energy sharply to about 20 millijoules, but it still produces a large number of spikes, roughly 20,000 per inference, because rate-coded values need repeated events. The surrogate-gradient SNN improves the balance: about 15 millijoules and roughly 12,000 spikes, while staying close to ANN-level accuracy. The STDP-based SNN reaches the sparsest and lowest-energy region, around 5 millijoules and roughly 4,000 spikes. The shape of this comparison tells a simple story: as training becomes more spike-native and activity becomes sparser, energy falls dramatically.

STDP, or spike-timing dependent plasticity, follows a more local learning rule. A connection strengthens or weakens depending on the relative timing of spikes before and after it. If a presynaptic neuron repeatedly fires just before a postsynaptic neuron, the connection can become stronger because that timing suggests influence. This rule is appealing for ultra-low-power or unsupervised settings because it does not require the same kind of global error-driven optimization as backpropagation. Its performance profile reflects that difference. STDP-based SNNs can reach very low energy and spike counts, but they usually give up some accuracy, with representative accuracy around 95.5%, and they converge more slowly.

The phrase "performance tradeoff" means that each metric answers a different practical question. Accuracy asks, "How often is the answer correct?" Latency asks, "How long does one decision take?" Energy asks, "How much power does each decision consume?" Spike count asks, "How much event activity did the network need?" Convergence asks, "How quickly did training settle into useful behavior?" A useful model comparison keeps these questions together because improving one number can push another in the wrong direction. A converted SNN may keep accuracy competitive but spend extra spikes to do it. An STDP model may conserve energy beautifully but lose some classification strength. A surrogate-gradient model often occupies the middle ground, preserving much of the accuracy while gaining large efficiency improvements.

**Question.** If surrogate-gradient SNNs are close to ANN accuracy, why not always use them instead of ANNs?

**Answer.** A surrogate-gradient SNN is attractive when energy, latency, or event-based hardware compatibility matters, but an ANN can still be simpler to train, easier to deploy with mature software tools, and slightly stronger in raw accuracy. The best choice depends on the deployment goal. For a battery-powered sensor, the SNN's efficiency can outweigh a small accuracy gap. For a server-side benchmark where the highest classification score is the priority, the ANN baseline may remain preferable.

For MNIST, these tradeoffs are easier to see because the task is clean enough that several models can perform well. Once accuracy is already high, energy and spike count become more decisive. A model that classifies digits nearly as well while using far fewer millijoules has a strong practical advantage. For CIFAR-10, the visual complexity makes the accuracy side of the tradeoff more demanding. Surrogate-gradient training becomes especially valuable because it can tune spiking behavior directly rather than relying only on a converted rate-coded version of an ANN. Converted SNNs can remain competitive, but their need for longer simulation windows and higher spike counts becomes more costly on harder recognition tasks.

A simple way to read the comparative results is to imagine paying for each inference with three currencies: correctness, time, and energy. The ANN pays heavily in energy to buy strong correctness. The converted SNN keeps much of the correctness but still pays with many spikes. The surrogate-gradient SNN spends fewer spikes and less energy while staying close to the accuracy target. The STDP-based SNN spends the least energy, making it useful when power is the tightest constraint, even though its accuracy and convergence behavior are less favorable.

**Question.** Does a lower spike count automatically mean a better spiking neural network?

**Answer.** A lower spike count usually helps energy efficiency because fewer events mean less activity to process, but spike count has to be judged alongside accuracy and latency. A model that fires very little but misses many classifications is sparse in a poor way. A strong SNN uses spikes selectively: it stays quiet when activity adds little value and fires when timing or frequency carries useful information for the decision.

The mental model is a set of knobs rather than a single leaderboard. ANN baselines show the accuracy target. Converted SNNs show how much of that target can be preserved by translating dense activity into spike rates. Surrogate-gradient SNNs show the benefit of training the spiking system to use its own event-based language efficiently. STDP-based SNNs show how far energy and spike activity can be reduced when local timing rules and sparse firing are prioritized. Across MNIST, CIFAR-10, and the broader comparative results, the strongest interpretation is qualitative and application-driven: raw accuracy favors ANNs, balanced low-latency efficiency favors surrogate-gradient SNNs, and the lowest-power operating point favors STDP-style spiking systems.
