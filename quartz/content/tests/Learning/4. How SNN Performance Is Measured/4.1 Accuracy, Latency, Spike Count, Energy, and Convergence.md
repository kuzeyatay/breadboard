Imagine an SNN reading a handwritten digit from a sensor. The input arrives as spikes over a short window of time, the neurons respond with their own spikes, and eventually the network makes a decision such as "this digit is a 7." To judge whether that system is useful, one number is never enough. A model that gets many answers right may take too long. A model that responds quickly may fire so many spikes that it wastes energy. A model that saves energy may learn slowly or settle at a weaker solution. Performance measurement exists to make those tradeoffs visible before choosing a model for a real device.

Accuracy measures how often the network's final decisions are correct. In a classification task, each input has a true label, and the network predicts one label. Accuracy asks a simple counting question: out of all predictions made, what fraction matched the true labels?

![Accuracy as correct predictions divided by total predictions](/tests/assets/2510-27379v1-page-006.png)

*Accuracy as correct predictions divided by total predictions* *(p. 6)*

The formula can be read directly: accuracy equals the number of correct predictions divided by the total number of predictions. If an SNN classifies 1,000 digit images and gets 950 of them right, its accuracy is \(950 / 1000 = 0.95\), or 95%. The numerator is the count of successful decisions; the denominator is the full set of attempts. This lets different models be compared on the same task even if their internal spike patterns look very different.

Accuracy becomes especially meaningful when it is read beside the kind of training method used. A surrogate-gradient SNN can approach ANN-level accuracy, often within a small gap, because it uses gradient-based learning to shape spike patterns toward the desired labels. A converted SNN can also reach competitive accuracy by starting from a trained ANN and translating its activations into spike rates, although that translation often requires longer simulation windows and more spikes. An STDP-based SNN learns from local timing relationships between spikes, so it can be extremely efficient, while its classification accuracy may be lower for tasks that demand precise supervised labels.

Latency measures how long the network takes to turn an input stimulus into a decision. A spike-based system unfolds over time, so the decision is tied to when enough evidence has accumulated. In a real-time gesture recognizer, a delay of a few milliseconds can feel immediate, while a long simulation window may be unsuitable for fast interaction.

![Latency as decision time minus input stimulus time](/tests/assets/2510-27379v1-page-006.png)

*Latency as decision time minus input stimulus time* *(p. 6)*

The formula says latency equals decision time minus input stimulus time. The input stimulus time is when the example begins entering the network. The decision time is when the output is ready. If a gesture segment starts at 0 ms and the model decides at 10 ms, the latency is 10 ms. This value tells you how responsive the model is during inference. Surrogate-gradient SNNs are attractive here because they can reach low inference latency, including sub-15 ms behavior, while maintaining competitive accuracy. Converted SNNs may need longer windows because rate coding often depends on counting spikes over time: a bright pixel or strong activation is represented by more frequent spikes, and reliable counting usually needs enough time to observe those spikes.

Spike count measures how much event activity the network produces during one inference. In an SNN, spikes are the main computational events. Each spike represents a neuron firing at a time step, and a low spike count usually means the system is using sparse communication. Sparse activity is valuable because neuromorphic hardware can spend energy mainly when events occur.

![Total spikes summed over neurons and timesteps](/tests/assets/2510-27379v1-page-006.png)

*Total spikes summed over neurons and timesteps* *(p. 6)*

The displayed formula sums spikes over neurons and timesteps. Each neuron can either fire or stay silent at each moment in the simulation window, and the total spike count adds up all those firing events. If 100 neurons are observed over 50 time steps, the count considers every neuron-time pair. A spike at neuron 12 during time step 8 contributes one event; silence contributes none. This gives a concrete measure of how much event traffic the model creates while solving one input.

Spike count explains why two models with similar accuracy can behave very differently on hardware. A converted SNN may produce around 20,000 spikes per inference because rate-coded propagation uses spike frequency to approximate continuous ANN activations. A surrogate-gradient SNN may reduce that to around 12,000 spikes by learning spiking patterns more directly. An STDP-based SNN may be much sparser, around 4,000 spikes per inference, because local timing-based adaptation can produce compact event activity. Those counts are meaningful because each spike is a small communication and computation cost.

Energy per inference measures how much energy the model consumes to process one input and produce one decision. It is often reported in millijoules, abbreviated mJ. The phrase "per inference" means the cost is attached to one complete forward pass through the trained model: one image, one gesture window, or one audio command segment. Energy depends on the operations required, the number of spikes, and the hardware style. Dense ANNs can consume much more energy because they use continuous-valued operations across many units. Spike-based models can reduce energy because silence carries information: when neurons do not fire, event-driven hardware can avoid unnecessary work.

A useful comparison makes the tradeoff concrete. An ANN may consume about 200 mJ per inference. A converted SNN can reduce that to about 20 mJ, while still producing a relatively high spike count. A surrogate-gradient SNN may use about 15 mJ with fewer spikes. An STDP-based SNN may reach about 5 mJ per inference with the sparsest activity among the compared models. These numbers show why energy and spike count belong together: fewer spikes often mean less event processing, although the final energy also depends on the hardware and implementation.

**Question.** If an STDP-based SNN uses the least energy, should it always be chosen?

**Answer.** It should be chosen when low power and local adaptation are the main goals, such as energy-constrained or unsupervised settings. For tasks that need the highest classification accuracy or fast supervised optimization, a surrogate-gradient SNN may be a better fit because it balances accuracy, latency, spike count, and energy more evenly.

Convergence time measures how quickly training settles into a stable useful behavior. During training, the model's loss tracks how far its predictions are from the desired outcomes. A model that converges quickly reduces its loss in fewer epochs. An epoch is one full pass through the training data. If a model stabilizes by the 20th epoch, its learning curve has largely flattened by then, meaning additional passes may bring smaller improvements.

Convergence matters because training cost is part of practical model selection. A surrogate-gradient SNN can converge faster because gradient-based updates directly push the network toward lower loss, even though spikes themselves are discrete events. The surrogate gradient supplies a trainable approximation that makes optimization workable. A converted SNN inherits much of its behavior from a previously trained ANN, so its evaluation may depend more on how well the conversion preserves performance across a simulation window. An STDP-based SNN can converge more slowly and plateau at a higher loss because its updates are local: synapses change according to the timing relationship between presynaptic and postsynaptic spikes rather than a global supervised error signal.

**Question.** Why are latency and convergence time separate measurements if both involve time?

**Answer.** Latency is inference time: how long one trained model takes to answer one input. Convergence time is training behavior: how many epochs the model needs before learning stabilizes. A model can answer quickly after training while still having required many epochs to learn, or it can train efficiently while needing a longer simulation window during inference.

The five measurements fit together as one mental model. Accuracy asks whether the decision is right. Latency asks how quickly the decision appears. Spike count asks how much event activity the network generates to reach that decision. Energy per inference asks how costly one decision is to run. Convergence time asks how efficiently the model learns before deployment. Reading SNN results through all five dimensions turns each table or graph into evidence about a tradeoff: a high-accuracy model may spend more spikes, a low-energy model may accept lower accuracy, and a fast-converging model may be preferable when training time is part of the design constraint.
