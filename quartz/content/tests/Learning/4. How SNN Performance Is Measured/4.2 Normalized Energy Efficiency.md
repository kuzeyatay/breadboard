### Normalized Energy Efficiency

Imagine choosing a neural network for a small battery-powered camera that recognizes objects at the edge of a field. The camera may spend most of its day watching quiet scenes, and every inference costs a little energy. A highly accurate model sounds attractive, but if it drains the battery quickly, the device fails at its real job. A very low-energy model also sounds attractive, but if it misclassifies too often, the saved energy buys unreliable decisions. Normalized energy efficiency gives a way to look at these two needs together: how much correct performance a model delivers for each unit of energy it spends.

The basic measurement starts with two quantities that are easier to understand separately. Accuracy measures how often the model gives the correct answer. If a model classifies 1,000 images and gets 978 correct, its accuracy is 97.8%. Energy measures how much physical work the hardware must spend to make one prediction, often reported in millijoules per inference. A millijoule, abbreviated mJ, is a small unit of energy; one inference means one completed prediction. If a model uses 15 mJ per inference, each prediction costs 15 millijoules.

Those two numbers answer different questions. Accuracy asks, "How often is the answer right?" Energy asks, "How expensive is one answer?" Normalized energy efficiency combines them into a derived metric, meaning it is calculated from existing measurements rather than measured as a separate raw axis. The five main performance axes are accuracy, latency, energy consumption, spike count, and convergence behavior. Normalized energy efficiency sits beside them as an interpretation tool: it turns accuracy and energy into a single ratio so the efficiency of a correct prediction becomes easier to compare.

A natural way to build the ratio is to place the benefit on top and the cost underneath:

\[
\text{Energy efficiency} = \frac{\text{Accuracy}}{\text{Energy per inference}}
\]

Here, **accuracy** is the model's fraction or percentage of correct predictions, and **energy per inference** is the energy used to produce one prediction, usually in mJ. The formula lets you compute "accuracy per unit energy." If accuracy is written as a percentage, the result can be read as percentage points of accuracy per millijoule. If accuracy is written as a fraction between 0 and 1, the result can be read as correct-decision fraction per millijoule. Either version is acceptable as long as every model is compared using the same convention.

Consider a surrogate-gradient SNN with about 97.8% accuracy and 15 mJ per inference. Using percentage points, its energy efficiency is

\[
\frac{97.8}{15} \approx 6.52
\]

This means the model delivers about 6.52 percentage points of accuracy per millijoule. Now consider an STDP-based SNN with about 95.5% accuracy and 5 mJ per inference:

\[
\frac{95.5}{5} = 19.1
\]

This model delivers 19.1 percentage points of accuracy per millijoule. The STDP model has lower raw accuracy, yet it gives more accuracy for each millijoule spent because its energy cost is much smaller. That is the kind of tradeoff the ratio is designed to reveal.

The word "normalized" adds one more layer. Raw ratios can be useful, but they may be easier to interpret when scaled relative to a reference model. A common reference is the strongest or most familiar baseline, such as an ANN/CNN. If the baseline uses about 200 mJ per inference and a spiking model uses 20 mJ, 15 mJ, or 5 mJ, the energy savings are large enough that the ratio can change dramatically even when accuracy changes by only a few percentage points. Normalization turns the comparison into a relative scale, so a learner can ask, "How efficient is this model compared with the reference?" rather than only "What is its raw ratio?"

One simple normalized form is

\[
\text{Normalized energy efficiency} =
\frac{\left(\frac{\text{Accuracy}_{\text{model}}}{\text{Energy}_{\text{model}}}\right)}
{\left(\frac{\text{Accuracy}_{\text{reference}}}{\text{Energy}_{\text{reference}}}\right)}
\]

In this formula, \(\text{Accuracy}_{\text{model}}\) is the accuracy of the model being evaluated, \(\text{Energy}_{\text{model}}\) is its energy per inference, \(\text{Accuracy}_{\text{reference}}\) is the accuracy of the comparison baseline, and \(\text{Energy}_{\text{reference}}\) is the baseline's energy per inference. A value of 1 means the model has the same accuracy-per-energy ratio as the reference. A value greater than 1 means the model delivers more accuracy per unit energy than the reference. A value below 1 means the model delivers less accuracy per unit energy than the reference.

This derived metric is especially useful for spiking neural networks because their main advantage often comes from sparse event-based computation. A conventional ANN processes continuous-valued activations throughout the network. An SNN sends discrete spike events, and a quiet neuron can avoid unnecessary activity. Fewer spikes usually mean fewer operations, and fewer operations usually mean lower energy. That connection appears directly in the reported energy and spike-count pattern: the ANN/CNN uses around 200 mJ per inference, the converted SNN around 20 mJ with roughly 20,000 spikes, the surrogate-gradient SNN around 15 mJ with roughly 12,000 spikes, and the STDP-based SNN around 5 mJ with roughly 4,000 spikes. The downward movement in both energy and spike count shows why spike sparsity matters. When the network communicates through fewer events, the hardware has less work to perform.

A converted SNN illustrates why the ratio is needed. Conversion can preserve competitive accuracy by translating an ANN-style model into a spiking form, but rate-coded spike propagation can require many spikes across a longer simulation window. That gives the converted model much lower energy than the ANN/CNN, yet still higher spike activity than the more directly optimized spiking approaches. A learner looking only at accuracy might treat the converted model as nearly ideal. A learner looking at normalized energy efficiency sees the extra cost of producing that accuracy.

Surrogate-gradient training gives a different balance. It learns spiking patterns with gradient-based optimization, so it can approach ANN-like accuracy while using far less energy. With about 97.8% accuracy, roughly 10 ms latency, and around 15 mJ per inference, it fits applications that need accurate and quick decisions while still gaining large energy savings. Its normalized energy efficiency can be strong because the accuracy remains high and the energy is much lower than the ANN/CNN baseline.

STDP-based learning gives the clearest example of an efficiency-oriented choice. Spike-Timing Dependent Plasticity adjusts connections based on the timing relationship between spikes, and the resulting activity can be very sparse. Around 5 mJ per inference and roughly 4,000 spikes makes this model extremely efficient for energy-constrained settings. Its accuracy around 95.5% is lower than the surrogate-gradient model, but the energy denominator is so small that the accuracy-per-energy ratio becomes very large. For a device that must survive on limited power, that tradeoff can be exactly the desired behavior.

**Question.** If the STDP-based SNN has lower accuracy than the surrogate-gradient SNN, why can it have higher normalized energy efficiency?

**Answer.** Normalized energy efficiency compares accuracy with energy cost. The STDP-based SNN gives up some accuracy, around 95.5% instead of about 97.8%, but it uses far less energy, around 5 mJ instead of 15 mJ per inference. Because the energy value sits in the denominator, reducing energy by a factor of three can raise the ratio more than a small accuracy decrease lowers it.

A common beginner confusion is to treat normalized energy efficiency as the model's overall score. It is better understood as one lens on the tradeoff. A high value means the model produces a lot of accuracy for each millijoule, but deployment decisions can still depend on latency, convergence, and the minimum acceptable accuracy. A medical alert system may prefer a model with the highest reliable accuracy and low latency. A remote environmental sensor may prefer the model that stretches battery life for months while keeping accuracy within an acceptable range. The same ratio can guide both decisions, but it does so by clarifying the cost of correct performance rather than replacing the full evaluation.

**Question.** Why separate normalized energy efficiency from the five main metrics instead of making it one of them?

**Answer.** The five main metrics describe distinct measured behaviors: accuracy, latency, energy, spike count, and convergence. Normalized energy efficiency is calculated from at least two of those measurements, mainly accuracy and energy. Keeping it separate helps prevent double-counting while still giving a compact way to compare the practical value of each millijoule.

The mental model is a marketplace for predictions. Accuracy is the quality of the product, energy is the price paid for each product, and normalized energy efficiency asks how much quality comes back per unit of price. Spiking models are interesting because their event-based activity can lower the price dramatically. Converted SNNs reduce the cost compared with continuous ANN computation, surrogate-gradient SNNs preserve high accuracy while improving spike efficiency, and STDP-based SNNs push energy and spike count especially low. Reading later tables and graphs becomes easier once this ratio is in place: raw accuracy shows how correct the model is, raw energy shows how costly it is, and normalized energy efficiency shows how effectively the model converts energy into correct predictions.
