# Spiking Neural Networks

Spiking neural networks are neural models built around **discrete events in time**. Instead of sending continuously updated activation values at every step, they send spikes: brief signals that occur when a neuron becomes active enough to cross a threshold. That shift changes the whole style of computation. A conventional neural network is usually organized around dense, synchronous updates. A spiking neural network is organized around **when events happen**, so timing is part of the representation itself.

This matters because many important systems are not limited only by accuracy. They are also limited by **latency**, **energy use**, and the need to react to changing inputs in real time. Robotics, neuromorphic vision, sensory processing, edge AI systems, and brain-computer interfaces all push in that direction. In these settings, a model that communicates through sparse, event-driven spike trains can become attractive because computation does not have to proceed in the same dense way at every moment. Spiking neural networks are therefore best understood as a way to explore a different balance among intelligence, speed, and power.

A useful way to enter the topic is to start with the limitation of synchronous continuous networks. Standard architectures such as ANNs, CNNs, RNNs, LSTMs, GRUs, and Transformers are powerful, but they are commonly framed around continuous activations and coordinated computation. That style can bring high computational cost, substantial memory or processing demand, weaker alignment with biological signaling, and less natural handling of information whose meaning depends on timing. Spiking neural networks respond to that problem by replacing always-on communication with **sparse spike-based communication**. Information is no longer carried only by how large an activation is. Information is also carried by **whether a spike occurs and when it occurs**.

That is the first big idea to keep in mind while reading: a spiking network does not merely compute different numbers. It computes in a different way. Timing becomes central, silence becomes meaningful, and activity can be concentrated around events instead of spread evenly across all steps. This is why spiking models are closely connected to neuromorphic hardware such as IBM TrueNorth and Intel Loihi, where event-driven processing and low-power operation are especially relevant.

The second big idea is that learning strategy is not a side detail. It is the main comparison axis of this topic. Spiking neural networks are often discussed as if they were one family with one behavior, but the training route strongly shapes what kind of model you actually get. Three named strategies organize the core of this garden: [[Surrogate Gradient Training]], [[ANN-to-SNN Conversion]], and [[Spike-Timing Dependent Plasticity]]. These strategies are worth learning side by side because they lead to different tradeoffs in accuracy, latency, spike activity, energy use, and convergence behavior. The topic is not just "what is an SNN?" but also "what kind of SNN do you get when you choose one learning path rather than another?"

The third big idea is that spiking models should not be judged by accuracy alone. Five metrics form the measurement spine of this garden. **Accuracy** asks how many predictions are correct out of all predictions. **Latency** asks how much time passes between the input stimulus and the model's decision. **Total spike count** asks how much spiking activity occurs across neurons and time steps. **Total energy** asks for the energy cost associated with spike-related and synaptic operations. **Normalized energy efficiency** compares correctness to energy use by relating accuracy to energy consumption. **Convergence time** asks how quickly training reaches a target accuracy, measured by the earliest epoch that achieves it. These metrics matter because a model can look strong on one axis and weak on another, and deployment decisions often depend on those differences.

Once those metrics are in view, the tradeoff story becomes much clearer. Surrogate gradient-trained spiking networks are presented as coming close to conventional neural accuracy, within about **1-2%**, while also showing fast convergence by the **20th epoch** and latency as low as **10 milliseconds**. Converted spiking networks remain competitive, but that competitiveness comes with **higher spike counts** and **longer simulation windows**. STDP-based spiking networks converge more slowly, yet they stand out for **very low spike counts** and **very low energy use**, with energy as low as **5 millijoules per inference**. No single result makes one strategy universally best. The real lesson is that different training strategies favor different deployment priorities.

A simple neuron model helps make that picture concrete. The [[Leaky Integrate-and-Fire Neuron]] gives a basic mental model in which membrane potential changes over time, approaches a threshold, and produces a spike when that threshold is crossed. That is enough intuition for this garden: a spiking neuron is a time-evolving unit whose output is event-like rather than continuously valued. From there, [[Spiking Network Architecture and Lateral Inhibition]] adds a structural picture of how spike-based processing can be organized at the network level.
