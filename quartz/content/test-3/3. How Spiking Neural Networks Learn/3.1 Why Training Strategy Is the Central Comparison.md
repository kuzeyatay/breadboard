A spiking neural network is useful when its discrete spike-based computation leads to useful behavior on a real task. That is why the central question quickly becomes not just what an SNN is, but how it should be trained. SNNs are introduced as a sparse, event-driven alternative to conventional neural networks that rely on synchronous updates and continuous activations. That difference matters when timing, power use, and temporal structure are part of the problem itself. In robotics, neuromorphic vision, edge AI, sensory processing, and brain-computer interfaces, a model must do more than end with the right answer. It must often respond quickly, use little energy, and keep computation sparse.

This event-driven view also changes how to picture the network. A spiking neuron carries information in discrete events, and the named neuron model here is the leaky integrate-and-fire, or LIF, neuron. The important idea is simple: membrane potential changes over time, and a spike is emitted when a threshold is reached. A conceptual SNN architecture is also associated with lateral inhibition, which shows that the network is not just a collection of isolated spiking units but an organized system whose activity can be shaped by interactions between neurons. The detailed equations and full architecture specification are not available here, so the safe lesson is the conceptual one: SNNs compute through timed spike events, threshold behavior, and sparse network activity rather than through continuously active units updating in lockstep.

That difference in computation is exactly why training strategy matters so much. Three training routes define the comparison: surrogate gradient training, ANN-to-SNN conversion, and spike-timing dependent plasticity, or STDP. The point is not that one route is universally best. The point is that each route leads to a different balance of performance, speed, and efficiency. Surrogate gradient-trained SNNs are reported to approach ANN accuracy within about 1-2%, and they also show faster convergence by the 20th epoch. Converted SNNs remain competitive in performance, but they are associated with higher spike counts and longer simulation windows. STDP-based SNNs converge more slowly, yet they are associated with the lowest spike counts and the lowest energy use, reported as low as 5 millijoules per inference. So training is not a minor implementation detail. Training determines the tradeoff profile the final spiking network will have.

A fair comparison therefore needs more than one metric. Accuracy measures correctness, meaning correct predictions divided by total predictions. Latency measures response speed, meaning decision time minus the time when the input stimulus arrived. Total spike count measures how much spiking activity the network produces across neurons and time steps. Total energy measures the energy used by spike-related and synaptic operations during inference. Normalized energy efficiency compares correctness with energy use by asking how much accuracy is achieved per unit of energy consumed. Convergence time measures training speed as the earliest epoch at which a target accuracy is reached. Each metric answers a different practical question. Accuracy asks whether the model is right. Latency asks whether it is fast enough. Spike count and energy ask whether it is efficient enough. Convergence asks whether it becomes useful quickly enough during training. Once those questions are placed side by side, it becomes clear why one number cannot decide the best training strategy.

The first tradeoff to read is accuracy together with normalized energy efficiency, because high performance is not the same thing as efficient performance.

Latency matters most in real-time use. Surrogate gradient-trained SNNs are reported to reach latency as low as 10 milliseconds, which makes them appealing when fast response is essential. Converted SNNs remain competitive in performance, but longer simulation windows work against very low-latency deployment. STDP-based SNNs again occupy a different position: they may be less attractive when fast response and fast convergence are the priority, yet more attractive when sparse spiking and low energy use matter more. The same trained model can therefore be judged very differently depending on whether the deployment target cares most about speed, energy, or training effort.

Spike count and energy sharpen that lesson further. Sparse spiking is not just a stylistic feature of SNNs. It is one of the main reasons they are interesting for low-power computation and neuromorphic hardware. Energy-per-inference and spike-count comparisons are part of the same decision as accuracy and latency, because a method that uses fewer spikes can also become more attractive for efficient deployment. The promise is not just that a model can classify correctly, but that it can do so with sparse event-driven computation that better fits low-power hardware.

Convergence completes the picture because training cost matters too. Final accuracy tells where a model ends up. Convergence tells how quickly it becomes useful. That distinction matters whenever models must be tuned, repeated, or scaled.

This leads to a practical way to think about model choice. If the main goal is ANN-like accuracy, low latency, and faster convergence, surrogate gradient training stands out. If the goal is competitive spiking performance through conversion, ANN-to-SNN conversion remains meaningful, but its higher spike counts and longer simulation windows must be kept in view. If the goal is the lowest spike count and the lowest energy use, STDP becomes attractive even though it converges more slowly. That is the real reason training strategy is the main comparison axis: it decides which tradeoff the final network will embody.

Two longer-term challenges remain part of the picture. Hardware standardization is still unresolved, which matters because efficient deployment depends on how well models and hardware ecosystems align. Scalable training is also still a challenge, which matters because practical success requires not only efficient inference but also methods that can be trained effectively at larger scale. So the lesson is not that SNNs have already solved every problem. The lesson is that they offer a different computational style whose value becomes visible only when training, timing, energy, spike activity, and convergence are evaluated together.

**Question.** Why is accuracy alone not enough for choosing among SNN training strategies?

**Answer.** Accuracy measures correctness, but it does not tell you how quickly a model responds, how much energy it uses, how many spikes it emits, or how fast it reaches useful performance during training. Different training strategies can rank differently on those metrics, so accuracy alone hides the practical reasons one method may be better for edge, mobile, neuromorphic, or real-time deployment.

**Question.** Why can a slower-converging training strategy still be the right choice?

**Answer.** Convergence time measures how quickly training reaches a target accuracy, but deployment depends on inference-time behavior as well. A slower-converging strategy can still be the better choice if it produces much lower spike counts or much lower energy use, because those properties may matter more than training speed in low-power event-driven systems.
