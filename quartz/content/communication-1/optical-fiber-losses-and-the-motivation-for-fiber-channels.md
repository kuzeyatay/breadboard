## Optical Fiber Losses and the Motivation for Fiber Channels

Source: [[communications-1-coursereader|Communications 1 Course Reader - Sampling, PAM, PCM, and Noise]]

Locations: Page 140, Page 141, Section: 11.5.1 Introduction

The wired-channel section motivates the transition from copper to optical fiber by comparing attenuation behavior across media. Copper-based telegraph and telephone lines historically supported global communications, but as digital services increased, the required transport rate rose as well. The text notes that twisted-pair and coaxial cables suffer rapidly increasing attenuation with increasing frequency, while optical fibers exhibit much lower loss at optical wavelengths. Around 1000 nm, the stated loss is about 1 dB per kilometre, and the text emphasizes that this loss does not appear tied to the frequency of the information being carried in the same way as copper channels. Optical fibers were proposed by Charles Kao in the 1960s and refined into ultra-low-loss systems. This section situates fiber as the backbone technology for global and increasingly local transport between fixed sites. It also reminds the reader that wavelength-frequency conversion remains fundamental by posing the question of the frequency corresponding to a 1500 nm wavelength in vacuum.

### Key points

- Copper lines were foundational for telegraph and telephone networks.
- As digital traffic increased, higher data transport speed was needed on wired links.
- Twisted pair and coaxial cable attenuation rises rapidly with increasing frequency.
- Optical fiber around 1000 nm is described as having only about 1 dB/km loss.
- Fiber loss is presented as not strongly dependent on the information frequency carried.
- Charles Kao proposed optical fiber communication in the 1960s.
- Optical fiber became a backbone medium for high-capacity fixed-site communication.
