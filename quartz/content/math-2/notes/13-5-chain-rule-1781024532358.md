## 13.5 Chain Rule



Partial derivatives tell us how a function of several variables changes when one input changes and the other inputs are held fixed. That is the right first step, but it is not enough when the inputs themselves are changing. In many problems, the variables $x$, $y$, and $z$ are not independent quantities chosen separately. A point may move along a curve, so its coordinates $x(t)$, $y(t)$, and $z(t)$ all change with time. A temperature field may depend on position and time, while a thermometer moves through the field. A quantity may be written in one coordinate system and then re-expressed in another coordinate system. In all these cases, the question is not only “how does the function change when one input changes?” but rather “how does the final output change when its inputs change through other variables?”

The chain rule answers this question. It appears here because the course has now introduced all the ingredients needed for it. From vector functions and parametrizations, we know how coordinates can depend on a parameter such as time. From functions of several variables, we know how an output such as $z=f(x,y)$ depends on more than one input. From partial derivatives, we know how to measure the separate influence of each input. The chain rule combines these separate influences into the actual rate of change of a composed quantity.

The word composition means that the output of one function is used as the input of another. In one-variable calculus, if

$$
y=f(u), \qquad u=g(t),
$$

then $y$ depends on $t$ indirectly, through $u$. The derivative is

$$
\frac{dy}{dt}=\frac{dy}{du}\frac{du}{dt}.
$$

Here $t$ is the independent variable, $u$ is the intermediate variable, and $y$ is the final output. The formula says that the rate at which $y$ changes with respect to $t$ is the rate at which $y$ changes with respect to $u$, multiplied by the rate at which $u$ changes with respect to $t$. In several variables, the same idea remains true, but there may be several routes through which a change can reach the final output. For this reason, the multivariable chain rule is best understood as a procedure: follow every route of dependence from the final output to the variable of differentiation, multiply the derivative factors along each route, and add the route contributions.

The simplest multivariable case is a function of two variables evaluated along a moving point. Let

$$
z=f(x,y),
$$

where $x$ and $y$ are the input variables and $z$ is the output. Suppose now that the point $(x,y)$ moves with time, so that

$$
x=u(t), \qquad y=v(t).
$$

The functions $u(t)$ and $v(t)$ give the two coordinates at time $t$. The output is therefore

$$
z=f(u(t),v(t)).
$$

This means that $z$ is now indirectly a function of $t$. A change in $t$ can change $z$ in two ways: it can change $x$, and it can change $y$. The contribution through $x$ is the sensitivity of $z$ to $x$, multiplied by the rate at which $x$ changes with time. The contribution through $y$ is the sensitivity of $z$ to $y$, multiplied by the rate at which $y$ changes with time. Therefore,

$$
\frac{dz}{dt}=\frac{\partial z}{\partial x}\frac{dx}{dt}+\frac{\partial z}{\partial y}\frac{dy}{dt}.
$$

In this formula, $z=f(x,y)$, while $x=x(t)$ and $y=y(t)$. The partial derivative $\partial z/\partial x$ means the rate of change of $z$ with respect to $x$, with $y$ held fixed. The derivative $dx/dt$ means the rate at which the moving point’s $x$-coordinate changes with time. Their product gives the contribution to $dz/dt$ coming through the $x$-coordinate. The second product has the same meaning for the $y$-coordinate. Both terms must be included when both coordinates depend on $t$.

A common mistake is to compute only one term. That would mean pretending that only one coordinate changes. Another common mistake is to evaluate the partial derivatives at the wrong point. The partial derivatives $\partial z/\partial x$ and $\partial z/\partial y$ must be evaluated at the current input point $(x(t),y(t))$, not at $(t,t)$ and not at an unrelated point. More explicitly,

$$
\frac{d}{dt}f(u(t),v(t))=f_1(u(t),v(t))u'(t)+f_2(u(t),v(t))v'(t),
$$

where $f_1$ means the partial derivative of $f$ with respect to its first input, and $f_2$ means the partial derivative of $f$ with respect to its second input.

As a concrete example, let the height of a landscape above the $xy$-plane be

$$
H(x,y)=\sin x \sin y.
$$

Suppose a person moves along the path

$$
y=x^2-2
$$

with speed $5$ in the $xy$-plane, and suppose the person is at the point $(-1,-1)$. The phrase “speed $5$ in the $xy$-plane” means that the horizontal velocity vector

$$
\left\langle \frac{dx}{dt},\frac{dy}{dt}\right\rangle
$$

has length $5$. Because the person stays on the path $y=x^2-2$, the coordinate rates are not independent. Differentiating the path equation with respect to $t$ gives

$$
\frac{dy}{dt}=2x\frac{dx}{dt}.
$$

At $x=-1$, this becomes

$$
\frac{dy}{dt}=-2\frac{dx}{dt}.
$$

The speed condition gives

$$
5=\sqrt{\left(\frac{dx}{dt}\right)^2+\left(\frac{dy}{dt}\right)^2}.
$$

Substituting $\frac{dy}{dt}=-2\frac{dx}{dt}$, we obtain

$$
5=\sqrt{\left(\frac{dx}{dt}\right)^2+4\left(\frac{dx}{dt}\right)^2}=\sqrt{5}\left|\frac{dx}{dt}\right|.
$$

Thus

$$
\left|\frac{dx}{dt}\right|=\sqrt{5}.
$$

There are two possible directions along the path. If $\frac{dx}{dt}=\sqrt{5}$, then $\frac{dy}{dt}=-2\sqrt{5}$. If $\frac{dx}{dt}=-\sqrt{5}$, then $\frac{dy}{dt}=2\sqrt{5}$. To decide which one corresponds to descending, compute the rate of change of the height.

The partial derivatives of $H$ are

$$
H_x(x,y)=\cos x\sin y,
\qquad
H_y(x,y)=\sin x\cos y.
$$

At $(-1,-1)$, these become

$$
H_x(-1,-1)=-\cos(1)\sin(1),
\qquad
H_y(-1,-1)=-\sin(1)\cos(1).
$$

The chain rule gives

$$
\frac{dH}{dt}=H_x\frac{dx}{dt}+H_y\frac{dy}{dt}.
$$

If $\frac{dx}{dt}=\sqrt{5}$ and $\frac{dy}{dt}=-2\sqrt{5}$, then

$$
\frac{dH}{dt}=\sqrt{5}\sin(1)\cos(1)>0.
$$

This direction is uphill. Therefore the descending velocity is the opposite one:

$$
\left\langle \frac{dx}{dt},\frac{dy}{dt}\right\rangle=\left\langle -\sqrt{5},2\sqrt{5}\right\rangle.
$$

In that direction,

$$
\frac{dH}{dt}=-\sqrt{5}\sin(1)\cos(1)<0.
$$

This example shows the meaning of the chain rule in a practical setting. A partial derivative such as $H_x$ or $H_y$ does not by itself give the rate of ascent or descent along the actual path. The actual rate depends on both the local height sensitivities and the actual velocity of the moving point.

The same distinction appears in temperature problems. Suppose the temperature of a liquid is modeled by

$$
T=e^{-tz},
$$

where $T$ is temperature, $t$ is time, and $z$ is depth. If the depth of a moving object is itself a function of time,

$$
z=f(t),
$$

then the measured temperature is the composite function

$$
T(t)=e^{-t f(t)}.
$$

The variable $t$ affects $T$ in two ways. It appears directly in the expression $e^{-tz}$, and it also affects $T$ indirectly because $z$ changes with $t$. The chain rule gives

$$
\frac{dT}{dt}=\frac{\partial T}{\partial t}+\frac{\partial T}{\partial z}\frac{dz}{dt}.
$$

Here $\partial T/\partial t$ means the rate of change with respect to time while depth is held fixed, and $\partial T/\partial z$ means the rate of change with respect to depth while time is held fixed. Since

$$
\frac{\partial T}{\partial t}=-z e^{-tz},
\qquad
\frac{\partial T}{\partial z}=-t e^{-tz},
$$

we obtain

$$
\frac{dT}{dt}=-z e^{-tz}-t e^{-tz}\frac{dz}{dt}.
$$

After substituting $z=f(t)$, this becomes

$$
\frac{dT}{dt}=-f(t)e^{-t f(t)}-t f'(t)e^{-t f(t)}.
$$

The first term measures the direct time effect at fixed depth. The second term measures the effect of moving to a different depth. This is the same chain-rule structure as before, but here the independent variable $t$ reaches $T$ both directly and through an intermediate variable.

This leads to an important notation issue. In multivariable calculus, a partial derivative only has a clear meaning when we know which variables are being held fixed. For example, if $w=w(x,y,z)$, then

$$
\frac{\partial w}{\partial z}
$$

usually means that $x$ and $y$ are held fixed while $z$ changes. Sometimes this is written more explicitly as

$$
\left(\frac{\partial w}{\partial z}\right)_{x,y}.
$$

The subscript $x,y$ means “holding $x$ and $y$ fixed.” If instead $y$ depends on $z$ while $x$ is held fixed, then the derivative of $w$ with respect to $z$ is different. In that situation,

$$
\left(\frac{\partial w}{\partial z}\right)_x=\frac{\partial w}{\partial y}\left(\frac{\partial y}{\partial z}\right)_x+\frac{\partial w}{\partial z}.
$$

The notation $\left(\partial w/\partial z\right)_x$ means that $x$ is held fixed, but $y$ is allowed to change with $z$. By contrast,

$$
\left(\frac{\partial w}{\partial z}\right)_{x,y}=\frac{\partial w}{\partial z}
$$

because both $x$ and $y$ are held fixed. This distinction is essential: the same-looking derivative “with respect to $z$” can mean different things depending on which other variables are fixed and which are allowed to vary.

The chain rule can also involve two independent variables. Suppose

$$
z=f(x,y),
\qquad
x=u(s,t),
\qquad
y=v(s,t).
$$

Here $z$ depends on $s$ and $t$ through the intermediate variables $x$ and $y$. The composed function is

$$
z=f(u(s,t),v(s,t)).
$$

To compute $\partial z/\partial s$, we vary $s$ while holding $t$ fixed. Even though $t$ is fixed, both $x$ and $y$ may still change because both depend on $s$. Therefore,

$$
\frac{\partial z}{\partial s}=\frac{\partial z}{\partial x}\frac{\partial x}{\partial s}+\frac{\partial z}{\partial y}\frac{\partial y}{\partial s}.
$$

Similarly, to compute $\partial z/\partial t$, we vary $t$ while holding $s$ fixed:

$$
\frac{\partial z}{\partial t}=\frac{\partial z}{\partial x}\frac{\partial x}{\partial t}+\frac{\partial z}{\partial y}\frac{\partial y}{\partial t}.
$$

The variables $s$ and $t$ are the independent variables of the composed function. The variables $x$ and $y$ are the immediate input variables of the outer function $f$. The rule says that each derivative of $z$ is obtained by adding one contribution for each immediate input variable of $f$.

For example, let

$$
z=\sin(x^2y),
\qquad
x=st^2,
\qquad
y=s^2+\frac{1}{t}.
$$

We want $\partial z/\partial s$ and $\partial z/\partial t$. First compute the partial derivatives of $z$ with respect to its own input variables $x$ and $y$:

$$
\frac{\partial z}{\partial x}=2xy\cos(x^2y),
\qquad
\frac{\partial z}{\partial y}=x^2\cos(x^2y).
$$

Next compute the partial derivatives of $x$ and $y$ with respect to $s$ and $t$:

$$
\frac{\partial x}{\partial s}=t^2,
\qquad
\frac{\partial y}{\partial s}=2s,
$$

and

$$
\frac{\partial x}{\partial t}=2st,
\qquad
\frac{\partial y}{\partial t}=-\frac{1}{t^2}.
$$

Therefore,

$$
\frac{\partial z}{\partial s}=\left(2xy\cos(x^2y)\right)t^2+\left(x^2\cos(x^2y)\right)2s.
$$

Now substitute $x=st^2$ and $y=s^2+\frac{1}{t}$. Since

$$
x^2y=(st^2)^2\left(s^2+\frac{1}{t}\right)=s^4t^4+s^2t^3,
$$

we obtain

$$
\frac{\partial z}{\partial s}=(4s^3t^4+2st^3)\cos(s^4t^4+s^2t^3).
$$

Similarly,

$$
\frac{\partial z}{\partial t}=\left(2xy\cos(x^2y)\right)2st+\left(x^2\cos(x^2y)\right)\left(-\frac{1}{t^2}\right),
$$

so

$$
\frac{\partial z}{\partial t}=(4s^4t^3+3s^2t^2)\cos(s^4t^4+s^2t^3).
$$

One could also substitute first:

$$
z=\sin(s^4t^4+s^2t^3),
$$

and then differentiate directly with respect to $s$ and $t$. The result is the same. The value of the chain rule is that it gives a systematic method even when substitution is inconvenient or when the outer function is not explicitly known.

A slightly different composition occurs when the outer function has only one input, but that input depends on several variables. Suppose

$$
h(s,t)=f(g(s,t)).
$$

Here $g(s,t)$ is a function of two variables, but $f$ is an ordinary one-variable function. The output $h$ changes with $s$ because $g$ changes with $s$, and then $f$ responds to that change in $g$. Therefore,

$$
\frac{\partial h}{\partial s}=f'(g(s,t))\frac{\partial g}{\partial s}(s,t),
$$

and

$$
\frac{\partial h}{\partial t}=f'(g(s,t))\frac{\partial g}{\partial t}(s,t).
$$

This is still the chain rule. The difference is that there is only one route from $h$ to $s$, because the outer function $f$ has only one input. In the previous case $z=f(x,y)$, there were two routes because the outer function had two inputs.

The chain rule is also useful when the outer function is written abstractly. Suppose $f$ is a differentiable function of two variables, but its formula is not given. We can still differentiate an expression such as

$$
f(x^2y,x+2y).
$$

Let

$$
u=x^2y,
\qquad
v=x+2y.
$$

Then the expression is $f(u,v)$. The derivative with respect to $x$ is

$$
\frac{\partial}{\partial x}f(x^2y,x+2y)=f_1(u,v)\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}+f_2(u,v)\frac{\partial v}{\partial x}.
$$

Here $f_1$ means the partial derivative of $f$ with respect to its first input, and $f_2$ means the partial derivative of $f$ with respect to its second input. Since

$$
\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}=2xy,
\qquad
\frac{\partial v}{\partial x}=1,
$$

we get

$$
\frac{\partial}{\partial x}f(x^2y,x+2y)=2xy f_1(x^2y,x+2y)+f_2(x^2y,x+2y).
$$

Similarly,

$$
\frac{\partial}{\partial y}f(x^2y,x+2y)=x^2 f_1(x^2y,x+2y)+2f_2(x^2y,x+2y).
$$

This notation is a frequent source of confusion. The expression $f_1(x^2y,x+2y)$ does not mean “differentiate with respect to $x$.” It means “differentiate $f$ with respect to its first input, then evaluate that derivative at the input pair $(x^2y,x+2y)$.” The input variables of the outer function $f$ are not necessarily the same as the variables $x$ and $y$ appearing in the final expression.

Dependency charts are a useful way to organize chain-rule problems. A dependency chart is a diagram showing which variables directly depend on which other variables. To use the chart, start from the final output and follow every route to the variable of differentiation. Each route gives one product of derivative factors, and the full derivative is the sum of all route products.

For instance, let

$$
T=T(x,y,z,t)
$$

be the temperature at position $(x,y,z)$ and time $t$. Suppose a weather balloon carries a thermometer and moves according to

$$
x=x(t),
\qquad
y=y(t),
\qquad
z=z(t).
$$

The measured temperature changes with time in two different ways. First, $T$ may change directly with time even at a fixed position. Second, the balloon moves to different positions, and temperature may vary from place to place. Therefore,

$$
\frac{dT}{dt}=\frac{\partial T}{\partial x}\frac{dx}{dt}+\frac{\partial T}{\partial y}\frac{dy}{dt}+\frac{\partial T}{\partial z}\frac{dz}{dt}+\frac{\partial T}{\partial t}.
$$

The first three terms are the spatial-motion contributions. The last term is the direct time contribution at fixed $x$, $y$, and $z$. This distinction is important. The total derivative $dT/dt$ is the rate measured by the moving balloon. The partial derivative $\partial T/\partial t$ is only the rate of temperature change at a fixed point in space.

For example, let

$$
T(x,y,z,t)=\frac{xy}{1+z}(1+t),
$$

and suppose the balloon moves according to

$$
x=t,
\qquad
y=2t,
\qquad
z=t-t^2.
$$

At $t=1$,

$$
x=1,
\qquad
y=2,
\qquad
z=0,
$$

and

$$
\frac{dx}{dt}=1,
\qquad
\frac{dy}{dt}=2,
\qquad
\frac{dz}{dt}=1-2t=-1.
$$

The partial derivatives of $T$ are

$$
T_x=\frac{y}{1+z}(1+t),
\qquad
T_y=\frac{x}{1+z}(1+t),
$$

$$
T_z=-\frac{xy}{(1+z)^2}(1+t),
\qquad
T_t=\frac{xy}{1+z}.
$$

At $t=1$, these are

$$
T_x=4,
\qquad
T_y=2,
\qquad
T_z=-4,
\qquad
T_t=2.
$$

Thus

$$
\frac{dT}{dt}=4(1)+2(2)+(-4)(-1)+2=14.
$$

If temperature is measured in degrees Celsius and time in hours, this means that the moving thermometer records an increase of $14$ degrees Celsius per hour.

More complicated dependency charts are handled by the same rule. Suppose $z$ depends on $u$, $v$, and $r$. Suppose $u$ and $v$ depend on $x$, $y$, and $r$. Suppose finally that $r$ depends on $x$ and $y$. To compute $\partial z/\partial x$, we must include every route from $z$ to $x$. There is a route from $z$ to $u$ to $x$, a route from $z$ to $u$ to $r$ to $x$, a route from $z$ to $v$ to $x$, a route from $z$ to $v$ to $r$ to $x$, and a route from $z$ directly through $r$ to $x$. Hence,

$$
\frac{\partial z}{\partial x}=\frac{\partial z}{\partial u}\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}+\frac{\partial z}{\partial u}\frac{\partial u}{\partial r}\frac{\partial r}{\partial x}+\frac{\partial z}{\partial v}\frac{\partial v}{\partial x}+\frac{\partial z}{\partial v}\frac{\partial v}{\partial r}\frac{\partial r}{\partial x}+\frac{\partial z}{\partial r}\frac{\partial r}{\partial x}.
$$

The point is not to memorize this formula. The point is to learn the procedure: draw the dependency structure, follow every path to the variable of differentiation, multiply along each path, and add.

The chain rule can also be applied repeatedly to compute higher-order derivatives. This is where careful bookkeeping becomes especially important. Suppose

$$
z=f(x,y),
\qquad
x=2s+3t,
\qquad
y=3s-2t.
$$

The first derivatives are

$$
z_s=f_1x_s+f_2y_s=2f_1+3f_2,
$$

and

$$
z_t=f_1x_t+f_2y_t=3f_1-2f_2.
$$

Here $f_1$ and $f_2$ are evaluated at

$$
(x,y)=(2s+3t,3s-2t).
$$

To compute $z_{ss}$, differentiate $z_s=2f_1+3f_2$ with respect to $s$. Since $f_1$ and $f_2$ are themselves functions of $x$ and $y$, and since $x$ and $y$ depend on $s$, the chain rule must be used again:

$$
z_{ss}=2(f_{11}x_s+f_{12}y_s)+3(f_{21}x_s+f_{22}y_s).
$$

Using $x_s=2$, $y_s=3$, and $f_{12}=f_{21}$ when the second partial derivatives are continuous, this becomes

$$
z_{ss}=4f_{11}+12f_{12}+9f_{22}.
$$

Similarly, to compute $z_{st}$, differentiate $z_s=2f_1+3f_2$ with respect to $t$:

$$
z_{st}=2(f_{11}x_t+f_{12}y_t)+3(f_{21}x_t+f_{22}y_t).
$$

Using $x_t=3$, $y_t=-2$, and $f_{12}=f_{21}$, we obtain

$$
z_{st}=6f_{11}+5f_{12}-6f_{22}.
$$

To compute $z_{tt}$, differentiate $z_t=3f_1-2f_2$ with respect to $t$:

$$
z_{tt}=3(f_{11}x_t+f_{12}y_t)-2(f_{21}x_t+f_{22}y_t).
$$

Therefore,

$$
z_{tt}=9f_{11}-12f_{12}+4f_{22}.
$$

Every derivative of $f$ in these formulas is evaluated at $(2s+3t,3s-2t)$, not at $(s,t)$. This is one of the main points in higher-order chain-rule problems. The function $f$ is originally a function of $x$ and $y$, so its derivatives are evaluated at values of $x$ and $y$.

A useful pattern appears when $x$ and $y$ are linear functions of $s$ and $t$. If

$$
x=as+bt,
\qquad
y=cs+dt,
$$

then

$$
z_{tt}=b^2f_{11}+2bd f_{12}+d^2f_{22}.
$$

This formula follows by applying the chain rule twice and using $f_{12}=f_{21}$. For example, if

$$
x=2s+4t,
\qquad
y=3s-2t,
$$

then $b=4$ and $d=-2$, so

$$
z_{tt}=16f_{11}-16f_{12}+4f_{22}.
$$

This is the kind of expression where it is easy to lose a factor of $2$ in the mixed term. The mixed term is $2bd f_{12}$, not merely $bd f_{12}$, because it receives contributions from both $f_{12}$ and $f_{21}$.

Higher-order chain-rule calculations also occur when the inner variables are nonlinear. Let

$$
u=x^2-y^2,
\qquad
v=xy,
\qquad
z=f(u,v).
$$

First compute the derivative with respect to $y$:

$$
z_y=f_1u_y+f_2v_y.
$$

Since

$$
u_y=-2y,
\qquad
v_y=x,
$$

we have

$$
z_y=-2y f_1(u,v)+x f_2(u,v).
$$

Now differentiate this expression with respect to $x$. The first term requires the product rule because it contains $-2y$ multiplied by $f_1(u,v)$, although $y$ is treated as constant when differentiating with respect to $x$. The second term also requires the product rule because it contains $x f_2(u,v)$. Applying the chain rule to $f_1(u,v)$ and $f_2(u,v)$ gives

$$
z_{xy}=-2y\left(f_{11}u_x+f_{12}v_x\right)+f_2+x\left(f_{21}u_x+f_{22}v_x\right).
$$

Since

$$
u_x=2x,
\qquad
v_x=y,
$$

this becomes

$$
z_{xy}=-2y(2x f_{11}+y f_{12})+f_2+x(2x f_{21}+y f_{22}).
$$

Using $f_{12}=f_{21}$, we obtain

$$
z_{xy}=f_2(u,v)-4xyf_{11}(u,v)+2(x^2-y^2)f_{12}(u,v)+xyf_{22}(u,v).
$$

All derivatives of $f$ are evaluated at

$$
(u,v)=(x^2-y^2,xy).
$$

They are not evaluated at $(x,y)$. This distinction is essential because $x$ and $y$ are not the direct inputs of $f$; they affect $f$ only through $u$ and $v$.

The chain rule also explains a useful result about homogeneous functions. A function of $n$ variables,

$$
f(x_1,x_2,\ldots,x_n),
$$

is called positively homogeneous of degree $k$ if scaling all input variables by the same positive factor $t$ scales the output by $t^k$. Formally,

$$
f(tx_1,tx_2,\ldots,tx_n)=t^k f(x_1,x_2,\ldots,x_n),
\qquad
t>0.
$$

The number $k$ is called the degree of homogeneity. For example,

$$
f(x,y)=x^2+xy-y^2
$$

is homogeneous of degree $2$, because

$$
f(tx,ty)=(tx)^2+(tx)(ty)-(ty)^2=t^2(x^2+xy-y^2).
$$

The function

$$
f(x,y)=\sqrt{x^2+y^2}
$$

is homogeneous of degree $1$, because multiplying both coordinates by $t>0$ multiplies the distance from the origin by $t$. A function homogeneous of degree $0$, such as

$$
f(x,y)=\frac{2xy}{x^2+y^2},
$$

does not change when both variables are multiplied by the same positive factor. Such a function is constant along each ray from the origin, because scaling changes distance from the origin but not direction.

Euler’s theorem for homogeneous functions follows directly from the chain rule. Suppose $f(x_1,\ldots,x_n)$ has continuous first partial derivatives and is positively homogeneous of degree $k$. Then

$$
f(tx_1,\ldots,tx_n)=t^k f(x_1,\ldots,x_n).
$$

Differentiate both sides with respect to $t$. On the left side, the input point

$$
(tx_1,\ldots,tx_n)
$$

moves as $t$ changes, so the chain rule gives

$$
x_1f_1(tx_1,\ldots,tx_n)+x_2f_2(tx_1,\ldots,tx_n)+\cdots+x_nf_n(tx_1,\ldots,tx_n).
$$

On the right side,

$$
\frac{d}{dt}\left(t^k f(x_1,\ldots,x_n)\right)=kt^{k-1}f(x_1,\ldots,x_n),
$$

because $x_1,\ldots,x_n$ are held fixed while differentiating with respect to $t$. Thus,

$$
x_1f_1(tx_1,\ldots,tx_n)+\cdots+x_nf_n(tx_1,\ldots,tx_n)=kt^{k-1}f(x_1,\ldots,x_n).
$$

Setting $t=1$ gives Euler’s theorem:

$$
\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_i f_i(x_1,\ldots,x_n)=k f(x_1,\ldots,x_n).
$$

This identity says that if a function has a predictable scaling behavior, then its partial derivatives satisfy a matching scaling relation. The proof is not a new method; it is simply the chain rule applied to the scaling path

$$
t\mapsto (tx_1,\ldots,tx_n).
$$

The chain rule also appears in coordinate changes. This course already introduced cylindrical and spherical coordinates, so it is natural to ask how derivatives behave when a function is re-expressed in a different coordinate system. In polar coordinates in the plane,

$$
x=r\cos\theta,
\qquad
y=r\sin\theta.
$$

If $z=f(x,y)$, then after substitution $z$ may be viewed as a function of $r$ and $\theta$:

$$
z=f(r\cos\theta,r\sin\theta).
$$

The chain rule gives

$$
z_r=f_x x_r+f_y y_r.
$$

Since

$$
x_r=\cos\theta,
\qquad
y_r=\sin\theta,
$$

we obtain

$$
z_r=f_x\cos\theta+f_y\sin\theta.
$$

Similarly,

$$
z_\theta=f_xx_\theta+f_yy_\theta.
$$

Since

$$
x_\theta=-r\sin\theta,
\qquad
y_\theta=r\cos\theta,
$$

we get

$$
z_\theta=-r f_x\sin\theta+r f_y\cos\theta.
$$

These formulas show how changes in $r$ and $\theta$ affect $z$ through the Cartesian variables $x$ and $y$. The factor $r$ in the $\theta$-formula is not accidental. Changing the angle by a small amount moves a point along an arc, and the length of that arc depends on the distance $r$ from the origin.

Repeated use of the chain rule in coordinate changes can produce extra terms. For example, when the expression

$$
f_{xx}+f_{yy}
$$

is rewritten in polar coordinates, the result is not simply $z_{rr}+z_{\theta\theta}$. The correct polar form contains scale factors:

$$
f_{xx}+f_{yy}=z_{rr}+\frac{1}{r}z_r+\frac{1}{r^2}z_{\theta\theta}.
$$

Here $z(r,\theta)=f(r\cos\theta,r\sin\theta)$. The extra term $(1/r)z_r$ and the factor $1/r^2$ appear because polar coordinates do not measure distance the same way in the radial and angular directions. This is a useful warning: when changing coordinates, do not transform higher-order derivatives by simply replacing symbols. The chain rule must be applied carefully.

A related chain-rule issue appears in cylindrical-coordinate velocity. In cylindrical coordinates, the position of a point in the plane can be written as

$$
\mathbf{r}(t)=r(t)\hat{\mathbf{r}}(\theta(t)).
$$

Here $r(t)$ is the distance from the origin or axis, and $\hat{\mathbf{r}}(\theta(t))$ is the radial unit vector pointing in the direction determined by the angle $\theta(t)$. The unit vector $\hat{\mathbf{r}}$ is not fixed. It changes when $\theta$ changes. Differentiating $\mathbf{r}(t)$ therefore requires both the product rule and the chain rule:

$$
\frac{d\mathbf{r}}{dt}=\frac{dr}{dt}\hat{\mathbf{r}}+r\frac{d\hat{\mathbf{r}}}{dt}.
$$

Since $\hat{\mathbf{r}}$ depends on $\theta$, and $\theta$ depends on $t$,

$$
\frac{d\hat{\mathbf{r}}}{dt}=\frac{d\hat{\mathbf{r}}}{d\theta}\frac{d\theta}{dt}.
$$

In cylindrical coordinates,

$$
\frac{d\hat{\mathbf{r}}}{d\theta}=\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}},
$$

where $\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}$ is the unit vector perpendicular to $\hat{\mathbf{r}}$ in the direction of increasing $\theta$. Hence,

$$
\mathbf{v}=\dot{r}\,\hat{\mathbf{r}}+r\dot{\theta}\,\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}.
$$

The dot notation means differentiation with respect to time:

$$
\dot{r}=\frac{dr}{dt},
\qquad
\dot{\theta}=\frac{d\theta}{dt}.
$$

The term $\dot{r}\,\hat{\mathbf{r}}$ is radial velocity. The term $r\dot{\theta}\,\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}$ is tangential velocity. A frequent error is to write

$$
\dot{r}\,\hat{\mathbf{r}}+\dot{\theta}\,\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}},
$$

forgetting the factor $r$. That expression has the wrong units and the wrong geometry. Angular speed $\dot{\theta}$ measures radians per unit time, while tangential speed is arc length per unit time, which is $r\dot{\theta}$.

For example, suppose a slider in a rotating slotted arm has

$$
r=2-0.1t,
\qquad
\theta=0.5t.
$$

At $t=2$,

$$
r=2-0.2=1.8,
\qquad
\dot{r}=-0.1,
\qquad
\dot{\theta}=0.5.
$$

Therefore,

$$
\mathbf{v}=-0.1\,\hat{\mathbf{r}}+(1.8)(0.5)\,\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}=-0.1\,\hat{\mathbf{r}}+0.9\,\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}.
$$

This is a chain-rule calculation because the direction of $\hat{\mathbf{r}}$ changes through the changing angle $\theta(t)$.

The central idea of this section is that derivatives must follow the actual dependency structure of the variables. A partial derivative measures the sensitivity of an outer function to one of its immediate inputs. A derivative of an inner variable measures how that input changes with respect to the variable of differentiation. The chain rule multiplies these factors along each route of dependence and adds all route contributions. Along a path, this gives rates such as ascent, descent, or measured temperature change. With several independent variables, it gives partial derivatives such as $\partial z/\partial s$ and $\partial z/\partial t$. In dependency diagrams, it gives a reliable bookkeeping method. In higher-order derivatives, it must be applied repeatedly while keeping track of the true input variables of the outer function. In homogeneous functions, it turns scaling behavior into Euler’s identity. In coordinate systems, it explains why angular and radial changes carry different factors. Across all cases, the method is the same: identify what depends on what, follow every route, multiply along each route, and add the contributions.
