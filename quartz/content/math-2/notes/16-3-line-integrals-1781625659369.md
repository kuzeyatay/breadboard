## 16.3 Line Integrals

Integration has already been used to add quantities over intervals, plane regions, and solid regions. A single integral adds small pieces along a straight interval on the real line. A double integral adds small area contributions over a region in the plane. A triple integral adds small volume contributions throughout a solid in space. The new situation in this section is that the object over which we want to integrate is not a straight interval, an area, or a volume. It is a curve.

This problem appears naturally when a physical quantity is distributed along a thin curved object. A wire, for example, may be bent into a curve in space. If its density varies from point to point, then its mass is not found by multiplying one constant density by one length. Instead, the wire must be divided into many very small curve pieces. On each small piece, the density is approximately constant, so the mass of that piece is approximately

$$
\text{density at the piece}\times \text{length of the piece}.
$$

A line integral is the mathematical operation that performs this addition along a curve. The word “line” here does not mean that the path must be a straight line. It means that the integration takes place along a one-dimensional curve.

[Insert figure here: a curved wire in space divided into many short arc pieces. Label one short piece by $ds$, and label a point on the wire by $\mathbf r(t)$. Indicate that a scalar value $f(\mathbf r(t))$, such as density, is attached to that point. This figure belongs here because it shows why the basic infinitesimal object in a scalar line integral is a small length along the curve.]

To integrate along a curve, we first need a way to describe the curve. Suppose $\mathcal C$ is a curve in $\mathbb R^3$. A parametrization of $\mathcal C$ is a vector-valued function

$$
\mathbf r(t)=x(t)\mathbf i+y(t)\mathbf j+z(t)\mathbf k,\qquad a\leq t\leq b.
$$

Here $t$ is the parameter, $\mathbf r(t)$ is the position vector of the point on the curve corresponding to $t$, and $x(t)$, $y(t)$, and $z(t)$ are the coordinate functions of that point. The interval $a\leq t\leq b$ tells us which part of the curve is traced. In the plane, the same idea is used with

$$
\mathbf r(t)=x(t)\mathbf i+y(t)\mathbf j.
$$

The derivative of the parametrization is

$$
\mathbf r'(t)=\frac{d\mathbf r}{dt}=x'(t)\mathbf i+y'(t)\mathbf j+z'(t)\mathbf k.
$$

This vector points tangent to the curve, in the direction in which the parametrization moves. Its length is

$$
|\mathbf r'(t)|=\sqrt{(x'(t))^2+(y'(t))^2+(z'(t))^2}.
$$

This quantity is the speed of the parametrization. Even when $t$ is not literally time, $|\mathbf r'(t)|$ tells us how much arc length is traced per unit change in $t$. Therefore a small change $dt$ in the parameter corresponds to a small length

$$
ds=|\mathbf r'(t)|\,dt
$$

along the curve. The symbol $ds$ means a small scalar arc-length element. It is not the same thing as $dx$, $dy$, $dz$, or $dt$. It is the actual distance measured along the curve.

A curve is called smooth on an interval if $\mathbf r'(t)$ is continuous and nonzero there. The continuity condition prevents sudden jumps in the tangent direction, and the nonzero condition prevents the parametrization from stopping. A curve is called piecewise smooth if it can be split into finitely many smooth pieces. Line integrals over piecewise smooth curves are computed by integrating over each smooth piece and then adding the results.

Now let $f(x,y,z)$ be a scalar field. A scalar field assigns one real number to each point in its domain. For example, $f(x,y,z)$ could represent the density of a wire at the point $(x,y,z)$. If the curve is parametrized by $\mathbf r(t)$, then the value of the scalar field on the curve is

$$
f(\mathbf r(t))=f(x(t),y(t),z(t)).
$$

The scalar line integral of $f$ along $\mathcal C$ is written

$$
\int_{\mathcal C} f(x,y,z)\,ds.
$$

This notation means: add the scalar values of $f$ along the curve, weighted by small pieces of arc length. Using the parametrization, the computational formula is

$$
\int_{\mathcal C} f(x,y,z)\,ds=\int_a^b f(\mathbf r(t))|\mathbf r'(t)|\,dt.
$$

Here $\mathbf r(t)$ is the parametrization of the curve for $a\leq t\leq b$, $f(\mathbf r(t))$ is the scalar field evaluated on the curve, and $|\mathbf r'(t)|dt$ is the small length element $ds$. This formula is the main formula of scalar line integrals.

The formula can be understood directly from a Riemann-sum idea. Divide the parameter interval $[a,b]$ into many small subintervals. Each subinterval corresponds to a short piece of the curve. If $\Delta s_i$ denotes the length of the $i$-th small curve piece, and if $t_i^*$ is a sample parameter value in that piece, then the contribution from that piece is approximately

$$
f(\mathbf r(t_i^*))\,\Delta s_i.
$$

Adding these contributions gives

$$
\sum_i f(\mathbf r(t_i^*))\,\Delta s_i.
$$

As the pieces become smaller and smaller, this sum approaches the line integral

$$
\int_{\mathcal C}f\,ds.
$$

Since a small length along the curve satisfies $\Delta s\approx |\mathbf r'(t)|\Delta t$, the limiting integral becomes

$$
\int_a^b f(\mathbf r(t))|\mathbf r'(t)|\,dt.
$$

This explains why the speed factor $|\mathbf r'(t)|$ must appear. It is not optional. It converts the parameter increment $dt$ into actual distance along the curve.

A particularly important special case is $f=1$. Then

$$
\int_{\mathcal C}1\,ds=\int_a^b |\mathbf r'(t)|\,dt.
$$

This is exactly the arc length of the curve. Thus scalar line integrals generalize arc length: instead of adding only length, they add length weighted by a scalar field.

If $f$ represents density, then the mass of a wire lying along $\mathcal C$ is

$$
M=\int_{\mathcal C} f\,ds.
$$

If the density is $\delta(x,y,z)$, the mass formula is

$$
M=\int_{\mathcal C}\delta(x,y,z)\,ds.
$$

Here $\delta(x,y,z)$ means mass per unit length at the point $(x,y,z)$. The small mass element is

$$
dm=\delta(x,y,z)\,ds.
$$

Moments and centroids of wires are obtained in the same way. If a wire has density $\delta(x,y,z)$, then its centroid coordinates are

$$
\bar x=\frac{1}{M}\int_{\mathcal C}x\,\delta(x,y,z)\,ds,
$$

$$
\bar y=\frac{1}{M}\int_{\mathcal C}y\,\delta(x,y,z)\,ds,
$$

$$
\bar z=\frac{1}{M}\int_{\mathcal C}z\,\delta(x,y,z)\,ds.
$$

In each formula, the numerator is a moment and $M$ is the total mass. The factor $x$, $y$, or $z$ measures how far the small mass element lies in the corresponding coordinate direction.

The value of a scalar line integral does not depend on the particular parametrization, as long as the parametrization traces the same curve once. However, the choice of parametrization can make the computation much easier or much harder. A good parametrization should fit the geometry of the curve. If the curve is a graph-like curve, it is often convenient to choose $t=x$ or $t=y$. If the curve is a circle or ellipse, trigonometric functions are usually natural. If the curve is given as the intersection of two surfaces, the first task is to use the surface equations to build a one-parameter description of the curve.

As a simple example, consider the line segment from $(1,1)$ to $(3,2)$ in the plane, and compute

$$
\int_{\mathcal C}(x^2+y^2)\,ds.
$$

A convenient parametrization is

$$
\mathbf r(t)=(1+2t)\mathbf i+(1+t)\mathbf j,\qquad 0\leq t\leq 1.
$$

At $t=0$, this gives the starting point $(1,1)$. At $t=1$, it gives the endpoint $(3,2)$. The derivative is

$$
\mathbf r'(t)=2\mathbf i+\mathbf j,
$$

so

$$
|\mathbf r'(t)|=\sqrt{2^2+1^2}=\sqrt5.
$$

Along the curve,

$$
x=1+2t,\qquad y=1+t.
$$

Therefore

$$
x^2+y^2=(1+2t)^2+(1+t)^2.
$$

The line integral becomes

$$
\int_{\mathcal C}(x^2+y^2)\,ds=\int_0^1\left((1+2t)^2+(1+t)^2\right)\sqrt5\,dt.
$$

Expanding,

$$
(1+2t)^2+(1+t)^2=2+6t+5t^2.
$$

Thus

$$
\int_{\mathcal C}(x^2+y^2)\,ds=\sqrt5\int_0^1(2+6t+5t^2)\,dt.
$$

Evaluating the integral gives

$$
\sqrt5\left[2t+3t^2+\frac53t^3\right]_0^1
=\sqrt5\left(2+3+\frac53\right)
=\frac{20\sqrt5}{3}.
$$

The important step was not the final integration. The important step was converting the curve integral into an ordinary integral using

$$
ds=|\mathbf r'(t)|dt.
$$

A common error is to forget the speed factor. For the scalar line integral

$$
\int_{\mathcal C}f\,ds,
$$

the expression $ds$ must become

$$
|\mathbf r'(t)|dt.
$$

It should not be replaced by $\mathbf r'(t)dt$, because $\mathbf r'(t)dt$ is a vector displacement, not a scalar length.

[Insert figure here: the conical helix $\mathbf r(t)=(t\cos t,t\sin t,t)$ for $0\leq t\leq2\pi$, showing that the radius from the $z$-axis grows as $t$ increases. This figure belongs here because the example tests the full scalar line-integral method: substitute into the scalar field and multiply by the speed.]

Consider the conical helix

$$
\mathbf r(t)=t\cos t\,\mathbf i+t\sin t\,\mathbf j+t\,\mathbf k,
\qquad 0\leq t\leq 2\pi.
$$

This curve winds once around the $z$-axis while moving upward and outward. Suppose first that we want the general scalar line integral

$$
\int_{\mathcal C}\phi\,ds,
$$

where $\phi(x,y,z)$ is a scalar field. Along the curve,

$$
x=t\cos t,\qquad y=t\sin t,\qquad z=t.
$$

Therefore the scalar field on the curve is

$$
\phi(\mathbf r(t))=\phi(t\cos t,t\sin t,t).
$$

Next we compute the speed. Differentiating the parametrization gives

$$
\mathbf r'(t)=(\cos t-t\sin t)\mathbf i+(\sin t+t\cos t)\mathbf j+\mathbf k.
$$

Hence

$$
|\mathbf r'(t)|=\sqrt{(\cos t-t\sin t)^2+(\sin t+t\cos t)^2+1}.
$$

The first two squared terms simplify:

$$
(\cos t-t\sin t)^2+(\sin t+t\cos t)^2=1+t^2.
$$

Therefore

$$
|\mathbf r'(t)|=\sqrt{t^2+2}.
$$

So the scalar line integral is

$$
\int_{\mathcal C}\phi\,ds=\int_0^{2\pi}\phi(t\cos t,t\sin t,t)\sqrt{t^2+2}\,dt.
$$

This is often the expected final setup if the scalar field $\phi$ is not specified further. If, for example, the scalar field is $f(x,y,z)=z$, then along the curve $z=t$, and the integral becomes

$$
\int_{\mathcal C}z\,ds=\int_0^{2\pi}t\sqrt{t^2+2}\,dt.
$$

Using $u=t^2+2$, so that $du=2t\,dt$, we obtain

$$
\int_0^{2\pi}t\sqrt{t^2+2}\,dt
=\frac12\int_2^{4\pi^2+2}u^{1/2}\,du
=\frac13\left[(t^2+2)^{3/2}\right]_0^{2\pi}.
$$

Thus

$$
\int_{\mathcal C}z\,ds=\frac{(4\pi^2+2)^{3/2}-2\sqrt2}{3}.
$$

This example shows the standard scalar-line-integral procedure in its most compact form: compute $\mathbf r'(t)$, compute $|\mathbf r'(t)|$, substitute the parametrization into the scalar field, and integrate.

[Insert figure here: the curve formed by the intersection of the elliptic cylinder $x^2+2y^2=1$ and the plane $z=y$, restricted to $y\geq0$. Show the curve running from $(-1,0,0)$ to $(1,0,0)$. This figure belongs here because it illustrates the common situation where the curve is not given directly as $\mathbf r(t)$, so the first task is to parametrize it.]

Now consider a curve described as the intersection of two surfaces:

$$
x^2+2y^2=1,
$$

$$
z=y,
$$

with $y\geq0$. This is a curve in three-dimensional space. Since it is given by two equations, it is not yet ready for a line integral. We first need a one-parameter parametrization.

The equation

$$
x^2+2y^2=1
$$

is an elliptic cylinder. A useful trigonometric parametrization is obtained by choosing

$$
x=-\cos t.
$$

Then

$$
x^2=\cos^2t.
$$

Substituting this into the cylinder equation gives

$$
\cos^2t+2y^2=1.
$$

Therefore

$$
2y^2=1-\cos^2t=\sin^2t.
$$

Since the curve is restricted to $y\geq0$, choose

$$
y=\frac{\sin t}{\sqrt2}.
$$

The condition $y\geq0$ is satisfied for

$$
0\leq t\leq \pi.
$$

The plane equation $z=y$ then gives

$$
z=\frac{\sin t}{\sqrt2}.
$$

Thus a parametrization of the curve is

$$
\mathbf r(t)=-\cos t\,\mathbf i+\frac{\sin t}{\sqrt2}\mathbf j+\frac{\sin t}{\sqrt2}\mathbf k,
\qquad 0\leq t\leq \pi.
$$

At $t=0$, this gives $(-1,0,0)$. At $t=\pi$, this gives $(1,0,0)$. Differentiating,

$$
\mathbf r'(t)=\sin t\,\mathbf i+\frac{\cos t}{\sqrt2}\mathbf j+\frac{\cos t}{\sqrt2}\mathbf k.
$$

The speed is

$$
|\mathbf r'(t)|
=\sqrt{\sin^2t+\frac{\cos^2t}{2}+\frac{\cos^2t}{2}}
=\sqrt{\sin^2t+\cos^2t}
=1.
$$

Therefore

$$
ds=dt.
$$

This is a very convenient parametrization because the speed is exactly $1$. The arc length of the curve is therefore

$$
\int_{\mathcal C}1\,ds=\int_0^\pi 1\,dt=\pi.
$$

More generally, for any scalar field $f(x,y,z)$,

$$
\int_{\mathcal C}f\,ds
=\int_0^\pi
f\left(-\cos t,\frac{\sin t}{\sqrt2},\frac{\sin t}{\sqrt2}\right)\,dt.
$$

This example illustrates a crucial method: when a curve is given as an intersection of surfaces, do not start integrating immediately. First turn the curve into a parametrized curve.

Another common intersection-curve situation occurs when a curve lies on both

$$
z=x^2
$$

and

$$
z=2-x^2-2y^2
$$

in the first octant. The first octant condition means

$$
x\geq0,\qquad y\geq0,\qquad z\geq0.
$$

If the curve runs from $(0,1,0)$ to $(1,0,1)$, then $x$ naturally runs from $0$ to $1$. Choose

$$
x=t,\qquad 0\leq t\leq1.
$$

From $z=x^2$, we get

$$
z=t^2.
$$

Substitute $x=t$ and $z=t^2$ into the second surface:

$$
t^2=2-t^2-2y^2.
$$

Then

$$
2y^2=2-2t^2,
$$

so

$$
y^2=1-t^2.
$$

Since the curve is in the first octant, $y\geq0$, and therefore

$$
y=\sqrt{1-t^2}.
$$

Thus

$$
\mathbf r(t)=t\,\mathbf i+\sqrt{1-t^2}\,\mathbf j+t^2\,\mathbf k,
\qquad 0\leq t\leq1.
$$

If a wire lies along this curve with density

$$
\delta(x,y,z)=xy,
$$

then its mass is

$$
M=\int_{\mathcal C}xy\,ds.
$$

Along the curve,

$$
xy=t\sqrt{1-t^2}.
$$

Also,

$$
\mathbf r'(t)=\mathbf i-\frac{t}{\sqrt{1-t^2}}\mathbf j+2t\,\mathbf k.
$$

Therefore

$$
|\mathbf r'(t)|=\sqrt{1+\frac{t^2}{1-t^2}+4t^2}.
$$

The mass becomes

$$
M=
\int_0^1
t\sqrt{1-t^2}
\sqrt{1+\frac{t^2}{1-t^2}+4t^2}\,dt.
$$

The integrand simplifies to

$$
t\sqrt{1+4t^2-4t^4},
$$

so

$$
M=\int_0^1t\sqrt{1+4t^2-4t^4}\,dt.
$$

The most important part of this example is the parametrization step. The density and the speed are evaluated only after the curve has been written as $\mathbf r(t)$.

Scalar line integrals are not oriented. This means that if a curve is traversed in the opposite direction, the value of

$$
\int_{\mathcal C} f\,ds
$$

does not change. The reason is that $ds$ is a positive length element. Reversing direction changes the sign of the velocity vector $\mathbf r'(t)$, but it does not change its length $|\mathbf r'(t)|$. Scalar line integrals measure accumulated amount along the curve, not signed motion along the curve.

This is the point where another kind of line integral enters. Scalar line integrals use $ds$, a scalar length element. Vector fields require a different object. Suppose $\mathbf F(x,y,z)$ is a vector field. At each point, $\mathbf F$ assigns a vector. If $\mathbf F$ is a force field and a particle moves along a curve, then only the part of the force pointing along the motion contributes to work. A force perpendicular to the motion may be large, but it does no work along that motion.

[Insert figure here: a curve $\mathcal C$, a tangent vector $\mathbf r'(t)$, a unit tangent vector $\mathbf T(t)$, and a force vector $\mathbf F(\mathbf r(t))$ making an angle with the tangent. Show that the tangential component is measured by a dot product. This figure belongs here because it explains why vector line integrals use $\mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r$.]

The displacement vector along the curve is

$$
d\mathbf r=\mathbf r'(t)\,dt.
$$

This is different from $ds$. The quantity $d\mathbf r$ has direction, while $ds$ is only length. For a vector field $\mathbf F$, the vector line integral along $\mathcal C$ is

$$
\int_{\mathcal C}\mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r.
$$

If the curve is parametrized by $\mathbf r(t)$, $a\leq t\leq b$, then

$$
\int_{\mathcal C}\mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r
=\int_a^b \mathbf F(\mathbf r(t))\cdot \mathbf r'(t)\,dt.
$$

Here $\mathbf F(\mathbf r(t))$ is the vector field evaluated on the curve, and $\mathbf r'(t)$ is the tangent velocity vector of the curve. The dot product selects the component of $\mathbf F$ in the direction of the curve.

This formula can also be understood from a Riemann sum. Divide the curve into small directed displacement vectors

$$
\Delta\mathbf r_i=\mathbf r(t_i)-\mathbf r(t_{i-1}).
$$

On the $i$-th small piece, the vector field is approximately $\mathbf F(\mathbf r(t_i^*))$. The contribution is approximately

$$
\mathbf F(\mathbf r(t_i^*))\cdot \Delta\mathbf r_i.
$$

Adding over all pieces gives

$$
\sum_i \mathbf F(\mathbf r(t_i^*))\cdot \Delta\mathbf r_i.
$$

As the pieces become smaller, this sum approaches

$$
\int_{\mathcal C}\mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r.
$$

This explains why vector line integrals use $d\mathbf r$, not $ds$. The vector displacement carries the direction of travel.

If

$$
\mathbf F(x,y,z)=F_1(x,y,z)\mathbf i+F_2(x,y,z)\mathbf j+F_3(x,y,z)\mathbf k,
$$

and

$$
\mathbf r(t)=x(t)\mathbf i+y(t)\mathbf j+z(t)\mathbf k,
$$

then

$$
\mathbf r'(t)=x'(t)\mathbf i+y'(t)\mathbf j+z'(t)\mathbf k.
$$

Thus

$$
\mathbf F(\mathbf r(t))\cdot \mathbf r'(t)
=F_1(\mathbf r(t))x'(t)
+F_2(\mathbf r(t))y'(t)
+F_3(\mathbf r(t))z'(t).
$$

So the vector line integral becomes

$$
\int_{\mathcal C}\mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r
=\int_a^b
\left[
F_1(\mathbf r(t))x'(t)
+F_2(\mathbf r(t))y'(t)
+F_3(\mathbf r(t))z'(t)
\right]dt.
$$

This is often written in differential notation as

$$
\int_{\mathcal C}F_1\,dx+F_2\,dy+F_3\,dz.
$$

This notation means

$$
dx=x'(t)\,dt,\qquad dy=y'(t)\,dt,\qquad dz=z'(t)\,dt.
$$

It does not mean that $dx$, $dy$, and $dz$ are chosen independently. They are all determined by the same parametrized curve.

The distinction between the two kinds of line integrals is essential:

$$
\int_{\mathcal C} f\,ds
=\int_a^b f(\mathbf r(t))|\mathbf r'(t)|\,dt
$$

is a scalar line integral, while

$$
\int_{\mathcal C}\mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r
=\int_a^b \mathbf F(\mathbf r(t))\cdot \mathbf r'(t)\,dt
$$

is a vector line integral. In the first formula, the curve contributes its speed $|\mathbf r'(t)|$. In the second formula, the curve contributes its velocity vector $\mathbf r'(t)$. Reversing the direction of the curve leaves the scalar line integral unchanged, but it changes the sign of the vector line integral.

For the earlier intersection curve

$$
\mathbf r(t)=-\cos t\,\mathbf i+\frac{\sin t}{\sqrt2}\mathbf j+\frac{\sin t}{\sqrt2}\mathbf k,
\qquad 0\leq t\leq \pi,
$$

the derivative is

$$
\mathbf r'(t)=\sin t\,\mathbf i+\frac{\cos t}{\sqrt2}\mathbf j+\frac{\cos t}{\sqrt2}\mathbf k.
$$

Therefore, for a vector field $\mathbf F$,

$$
\int_{\mathcal C}\mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r
=\int_0^\pi
\mathbf F\left(-\cos t,\frac{\sin t}{\sqrt2},\frac{\sin t}{\sqrt2}\right)
\cdot
\left(
\sin t\,\mathbf i
+\frac{\cos t}{\sqrt2}\mathbf j
+\frac{\cos t}{\sqrt2}\mathbf k
\right)
dt.
$$

Compare this with the scalar formula for the same curve:

$$
\int_{\mathcal C}f\,ds
=\int_0^\pi
f\left(-\cos t,\frac{\sin t}{\sqrt2},\frac{\sin t}{\sqrt2}\right)dt.
$$

The curve is the same, but the integrals use different curve factors. The scalar integral uses the speed, which here equals $1$. The vector integral uses the full tangent vector.

If a curve $\mathcal C$ is closed, meaning that it starts and ends at the same point, then a vector line integral around it is often written

$$
\oint_{\mathcal C}\mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r.
$$

The circle on the integral sign indicates that the curve is closed. Such an integral is called the circulation of $\mathbf F$ around $\mathcal C$. Circulation measures the net tendency of the vector field to push in the direction of travel around the closed curve.

[Insert figure here: the vector field $\mathbf F(x,y)=\frac{-y\mathbf i+x\mathbf j}{x^2+y^2}$, with arrows tangent to circles around the origin. Show a counterclockwise circle and a counterclockwise square surrounding the origin. This figure belongs here because it clarifies circulation, orientation, and the importance of singular points.]

Consider the vector field

$$
\mathbf F(x,y)=\frac{-y\mathbf i+x\mathbf j}{x^2+y^2}.
$$

This field is defined everywhere in the plane except at the origin, where the denominator $x^2+y^2$ is zero. The vectors circulate around the origin. We compute the circulation around the circle

$$
x^2+y^2=2
$$

traversed counterclockwise. A convenient parametrization is

$$
\mathbf r(\theta)=\sqrt2\cos\theta\,\mathbf i+\sqrt2\sin\theta\,\mathbf j,
\qquad -\frac{\pi}{4}\leq \theta\leq \frac{7\pi}{4}.
$$

This starts at $(1,-1)$ and makes one full counterclockwise revolution. The derivative is

$$
\mathbf r'(\theta)=-\sqrt2\sin\theta\,\mathbf i+\sqrt2\cos\theta\,\mathbf j.
$$

On the circle,

$$
x^2+y^2=2,
$$

so

$$
\mathbf F(\mathbf r(\theta))=\frac{-\sqrt2\sin\theta\,\mathbf i+\sqrt2\cos\theta\,\mathbf j}{2}.
$$

Therefore

$$
\mathbf F(\mathbf r(\theta))\cdot\mathbf r'(\theta)=1.
$$

Thus

$$
\oint_{\mathcal C}\mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r
=\int_{-\pi/4}^{7\pi/4}1\,d\theta
=2\pi.
$$

The same value is obtained if the circle is replaced by the square $[-1,1]\times[-1,1]$, oriented counterclockwise. The computation can be done by splitting the square into four line segments. Each side contributes $\pi/2$, so the total is again

$$
2\pi.
$$

The important point is not that every closed curve gives $2\pi$. The important point is that this vector field measures angular circulation around the origin. A closed curve going once counterclockwise around the origin gives total circulation $2\pi$. If the same curve were traversed clockwise, the answer would be $-2\pi$. Direction matters for vector line integrals.

This example also warns against a common mistake. Away from the origin, the field has a local structure that resembles a gradient of an angle function, but the origin is missing from the domain. The missing point matters. One cannot ignore points where the vector field is not defined, especially when a curve surrounds such a point.

The connection with conservative fields gives an important shortcut, but only when its conditions are satisfied. If a vector field $\mathbf F$ is the gradient of a scalar potential $\phi$, so that

$$
\mathbf F=\nabla\phi,
$$

then $\mathbf F$ is conservative. For a conservative field,

$$
\int_{\mathcal C}\mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r
=\phi(\mathbf r(b))-\phi(\mathbf r(a)).
$$

Here $\mathbf r(a)$ is the starting point of the curve and $\mathbf r(b)$ is the endpoint. This formula says that the vector line integral depends only on the endpoints, not on the path taken between them.

For example, let

$$
\mathbf F(x,y)=y^2\mathbf i+2xy\mathbf j.
$$

This field is the gradient of

$$
\phi(x,y)=xy^2,
$$

because

$$
\nabla\phi
=\frac{\partial \phi}{\partial x}\mathbf i
+\frac{\partial \phi}{\partial y}\mathbf j
=y^2\mathbf i+2xy\mathbf j.
$$

Therefore, for any path from $(0,0)$ to $(1,1)$,

$$
\int_{\mathcal C}\mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r
=\phi(1,1)-\phi(0,0)
=1\cdot1^2-0
=1.
$$

This shortcut should not replace the basic method. The basic method is always to parametrize the curve and compute

$$
\int_a^b \mathbf F(\mathbf r(t))\cdot\mathbf r'(t)\,dt.
$$

The conservative-field shortcut is available only after a valid potential has been identified on the relevant domain.

The practical method for line integrals can now be summarized in words. First determine which type of line integral is being asked. If the integral has the form

$$
\int_{\mathcal C}f\,ds,
$$

it is a scalar line integral, so the curve contributes the speed $|\mathbf r'(t)|$. If the integral has the form

$$
\int_{\mathcal C}\mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r
$$

or

$$
\int_{\mathcal C}P\,dx+Q\,dy+R\,dz,
$$

it is a vector line integral, so the curve contributes the tangent vector $\mathbf r'(t)$. Next, parametrize the curve with correct bounds. Then substitute the parametrization into the scalar or vector field. Finally, integrate over the parameter interval. If the curve is piecewise smooth, do this separately on each piece and add the results.

Line integrals extend integration to curved one-dimensional objects. The scalar line integral

$$
\int_{\mathcal C}f\,ds
$$

adds a scalar quantity along a curve and is used for length, mass, moments, and centroids of wires. The vector line integral

$$

adds the tangential contribution of a vector field along an oriented curve and is used for work and circulation. Both are reduced to ordinary one-variable integrals by parametrizing the curve. The central distinction is that $ds$ is a positive length element, while $d\mathbf r$ is a directed displacement. Remembering this distinction prevents most mistakes in this section.
