In elementary calculus, the derivative of a function of one variable is introduced as a rate of change. If a function $f(x)$ is differentiable at a point $x$, then for a small change $dx$, the corresponding change in the function is approximately

$$
df = f'(x)\,dx.
$$

At first glance, it appears that the derivative is simply the number $f'(x)$. However, a deeper interpretation is possible. The derivative is not merely a number; it is a rule that takes a small displacement $dx$ and returns the corresponding first-order change in the function. In one dimension, every linear transformation from $\mathbb{R}$ to $\mathbb{R}$ is multiplication by a number, which is why the distinction is often hidden.

The situation changes dramatically for functions of several variables. Consider a function

$$
f:\mathbb{R}^2\to\mathbb{R}.
$$

At a point $(a,b)$, there is no longer a single direction in which one can move. Instead, there are infinitely many possible directions. A small displacement is described by a vector

$$
h=(h_x,h_y).
$$

The central question becomes: how does the value of the function change when we move by this vector?

A first attempt might be to compute partial derivatives. The partial derivative with respect to $x$ measures the instantaneous change in the function when movement is restricted to the $x$-axis, while the partial derivative with respect to $y$ measures the instantaneous change when movement is restricted to the $y$-axis. At first this seems insufficient. After all, there are infinitely many directions besides the coordinate axes. Why should information from only two directions tell us anything about the others?

The answer is that partial derivatives alone are not enough. A function may possess all of its partial derivatives and yet fail to behave predictably in other directions. The missing ingredient is differentiability. Differentiability is a much stronger condition than the mere existence of partial derivatives. A function is differentiable at a point if there exists a linear transformation $L$ such that

$$
f(p+h)=f(p)+L(h)+\text{error},
$$

where the error becomes negligible compared to the size of $h$ as $h$ approaches zero. This definition states that, no matter which infinitesimal direction we choose, the function is approximated by the same linear transformation.

This is where the infinitely many possible paths reappear. The requirement is not that all paths give the same rate of change. Rather, the requirement is that one single linear rule successfully describes the behavior of the function along all infinitesimal paths simultaneously. The existence of such a rule is precisely what differentiability means.

This linear transformation is called the differential of the function and is denoted by $df$. The differential is therefore the true derivative in several variables. It is a machine that accepts a displacement vector and produces the corresponding first-order change in the function. If $v$ is a vector, then

$$
df(v)
$$

represents the change predicted by the best linear approximation.

Because the differential is linear, its behavior is completely determined by what it does to a basis. In $\mathbb{R}^2$, every vector can be written as a linear combination of the basis vectors

$$
e_1=(1,0), \qquad e_2=(0,1).
$$

Consequently, if we know $df(e_1)$ and $df(e_2)$, then linearity determines $df(v)$ for every possible vector $v$. The values of the differential on these basis vectors turn out to be precisely the partial derivatives:

$$
df(e_1)=\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}, \qquad
df(e_2)=\frac{\partial f}{\partial y}.
$$

Therefore, for any vector $v=(v_x,v_y)$,

$$
df(v)=\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}v_x+\frac{\partial f}{\partial y}v_y.
$$

The appearance of partial derivatives is therefore not arbitrary. They arise because they describe the action of the differential on a basis. Once differentiability guarantees that a single linear approximation exists, knowledge of the basis directions automatically determines every other direction.

A remarkable fact from linear algebra states that every linear transformation from $\mathbb{R}^n$ to $\mathbb{R}$ can be represented as a dot product with a unique vector. Consequently, there exists a unique vector $\nabla f$ such that

$$
df(v)=\nabla f\cdot v.
$$

This vector is called the gradient. The gradient is therefore not the derivative itself. Rather, it is the vector representation of the differential.

This perspective explains the famous geometric properties of the gradient. Suppose $u$ is a unit vector. The directional derivative in the direction $u$ is defined by

$$
D_u f = df(u).
$$

Using the gradient representation,

$$
D_u f = \nabla f\cdot u.
$$

Since $u$ has unit length, the dot product becomes

$$
D_u f = |\nabla f|\cos\theta,
$$

where $\theta$ is the angle between $u$ and the gradient. The largest possible value occurs when $\theta=0$, meaning that $u$ points in the same direction as the gradient. Thus the gradient points in the direction of greatest increase not because this was built into its definition, but because this follows naturally from the geometry of the dot product.

The relationship between gradients and level curves follows from the same reasoning. A level curve consists of points where the function value remains constant. If a particle moves along such a curve, its velocity vector is tangent to the curve. Since the function does not change along the motion, the differential applied to the velocity vector must be zero. Therefore,

$$
df(v)=0.
$$

Using the gradient representation,

$$
\nabla f\cdot v=0.
$$

A zero dot product means that the vectors are perpendicular. Consequently, the gradient is normal to every level curve.

The conceptual hierarchy is therefore the reverse of the one often presented in elementary calculus. One usually learns partial derivatives, then gradients, and finally differentials. From a modern viewpoint, the differential is the fundamental object. Differentiability guarantees the existence of a single linear approximation that works in every infinitesimal direction. The partial derivatives arise from evaluating this linear approximation on a basis. The gradient is the unique vector that represents the differential through the dot product. The familiar facts that the gradient points in the direction of steepest ascent and is perpendicular to level curves are not definitions but consequences of this deeper structure.
