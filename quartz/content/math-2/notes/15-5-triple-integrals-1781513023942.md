# 15.5 Triple Integrals

Double integrals allowed us to accumulate a quantity over a two-dimensional region. If $D\subset \mathbb{R}^2$ is a plane region, then an integral such as

$$
\iint_D f(x,y)\,dA
$$

adds the values of $f$ over many small area elements $dA$. This can represent area, volume under a surface, mass of a thin plate, or any other quantity distributed across a plane region. Triple integrals solve the same kind of accumulation problem, but now the region itself is three-dimensional. Instead of adding contributions from small rectangles in a plane, we add contributions from small boxes inside a solid.

This is the natural next step after double integrals. A double integral treats a two-dimensional domain as the base of the calculation. A triple integral treats a three-dimensional solid as the domain itself. This matters because many physical objects occupy volume: a metal part, a fluid region, a planet, an electric charge distribution in space, or any solid whose density may vary from point to point. If the quantity being accumulated depends on $x$, $y$, and $z$, then a double integral no longer has enough variables to describe the problem.

![pasted 1781514070239](/math-2/assets/pasted-1781514070239.png)

Suppose first that $B$ is a rectangular box in three-dimensional space:

$$
B=\{(x,y,z):x_0\leq x\leq x_1,\ y_0\leq y\leq y_1,\ z_0\leq z\leq z_1\}.
$$

Here $x_0,x_1,y_0,y_1,z_0,z_1$ are fixed numbers. The set $B$ contains all points whose $x$-coordinate lies between $x_0$ and $x_1$, whose $y$-coordinate lies between $y_0$ and $y_1$, and whose $z$-coordinate lies between $z_0$ and $z_1$. If $f(x,y,z)$ is a scalar function defined on this box, then $f$ assigns one number to every point in the box.

To form the triple integral, we divide the box into many small subboxes. A typical subbox has side lengths $\Delta x$, $\Delta y$, and $\Delta z$, so its volume is

$$
\Delta V=\Delta x\,\Delta y\,\Delta z.
$$

If the subbox is very small, then $f(x,y,z)$ is approximately constant inside it. The contribution from that subbox is therefore approximately

$$
f(x,y,z)\,\Delta V.
$$

Adding these contributions over all subboxes gives a finite approximation. Taking the limit as the subboxes become arbitrarily small gives the triple integral

$$
\iiint_B f(x,y,z)\,dV.
$$

The symbol $dV$ is called the volume element. In Cartesian coordinates,

$$
dV=dx\,dy\,dz.
$$

This means that an infinitesimal volume element is built from an infinitesimal change in $x$, an infinitesimal change in $y$, and an infinitesimal change in $z$. The notation $dx\,dy\,dz$ is the limiting version of $\Delta x\,\Delta y\,\Delta z$. It represents the volume of a very small Cartesian box.

This interpretation is also useful computationally. A computer approximation of a solid often divides space into tiny boxes, or voxels. If one voxel lies inside the solid, its contribution is counted; if it lies outside, it is ignored. Triple integration is the exact mathematical limit of this idea: the boxes become infinitely small, and the sum becomes an integral.

Triple integrals are not restricted to rectangular boxes. If $D\subset\mathbb{R}^3$ is a bounded solid region, then

$$
\iiint_D f(x,y,z)\,dV
$$

means that $f$ is accumulated only over points inside $D$. Formally, one may imagine placing $D$ inside a large rectangular box and extending $f$ to be zero outside $D$. Practically, the important task is to describe the solid $D$ using inequalities, because those inequalities become the limits of integration.

The simplest and most important special case is $f(x,y,z)=1$. Then every small volume element contributes only its own volume. Therefore,

$$
\operatorname{Volume}(D)=\iiint_D 1\,dV=\iiint_D dV.
$$

Here $D$ is the solid region whose volume is being computed. This formula says that the volume of a solid is obtained by adding all the infinitesimal volume elements inside it. It is the three-dimensional analogue of

$$
\operatorname{Area}(D)=\iint_D dA
$$

for a plane region.

A second fundamental interpretation is mass. A mass density is mass per unit volume. If $\rho(x,y,z)$ is the density of a solid at the point $(x,y,z)$, then a small volume element $dV$ near that point contains approximately

$$
dm=\rho(x,y,z)\,dV
$$

units of mass. Here $dm$ means a small mass element. Adding all these small mass elements throughout the solid gives the total mass:

$$
m=\iiint_D \rho(x,y,z)\,dV.
$$

In this formula, $m$ is the total mass, $D$ is the region occupied by the solid, $\rho(x,y,z)$ is the density, and $dV$ is the volume element. If the density is constant, say $\rho(x,y,z)=\rho_0$, then

$$
m=\rho_0\iiint_D dV=\rho_0\operatorname{Volume}(D).
$$

So volume is the special case of mass where the density is equal to $1$.

Triple integrals have the same basic algebraic properties as double integrals. They are linear:

$$
\iiint_D \bigl(\alpha f+\beta g\bigr)\,dV
=
\alpha\iiint_D f\,dV+\beta\iiint_D g\,dV,
$$

where $\alpha$ and $\beta$ are constants, and $f$ and $g$ are integrable functions on $D$. They are additive over regions: if a solid is split into finitely many non-overlapping pieces, then the integral over the whole solid is the sum of the integrals over the pieces. They preserve positivity and order: if $f\geq 0$ on $D$, then

$$
\iiint_D f\,dV\geq 0,
$$

and if $f\leq g$ on $D$, then

$$
\iiint_D f\,dV\leq \iiint_D g\,dV.
$$

They also satisfy a triangle inequality:

$$
\left|\iiint_D f\,dV\right|
\leq
\iiint_D |f|\,dV.
$$

This inequality says that cancellation can make an integral smaller, but it cannot make the absolute value of the total larger than the integral of the absolute values. In applications, these properties justify splitting complicated solids into simpler pieces, pulling out constants, and using symmetry to simplify calculations.

Symmetry is one of the most useful shortcuts. Suppose $D$ is symmetric with respect to the plane $x=0$. This means that whenever $(x,y,z)$ is in $D$, the reflected point $(-x,y,z)$ is also in $D$. If $f$ is odd in $x$, meaning

$$
f(-x,y,z)=-f(x,y,z),
$$

then the contributions from reflected volume elements cancel. Hence

$$
\iiint_D f(x,y,z)\,dV=0.
$$

The same reasoning applies to symmetry in $y$ or $z$. For example, over a ball centered at the origin, the integrals of $x$, $y$, $z$, or $\sin z$ are zero, because the ball is symmetric about the coordinate planes and these functions are odd in one coordinate. However, symmetry can only be used when both requirements are satisfied: the region must be symmetric, and the integrand must change sign under the corresponding reflection.

For instance, over the ball

$$
x^2+y^2+z^2\leq a^2,
$$

the integral

$$
\iiint_{x^2+y^2+z^2\leq a^2}(2+x-\sin z)\,dV
$$

can be evaluated almost without calculation. The integral of $x$ is zero by symmetry in $x$, and the integral of $\sin z$ is zero by symmetry in $z$, since $\sin z$ is odd. Only the constant $2$ remains. The volume of a ball of radius $a$ is $\frac{4}{3}\pi a^3$, so the integral is

$$
2\cdot \frac{4}{3}\pi a^3=\frac{8}{3}\pi a^3.
$$

The region determines where the integration takes place; the integrand determines what is being accumulated there.

To actually compute a triple integral, we usually write it as an iterated integral. An iterated integral is a sequence of ordinary one-variable integrals. The innermost integral is evaluated first, then the next one, and finally the outermost one.

Suppose a solid region $D$ can be described in the form

$$
D=\{(x,y,z):a\leq x\leq b,\ c(x)\leq y\leq d(x),\ e(x,y)\leq z\leq g(x,y)\}.
$$

This description should be read in stages. First, $x$ runs from the constant lower bound $a$ to the constant upper bound $b$. Once $x$ has been fixed, $y$ runs from $c(x)$ to $d(x)$. Once both $x$ and $y$ have been fixed, $z$ runs from $e(x,y)$ to $g(x,y)$. Therefore,

$$
\iiint_D f(x,y,z)\,dV
=
\int_a^b\int_{c(x)}^{d(x)}\int_{e(x,y)}^{g(x,y)}
f(x,y,z)\,dz\,dy\,dx.
$$

The order $dz\,dy\,dx$ matters. It means that $z$ is integrated first, then $y$, then $x$. During the $z$-integration, both $x$ and $y$ are treated as constants. During the $y$-integration, $x$ is treated as constant. Finally, $x$ is integrated.

A common mistake is to write bounds that depend on variables that have not yet been fixed. In the order

$$
dz\,dy\,dx,
$$

the $x$-bounds are outermost, so they must be constants. The $y$-bounds may depend on $x$, because $x$ has already been fixed. The $z$-bounds may depend on both $x$ and $y$, because both have already been fixed before integrating with respect to $z$. If the order of integration is changed, the allowed dependency of the bounds also changes.

![pasted 1781513959446](/math-2/assets/pasted-1781513959446.png)

For a rectangular box,

$$
B=\{(x,y,z):0\leq x\leq a,\ 0\leq y\leq b,\ 0\leq z\leq c\},
$$

all bounds are constants. For example,

$$
\iiint_B (xy^2+z^3)\,dV
=
\int_0^c\int_0^b\int_0^a (xy^2+z^3)\,dx\,dy\,dz.
$$

The order $dx\,dy\,dz$ means that $x$ is integrated first. Since the region is a box, any order of integration is possible with constant bounds. General solids are harder because the surfaces bounding the solid usually force some bounds to depend on other variables.

The central geometric skill in this section is projection. When a solid is described by surfaces, one often chooses a direction of integration and projects the solid onto a coordinate plane. If the integration is first with respect to $z$, then we look along vertical lines parallel to the $z$-axis and project the solid onto the $xy$-plane. The projected region tells us the possible values of $x$ and $y$, while the lower and upper surfaces tell us the possible values of $z$.

![pasted 1781513982213](/math-2/assets/pasted-1781513982213.png)

Consider the tetrahedron $T$ with vertices

$$
(0,0,0),\qquad (1,0,0),\qquad (0,1,0),\qquad (0,0,1).
$$

This solid is bounded by the three coordinate planes

$$
x=0,\qquad y=0,\qquad z=0
$$

and by the plane

$$
x+y+z=1.
$$

The coordinate planes force the solid to lie in the first octant, where

$$
x\geq 0,\qquad y\geq 0,\qquad z\geq 0.
$$

The slanted plane forms the fourth face of the tetrahedron. Solving the plane equation for $z$ gives

$$
z=1-x-y.
$$

To integrate first with respect to $z$, we project the tetrahedron onto the $xy$-plane. The projection is the triangle bounded by

$$
x=0,\qquad y=0,\qquad x+y=1.
$$

Thus $x$ runs from $0$ to $1$, and for a fixed $x$, $y$ runs from $0$ to $1-x$. For a fixed point $(x,y)$ in this triangular projection, $z$ runs from the bottom plane $z=0$ to the top plane $z=1-x-y$. Therefore the volume is

$$
V=
\int_0^1\int_0^{1-x}\int_0^{1-x-y}1\,dz\,dy\,dx.
$$

The integrand is $1$ because we are computing volume. Evaluating the innermost integral gives

$$
\int_0^{1-x-y}1\,dz=1-x-y.
$$

So

$$
V=
\int_0^1\int_0^{1-x}(1-x-y)\,dy\,dx.
$$

Now,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\int_0^{1-x}(1-x-y)\,dy
&=\left[(1-x)y-\frac{1}{2}y^2\right]_0^{1-x} \\
&=\frac{1}{2}(1-x)^2.
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore,

$$
\begin{aligned}
V
&=\int_0^1\frac{1}{2}(1-x)^2\,dx \\
&=\frac{1}{2}\left[-\frac{(1-x)^3}{3}\right]_0^1 \\
&=\frac{1}{6}.
\end{aligned}
$$

The important part of this example is not the final number. The important part is the method: choose a direction, project the solid onto a coordinate plane, determine the base region, and then find lower and upper bounds in the chosen direction.

This also explains the relation between triple integrals and double integrals. Suppose a solid lies above a base region $D$ in the $xy$-plane, with lower surface

$$
z=\ell(x,y)
$$

and upper surface

$$
z=u(x,y).
$$

Then the volume can be written as

$$
V=\iiint_{\text{solid}}1\,dV
=
\iint_D\int_{\ell(x,y)}^{u(x,y)}1\,dz\,dA.
$$

The inner integral gives

$$
\int_{\ell(x,y)}^{u(x,y)}1\,dz
=
u(x,y)-\ell(x,y),
$$

so

$$
V=\iint_D \bigl(u(x,y)-\ell(x,y)\bigr)\,dA.
$$

The expression $u(x,y)-\ell(x,y)$ is the height of the solid above the point $(x,y)$. This is why some volume problems can be solved either as triple integrals or as double integrals of “top minus bottom.” These are not different concepts; the double integral appears after the vertical part of the triple integral has already been evaluated.

The same tetrahedron can be used to show the difference between computing volume and integrating a nonconstant function. Consider

$$
\iiint_T y\,dV.
$$

Using the same bounds as before,

$$
\iiint_T y\,dV
=
\int_0^1\int_0^{1-x}\int_0^{1-x-y}y\,dz\,dy\,dx.
$$

During the $z$-integration, $y$ is constant, so

$$
\int_0^{1-x-y}y\,dz
=
y(1-x-y).
$$

Thus,

$$
\iiint_T y\,dV
=
\int_0^1\int_0^{1-x}y(1-x-y)\,dy\,dx.
$$

Then

$$
\begin{aligned}
\int_0^{1-x}y(1-x-y)\,dy
&=\int_0^{1-x}\bigl((1-x)y-y^2\bigr)\,dy \\
&=\frac{1}{6}(1-x)^3,
\end{aligned}
$$

and hence

$$
\begin{aligned}
\iiint_T y\,dV
&=\int_0^1\frac{1}{6}(1-x)^3\,dx \\
&=\frac{1}{24}.
\end{aligned}
$$

This integral is not a volume, because the integrand is $y$, not $1$. Each small volume element is weighted by its $y$-coordinate before being added. In every triple integral, the bounds describe where we integrate, while the integrand describes what is being accumulated.

A typical solid is often described as the region between two surfaces. Consider the region lying below the plane

$$
z=3-2y
$$

and above the paraboloid

$$
z=x^2+y^2.
$$

The lower surface is $z=x^2+y^2$, and the upper surface is $z=3-2y$. The solid exists only where the upper surface is at least as high as the lower surface:

$$
x^2+y^2\leq 3-2y.
$$

Rearranging,

$$
x^2+y^2+2y\leq 3.
$$

Completing the square in $y$,

$$
x^2+(y+1)^2\leq 4.
$$

So the projection onto the $xy$-plane is the disk of radius $2$ centered at $(0,-1)$. The volume is

$$
V=
\iint_D\int_{x^2+y^2}^{3-2y}1\,dz\,dA,
$$

where

$$
D=\{(x,y):x^2+(y+1)^2\leq 4\}.
$$

After integrating with respect to $z$,

$$
V=
\iint_D \bigl(3-2y-x^2-y^2\bigr)\,dA.
$$

This example shows a key warning: the $z$-bounds come from the lower and upper surfaces, but the $(x,y)$-bounds come from the projection. The projection is found by solving the intersection condition between the surfaces. It is not enough to identify the top and bottom surfaces; one must also determine where those surfaces enclose a solid.

Since the projected region is a shifted disk, it is convenient to use polar coordinates in the projection. Set

$$
x=r\cos\theta,\qquad y=-1+r\sin\theta.
$$

Then the disk is described by

$$
0\leq r\leq 2,\qquad 0\leq \theta\leq 2\pi.
$$

In these coordinates,

$$
3-2y-x^2-y^2=4-r^2.
$$

The area element in the projected $xy$-plane is

$$
dA=r\,dr\,d\theta.
$$

Therefore,

$$
V=
\int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^2(4-r^2)r\,dr\,d\theta.
$$

Evaluating,

$$
\begin{aligned}
V
&=\int_0^{2\pi}
\left[2r^2-\frac{r^4}{4}\right]_0^2
\,d\theta \\
&=\int_0^{2\pi}4\,d\theta \\
&=8\pi.
\end{aligned}
$$

This calculation uses polar coordinates only for the two-dimensional projection. The full three-dimensional change-of-variables formula, including cylindrical and spherical coordinates, is treated separately. At this stage, the essential point is that projecting a solid can reduce the setup of a triple integral to a familiar two-dimensional domain problem.

A common construction begins with a plane region and then builds a solid above it. Suppose $R\subset\mathbb{R}^2$ is bounded below by the parabola

$$
y=x^2
$$

and above by the line

$$
y=x+2.
$$

The intersection points satisfy

$$
x^2=x+2,
$$

so

$$
x^2-x-2=0,
\qquad
(x-2)(x+1)=0.
$$

Thus $x=-1$ and $x=2$. The plane region is

$$
R=\{(x,y):-1\leq x\leq 2,\ x^2\leq y\leq x+2\}.
$$

Now suppose a solid lies above this base region, above the plane $z=0$, and below the plane

$$
y+z=4.
$$

The condition $z\geq 0$ gives the lower bound $z=0$. The plane equation $y+z=4$ gives the upper bound

$$
z=4-y.
$$

Therefore the solid is

$$
V=\{(x,y,z):-1\leq x\leq 2,\ x^2\leq y\leq x+2,\ 0\leq z\leq 4-y\}.
$$

For example,

$$
\iiint_V x\,dV
=
\int_{-1}^{2}\int_{x^2}^{x+2}\int_0^{4-y}x\,dz\,dy\,dx.
$$

Here the base region supplies the $x$- and $y$-bounds, while the vertical height of the solid supplies the $z$-bounds. This is often the most efficient way to set up triple integrals: first solve the two-dimensional projection problem, then add the third direction.

Sometimes the order of integration is inconvenient. The integral may already be given, but the innermost antiderivative may be difficult or impossible in that order. The solution is to describe the same solid using a different order of integration. This is called changing the order of integration.

Consider

$$
I=
\int_0^1\int_x^1\int_x^y f(x,y,z)\,dz\,dy\,dx.
$$

The original bounds mean

$$
0\leq x\leq 1,
$$

then, once $x$ is fixed,

$$
x\leq y\leq 1,
$$

and once $x$ and $y$ are fixed,

$$
x\leq z\leq y.
$$

Together, these inequalities describe the solid region. Suppose we want the order $dx\,dy\,dz$. That means $x$ will be integrated first, $y$ second, and $z$ last. Therefore $z$ must be the outermost variable.

From the inequalities $x\leq z\leq y$, together with $0\leq x\leq 1$ and $y\leq 1$, the possible values of $z$ are

$$
0\leq z\leq 1.
$$

For a fixed $z$, the inequality $z\leq y\leq 1$ gives

$$
z\leq y\leq 1.
$$

Finally, for fixed $z$ and $y$, the variable $x$ must satisfy

$$
0\leq x,\qquad x\leq z,\qquad x\leq y.
$$

Because $z\leq y$, the stricter upper bound is $x\leq z$. Thus,

$$
0\leq x\leq z.
$$

Therefore,

$$
I=
\int_0^1\int_z^1\int_0^z f(x,y,z)\,dx\,dy\,dz.
$$

The function $f(x,y,z)$ has not changed. The solid has not changed. Only the order in which the same solid is swept out has changed. This distinction is essential: when changing order, one must preserve the region and the integrand while rewriting the bounds.

Average value is another direct application of triple integration. If $f(x,y,z)$ is a scalar quantity defined throughout a solid $D$, its average value over $D$ is

$$
f_{\mathrm{avg}}
=
\frac{1}{\operatorname{Volume}(D)}
\iiint_D f(x,y,z)\,dV.
$$

The numerator accumulates the total amount of $f$ over the solid. The denominator divides by the volume of the solid. This is the same idea as an ordinary average: total amount divided by total size. For a plane region, the corresponding denominator is area; for a solid region, it is volume.

Centre of mass and moment of inertia are also built from triple integrals, but they are applications rather than new integration methods. If a solid $D$ has density $\rho(x,y,z)$, its total mass is

$$
m=\iiint_D \rho(x,y,z)\,dV.
$$

The $x$-coordinate of the centre of mass is

$$
\bar{x}
=
\frac{1}{m}\iiint_D x\,\rho(x,y,z)\,dV.
$$

The formulas for $\bar{y}$ and $\bar{z}$ are obtained by replacing $x$ with $y$ and $z$. The moment of inertia about the $z$-axis is

$$
I_z=
\iiint_D (x^2+y^2)\rho(x,y,z)\,dV.
$$

The factor $x^2+y^2$ is the squared distance from the point $(x,y,z)$ to the $z$-axis. These formulas all have the same structure: choose the correct physical quantity to accumulate, multiply by density if mass is involved, and integrate over the solid.

The main difficulty in triple integrals is usually not the final antiderivative. The main difficulty is setting up the region correctly. If integrating first with respect to $z$, find the projection onto the $xy$-plane and then determine the lower and upper $z$-surfaces. If integrating first with respect to $x$, project onto the $yz$-plane and find the left and right $x$-surfaces. If integrating first with respect to $y$, project onto the $xz$-plane and find the corresponding lower and upper $y$-surfaces.

Coordinate planes must also be interpreted carefully. The coordinate planes are

$$
x=0,\qquad y=0,\qquad z=0.
$$

A solid bounded by the coordinate planes and another surface often lies in the first octant, so the inequalities

$$
x\geq 0,\qquad y\geq 0,\qquad z\geq 0
$$

must be included. For example, the tetrahedron bounded by the coordinate planes and the plane $x+y+z=1$ is not described only by $x+y+z\leq 1$. It is described by

$$
x\geq 0,\qquad y\geq 0,\qquad z\geq 0,\qquad x+y+z\leq 1.
$$

Forgetting the coordinate-plane inequalities changes the region completely.

Some solids are much easier to describe in cylindrical or spherical coordinates. Spheres, cylinders, cones, and radially symmetric density functions often suggest such coordinates. However, the full rule for changing variables in triple integrals belongs to the next section. In this section, the focus is Cartesian triple integration: understanding $dV$, setting up bounds, projecting solids onto coordinate planes, computing volumes and masses, using symmetry, and changing the order of integration when necessary.

The core idea is simple but powerful. A triple integral adds contributions from infinitely many tiny volume elements inside a solid. If the integrand is $1$, the result is volume. If the integrand is density, the result is mass. If the integrand is a coordinate, a distance factor, or another physical quantity times density, the result becomes a weighted total such as a centre-of-mass numerator or a moment of inertia. Computationally, everything depends on describing the solid correctly. Once the bounds match the geometry, the triple integral is evaluated from the inside out, just like repeated one-variable integration.
