# 1.351 Microscopic Dipoles and Macroscopic Polarization

Source: [[engineering-electromagnetics-9th-ed-9nbsped-compress|Engineering Electromagnetics, Ninth Edition: Learning Spine and Vector Analysis Foundations]]

Locations: Page 583, Section: Origins of the Complex Permittivity

A dielectric is modeled as atoms or molecules whose positive and negative bound charges can be separated by an applied electric field. Each separated charge pair forms a microscopic electric dipole. The molecules may be arranged regularly, as in a crystal, or randomly, as in an amorphous material or liquid. They may also possess permanent dipole moments, although those moments normally have random orientations before a field is applied. The macroscopic polarization $\mathbf{P}$ is defined as the vector dipole moment per unit volume. In a differential volume $\Delta v$ containing $N\Delta v$ dipoles, it is the limiting volume density of the sum of the individual moments:
$$
\mathbf{P}=\lim_{\Delta v\to 0}\frac{1}{\Delta v}\sum_{i=1}^{N\Delta v}\mathbf{p}_i
$$
 The moment of dipole $i$ is
$$
\mathbf{p}_i=Q_i\mathbf{d}_i
$$
 where $Q_i$ is the magnitude of the positive bound charge and $\mathbf{d}_i$ points from the negative charge to the positive charge. This microscopic-to-macroscopic construction is the foundation for both resonant polarization and orientational relaxation.

### Origins of the Complex Permittivity

As we learned in Chapter 5, a dielectric can be modeled as an arrangement of atoms and molecules in free space, which can be polarized by an electric field. The field forces positive and negative bound charges to separate against their Coulomb attractive forces, thus producing an array of microscopic dipoles. The molecules can be arranged in an ordered and predictable manner (such as in a crystal) or may exhibit random positioning and orientation, as would occur in an amorphous material or a liquid. The molecules may or may not exhibit permanent dipole moments (existing before the field is applied), and if they do, they will usually have random orientations throughout the material volume. ## Core Ideas

- An electric field separates positive and negative bound charges against their Coulomb attraction.
- A microscopic dipole moment is $\mathbf{p}_i=Q_i\mathbf{d}_i$.
- $\mathbf{d}_i$ points from the negative bound charge to the positive bound charge.
- $N$ is the number of dipoles per unit volume.
- Macroscopic polarization is the dipole moment per unit volume.
- Permanent dipoles may exist before a field is applied but ordinarily have random orientations.
