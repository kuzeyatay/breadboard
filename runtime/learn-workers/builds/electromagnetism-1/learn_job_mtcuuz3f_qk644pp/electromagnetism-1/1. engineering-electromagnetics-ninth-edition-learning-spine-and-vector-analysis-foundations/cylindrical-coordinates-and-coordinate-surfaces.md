# 1.19 Cylindrical Coordinates and Coordinate Surfaces

Source: [[engineering-electromagnetics-9th-ed-9nbsped-compress|Engineering Electromagnetics, Ninth Edition: Learning Spine and Vector Analysis Foundations]]

Locations: Page 26, Section: 1.8 Other Coordinate Systems: Circular Cylindrical Coordinates, Section: 1.8.1 Point Coordinates, Page 27, Figure 1.6a

Circular cylindrical coordinates adapt three-dimensional geometry to systems with rotational symmetry around an axis. A point is specified by $P(\rho,\phi,z)$, where $\rho$ is the nonnegative distance from the $z$ axis, $\phi$ is the azimuthal angle measured from a chosen zero direction, and $z$ is the same axial coordinate used in rectangular coordinates. Rather than interpreting coordinates only as distances along fixed axes, the source locates a point as the intersection of three mutually perpendicular constant-coordinate surfaces. These are a circular cylinder $\rho=\text{constant}$, a radial plane $\phi=\text{constant}$, and a horizontal plane $z=\text{constant}$. This surface-based interpretation is the foundation for defining local unit vectors and differential elements. Figure 1.6a is source-central because it displays these intersecting surfaces and makes clear why cylindrical coordinates simplify problems possessing cylindrical symmetry.

#### 1.8 OTHER COORDINATE SYSTEMS: CIRCULAR CYLINDRICAL COORDINATES

The rectangular coordinate system is generally the one in which students prefer to work every problem. This often means a lot more work, because many problems possess a type of symmetry that pleads for a more logical treatment. It is easier to do now, once and for all, the work required to become familiar with cylindrical and spherical coordinates, instead of applying an equal or greater effort to every problem involving cylindrical or spherical symmetry later. With this in mind, we will take a careful and unhurried look at cylindrical and spherical coordinates.

##### 1.8.1 Point Coordinates

The circular cylindrical coordinate system is the three-dimensional version of the polar coordinates of analytic geometry. ## Core Ideas

- Cylindrical coordinates are the three-dimensional extension of plane polar coordinates.
- $\rho$ measures distance from the $z$ axis and is nonnegative.
- $\phi$ specifies rotation around the $z$ axis.
- $z$ measures distance from the reference plane $z=0$.
- The surface $\rho=\text{constant}$ is a circular cylinder.
- The surface $\phi=\text{constant}$ is a radial plane.
- The surface $z=\text{constant}$ is a horizontal plane.
- A generic point is the intersection of the three constant-coordinate surfaces.

## Source Anchors

- The source motivates cylindrical coordinates as a way to exploit cylindrical symmetry instead of forcing every problem into rectangular coordinates.
- A cylindrical point requires the polar coordinates $\rho$ and $\phi$ plus the axial coordinate $z$.
- The three locating surfaces are explicitly identified as a cylinder and two planes.
- Figure 1.6a shows the three mutually perpendicular surfaces.
- The text notes a coordinate singularity on the $z$ axis, where the azimuthal plane is not uniquely determined.
