# 1.50 Faraday Displacement Flux

Source: [[engineering-electromagnetics-9th-ed-9nbsped-compress|Engineering Electromagnetics, Ninth Edition: Learning Spine and Vector Analysis Foundations]]

Locations: Page 60, Page 61, Section: Electric Flux Density, Gauss's Law, and Divergence, Section: 3.1 Electric Flux Density, Section: 3.1.1 Faraday's Experiments on Electric Displacement

The source gives electric-field streamlines a physical accounting interpretation by introducing electric flux. Electric flux is not a stream of material particles; it is a conceptual quantity aligned with field streamlines and associated directly with source charge. Faraday's concentric-sphere experiment placed known positive charge on an inner conductor, surrounded it with dielectric, grounded the outer conductor, and measured the induced negative charge on the outer hemispheres. The induced charge always equaled the inner charge in magnitude, regardless of the dielectric filling the gap. This led to the idea that a medium-independent displacement flux extends from the inner charge to the outer conductor. In SI units, the proportionality constant between total electric flux $\Psi$ and enclosed charge $Q$ is one, so $\Psi=Q$, with both measured in coulombs.

### Electric Flux Density, Gauss's Law, and Divergence

After drawing the fields described in the previous chapter and becoming familiar with the concept of the streamlines that show the direction of the force on a test charge at every point, it is appropriate to give these lines a physical significance and to think of them as flux lines. No physical particle is projected radially outward from the point charge, and there are no steel tentacles reaching out to attract or repel an unwary test charge, but as soon as the streamlines are drawn on paper there seems to be a picture showing "something" is present.

It is very helpful to invent an electric flux that streams away symmetrically from a point charge and is coincident with the streamlines and to visualize this flux wherever an electric field is present.

This chapter introduces and uses the concept of electric flux and electric flux density to again solve several of the problems presented in Chapter 2. The work here turns out to be much easier for problems that possess a high degree of spatial symmetry. ■

#### 3.1.1 Faraday's Experiments on Electric Displacement

About 1837, the director of the Royal Society in London, Michael Faraday, became very interested in static electric fields and the effect of various insulating materials on these fields. This problem had been bothering him during the previous ten years when he was experimenting in his now-famous work on induced electromotive force, which we will discuss in Chapter 9. With that subject completed, he had a pair of concentric metallic spheres constructed, the outer one consisting of two hemispheres that could be firmly clamped together. He also prepared shells of insulating material (or dielectric material, or simply dielectric) that would occupy the entire volume between the concentric spheres. His experiment, then, consisted essentially of the following steps:

1. With the equipment dismantled, the inner sphere was given a known positive charge.

2. The hemispheres were then clamped together around the charged sphere with about 2 cm of dielectric material between them.

## Core Ideas

- Electric flux gives field streamlines a charge-accounting interpretation.
- Flux is conceptual and does not represent emitted physical particles.
- Faraday used charged concentric conductors separated by dielectric material.
- The induced outer charge equaled the inner charge in magnitude.
- The measured total displacement was independent of the dielectric material.
- In SI units, total flux satisfies $\Psi=Q$.

## Source Anchors

- The chapter introduction describes flux lines as coincident with electric-field streamlines.
- Faraday's procedure charged the inner sphere, enclosed it with dielectric and outer hemispheres, grounded the outer sphere, then measured induced charge.
- The total induced outer charge was equal and opposite to the original inner-sphere charge.
- Changing the dielectric did not change the total induced charge.
- The source writes
$$
\Psi=Q
$$
 and states that electric flux is measured in coulombs.
- The experiment is dated to about 1837 and attributed to Michael Faraday.
