## Fields as Spatial Vector Quantities

Imagine placing a small probe at several locations around a charged particle. At one location the probe responds strongly in one direction. Farther away, its response has a different strength, and moving around the particle changes its direction. A single number cannot describe both features, and a single arrow cannot describe every location. We need an assignment that gives one directed quantity to each point in space.

An electromagnetic field provides that assignment. The directed quantity at one chosen position is a **vector**, represented by an arrow whose orientation gives direction and whose length represents magnitude. When every position has its own vector, the complete assignment is called a **vector field**. Position and field value play separate roles: the coordinates identify where we evaluate the field, while the vector tells us what the field is there.

![Three-dimensional electromagnetic vector field surrounding a central charged particle](/electromagnetism-1/assets/source-visuals/engineering-electromagnetics-9th-ed-9nbsped-compress-page-1-diagram-f1-three-dimensional-electromagnetic-vector-field-s.png)

*Three-dimensional electromagnetic vector field surrounding a central charged particle* *(p. 1)*

Each arrow in the diagram belongs to a particular location around the central particle. Following the arrows from one location to another reveals a continuous change in orientation, and their lengths can change as well. One arrow gives one local field value. The full collection of spatially located arrows gives the field. This distinction becomes essential whenever the same physical influence produces different effects at different positions.

We can describe a position using Cartesian coordinates $(x,y,z)$. A field value at that position can then be written through three components, one along each coordinate direction. For a vector $\mathbf{B}$, the quantities $B_x$, $B_y$, and $B_z$ are its Cartesian component values. They tell us how much of the vector points along the $x$, $y$, and $z$ axes. Because these axes are mutually perpendicular, the three components form the edges of a right-angled geometric construction.

The arrow's scalar length is called its **vector magnitude**. The three-dimensional form of the Pythagorean relation gives that length:
$$
\lvert \mathbf{B} \rvert = \sqrt{B_x^2 + B_y^2 + B_z^2} \qquad (1)
$$
Here, $\mathbf{B}$ is the vector field value at the chosen position. The symbols $B_x$, $B_y$, and $B_z$ are its Cartesian component values. The expression $\lvert \mathbf{B} \rvert$ is the vector magnitude, a nonnegative scalar that measures the arrow's length. The square root of the component-square sum combines three perpendicular contributions into that single length. Squaring removes the signs of the components because a component pointing in a negative coordinate direction still contributes positively to the total length.

For a field confined to the $x$-$y$ plane, $B_z$ equals zero. Equation (1) then reduces to the familiar diagonal length formed from the $x$ and $y$ components. A vector can therefore point partly in several coordinate directions while still having one well-defined magnitude. Its components provide a convenient computational description, and the arrow provides the matching geometric picture.

Fields often receive contributions from several causes. At a chosen location, those contributions must be combined by **vector addition**, which accounts for both magnitude and direction. Suppose two field contributions at the same point are represented by $\mathbf{A}$ and $\mathbf{B}$. Their resultant is the vector obtained by adding them geometrically.

![Two vector-addition diagrams comparing the parallelogram and tip-to-tail methods for adding vectors A and B](/electromagnetism-1/assets/source-visuals/engineering-electromagnetics-9th-ed-9nbsped-compress-page-15-diagram-f1-two-vector-addition-diagrams-comparing-the-paral.png)

*Two vector-addition diagrams comparing the parallelogram and tip-to-tail methods for adding vectors A and B* *(p. 15)*

The left construction places $\mathbf{A}$ and $\mathbf{B}$ at a common starting point and completes a parallelogram. Its diagonal is the resultant. The right construction moves one arrow without rotating or resizing it, placing its tail at the tip of the other arrow. The arrow from the original starting point to the final tip is the same resultant. These constructions look different on the page, yet both preserve the directions and lengths of $\mathbf{A}$ and $\mathbf{B}$, so both produce the same vector sum.

**Question.** Does every arrow in a vector-field picture represent a separate field?

**Answer.** The arrows are local values of one field. Each arrow is attached to a different position and reports the magnitude and direction assigned there. The field is the complete rule that assigns those vectors throughout the region.

Describing a field point by point prepares us to study its effect along a path, across a surface, or throughout a volume. Cartesian coordinates divide space using planes associated with the $x$, $y$, and $z$ directions. Around any selected point, those directions let us construct a small rectangular region whose edges align with the coordinate axes.

![Three-panel 3D coordinate-system diagram showing coordinate planes through a point, a rectangular parallelepiped, and its differential volume element](/electromagnetism-1/assets/source-visuals/engineering-electromagnetics-9th-ed-9nbsped-compress-page-16-diagram-f1-three-panel-3d-coordinate-system-diagram-showing.png)

*Three-panel 3D coordinate-system diagram showing coordinate planes through a point, a rectangular parallelepiped, and its differential volume element* *(p. 16)*

The panels connect three scales of spatial description. Coordinate planes locate a point and establish the local directions. A rectangular parallelepiped then represents a finite portion of space around that point. Shrinking its dimensions leads to a **differential spatial element**, an extremely small geometric piece used to track local field behavior. Its edge lengths are written $dx$, $dy$, and $dz$, where each symbol represents a small displacement along its corresponding axis. Two edge lengths form a differential area, while all three form the differential volume $dV=dx\,dy\,dz$, where $dV$ is the small volume associated with the point.

The shrinking process links local field values to measurements over larger regions. A line can be assembled from differential lengths, a surface from differential areas, and a volume from differential volumes. The field may vary from one element to the next, so each element carries the field value evaluated at its own position. Later, spatial integrals will combine these many local contributions.

**Question.** When adding two field vectors, can they come from different spatial positions?

**Answer.** A physical resultant at one position combines contributions evaluated at that same position. Geometric vector addition allows an arrow to be translated for construction, but its associated field value still belongs to the location where it was evaluated.

A useful mental picture is a three-dimensional space filled with locally anchored arrows. Coordinates select an anchor point, components describe the arrow along perpendicular axes, Equation (1) gives its magnitude, and vector addition combines several contributions at that point. Differential lengths, areas, and volumes then organize neighboring points into paths, surfaces, and regions. At every chosen position, the field calculation produces one resultant arrow anchored to that position.
