The guided-wave sequence begins by distinguishing waveguides from ordinary transmission lines and explaining basic waveguide operation. Parallel-plate guides introduce wave geometry, plane-wave superposition, transverse resonance, cutoff, phase velocity, group velocity, and a direct wave-equation analysis. Rectangular waveguides then use Maxwell's equations to relate components and classify fields as transverse magnetic or transverse electric modes. The source indexes TM modes, TE modes, $\mathrm{TE}_{0p}$ modes, $\mathrm{TE}_{m0}$ modes, cutoff conditions, phase constants, and wavelength at cutoff. Planar dielectric waveguides use a plane-wave superposition model, surface waves, mode fields, and transverse resonance. Optical fibers extend these ideas to step-index structures, weak guidance, meridional and skew rays, linearly polarized modes, normalized frequency, mode parameters, cutoff conditions, and single-mode operation. Modal dispersion and mode number explain why different guided solutions can propagate differently. The Marcuse and Rudolf-Neumann formulas are indexed in connection with single-mode fiber mode-field radius and cutoff behavior.

### Key planning details

- Waveguides confine electromagnetic fields and support discrete propagation modes.
- Parallel-plate analysis introduces transverse resonance and cutoff through plane-wave geometry.
- TE modes have no longitudinal electric-field component, while TM modes have no longitudinal magnetic-field component.
- Rectangular-guide modes are indexed by transverse mode numbers.
- Cutoff conditions determine whether a mode can propagate.
- Planar dielectric guides confine fields through surface-wave and interface behavior.
- Weakly guiding fibers support approximately linearly polarized mode descriptions.
- Normalized frequency and mode parameters determine optical-fiber mode behavior.
- Single-mode operation excludes higher-order propagating modes.
- Modal dispersion results from different modes having different propagation characteristics.
