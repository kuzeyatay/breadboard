## ConceptNode: Transition from Static Fields to Time-Varying Electromagnetics

Planning node for [[transition-from-static-fields-to-time-varying-electromagnetics|1.141 Transition from Static Fields to Time-Varying Electromagnetics]].

Source: [[engineering-electromagnetics-9th-ed-9nbsped-compress|Engineering Electromagnetics, Ninth Edition: Learning Spine and Vector Analysis Foundations]]

Locations: Page 291, Page 292

Chapter 9 shifts from electrostatic and steady magnetic fields to fields that vary with time. The source identifies two central additions to the earlier theory: a changing magnetic field produces an electric field, and a changing electric field produces a magnetic field. The first relationship arose from Michael Faraday's experiments, while the second was introduced through James Clerk Maxwell's theoretical extension of Ampère's law. Faraday was motivated by Oersted's demonstration that electric current affects a compass needle. He reasoned that if current can produce magnetism, magnetism should be able to produce current. In 1831 he observed momentary galvanometer deflections when a battery circuit linked through an iron toroid was connected or disconnected. The opposite deflections for connection and disconnection revealed dependence on change rather than on a static magnetic state. He then showed that relative motion of a magnetic field and a coil could also produce a response. Maxwell later organized electromagnetic theory into four fundamental equations, using Faraday's field and line-of-force ideas as important conceptual foundations.

### Key planning details

- Time-varying electromagnetics adds coupling between changing electric and magnetic fields.
- A changing magnetic field produces a circulating electric field.
- A changing electric field contributes to the production of a magnetic field.
- Oersted's current-compass experiment motivated Faraday's search for electromagnetic induction.
- Faraday observed transient and oppositely directed galvanometer deflections when a linked battery circuit was switched.
- Relative motion between a magnetic field and a coil can also induce an emf.
- Maxwell's four equations organize the resulting electromagnetic field theory.

### Source coverage

- Page 291 states that two new concepts are the electric field produced by a changing magnetic field and the magnetic field produced by a changing electric field.
- The source attributes the first concept to Faraday's experiments and the second to Maxwell's theoretical work.
- Page 292 describes Faraday's two windings on an iron toroid, with a galvanometer in one circuit and a battery in the other.
- Closing the battery circuit produced a momentary deflection, while disconnection produced a deflection in the opposite direction.
- Faraday also demonstrated induction from either a moving magnetic field or a moving coil.
