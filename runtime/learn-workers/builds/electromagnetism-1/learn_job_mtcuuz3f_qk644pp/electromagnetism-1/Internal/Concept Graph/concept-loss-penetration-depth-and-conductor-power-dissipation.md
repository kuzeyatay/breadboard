## ConceptNode: Loss, Penetration Depth, and Conductor Power Dissipation

Planning node for [[loss-penetration-depth-and-conductor-power-dissipation|1.243 Loss, Penetration Depth, and Conductor Power Dissipation]].

Source: [[engineering-electromagnetics-9th-ed-9nbsped-compress|Engineering Electromagnetics, Ninth Edition: Learning Spine and Vector Analysis Foundations]]

Locations: Page 416, Problem 11.7, Page 417, Problems 11.11 through 11.16, Page 418, Problems 11.17 through 11.24, Page 419, Problems 11.25 through 11.27

Several chapter problems organize the analysis of waves in conducting media around attenuation, penetration depth, dissipated power, and skin effect. A field amplitude attenuating as $e^{-\alpha z}$ produces a power density proportional to $e^{-2\alpha z}$. The penetration depth $\delta$ provides a convenient distance scale for converting specified power reductions in decibels into material thickness. Poynting's theorem links incoming electromagnetic power to heat generated by conduction. For current density $\mathbf{J}_s=\sigma\mathbf{E}_s$, the time-average volumetric dissipation is $$\langle p_d\rangle=\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{Re}\{\mathbf{E}_s\cdot\mathbf{J}_s^*\}=\frac{\sigma}{2}|E_0|^2e^{-2\alpha z}.$$ At high frequency, skin effect confines current to a thin conductor region, increasing resistance relative to its direct-current value. The problem set applies these ideas to coaxial conductors, tubular conductors, microwave-oven steel, brass, and copper transmission lines.

### Key planning details

- Field amplitude decays as $e^{-\alpha z}$ in a lossy medium.
- Power density and conduction loss decay as $e^{-2\alpha z}$.
- Penetration depth can be used to express attenuation distances.
- The average dissipation density is $(1/2)\operatorname{Re}\{\mathbf{E}_s\cdot\mathbf{J}_s^*\}$.
- For $\mathbf{J}_s=\sigma\mathbf{E}_s$, $\langle p_d\rangle=(\sigma/2)|E_0|^2e^{-2\alpha z}$.
- Skin effect changes the effective conducting area and high-frequency resistance.
- Power-flow integration can verify Poynting's theorem in conducting structures.
