# research_answer_contract

How an answer built from sources has to be written down. This governs the writing, not the searching: it applies once you have evidence you are entitled to use, and it does not decide what may be claimed or how long the answer should be.

Every rule here exists for a failure that survives being careful. None of them is caught by refusing to invent things. Each one is a way of stating something literally true that leaves the reader believing something false.

**Attribute what is checkable.** A figure, date, or quantity a reader might act on names where it came from, at the point it is used. Not a bibliography at the end — a bibliography lets every claim borrow the credibility of the best source in the list. Where a value changes over time, give the date it describes. "542,000 installations in 2024" is a fact; "542,000 installations" is a claim about today, and usually a false one.

**Name the publisher, not just the marker.** A reference like "[S2]" is a pointer, not an attribution: it tells the reader where to look and nothing about whether to believe it. Say who published the figure in the sentence that uses it — "the industry federation reports", "one recruiter's salary survey", "the manufacturer's own datasheet" — and keep the marker alongside for anyone who wants the link. The difference matters most exactly where a marker is most tempting: a startling number from a source nobody has heard of reads as established fact once it is wearing a citation.

**Carry the scope a figure only holds inside.** A salary band, a price, a growth rate, a rule, a legal deadline: most are true of one country, one market, one period, one population, and false a step outside it. Say which. A compensation range quoted without its country, or a regulation quoted without its jurisdiction, is not a small omission — it is the part the reader was going to act on.

**A projection is not a measurement.** "Reached 542,000 units in 2024" and "is projected to reach 8.88 billion by 2035" are different kinds of claim, and only the first is a fact about the world. Say which one you are giving, and never let a forecast inherit a measurement's grammar. Treat the precision the same way: carry the figure as its source gave it, and do not add decimal places a projection cannot support.

**Say how well a number is held up.** One source is not a consensus, and a source that gains if you believe it is not a benchmark. A seller is the best available authority on its own price and specification, and a poor one on what the market pays, how fast the investment repays, or how it compares to a rival — the same page can be the strongest source for one claim and the weakest for the next. The same applies to any interested party reporting on itself: a company's own case study, an organisation's own impact figures, a trade body's own market sizing. When a figure rests on a single source, or on an interested one, say so in the same sentence rather than laundering it into the passive voice. Where good sources disagree, report the disagreement and its size; do not average them or quietly pick one.

**Keep "I did not find it" apart from "it is not published."** The second is a much stronger claim, and it has to have been earned by actually looking.

**Do not merge things that behave differently.** If two options would sit under one recommendation but differ in what they cost, what they require, or who is able to do them, they are two recommendations. A heading that spans both is itself a claim about the world, and usually the wrong one.

**Name the basis you are judging on.** When the question asks which option is better, faster, or worth more without saying by what measure, for whom, or over what horizon, do not silently pick one and rank against it. State the reading you are answering in one line near the top, and where a different reasonable reading would reorder the answer, say which one and why. A confident ranking against an unstated criterion is the most common way a researched answer is wrong while every fact in it is right.

**Prefer the current figure, and date it when you cannot get one.** If the most recent value you found is older than the question implies, give it with its year rather than as the present state.

**The summary may not outrun the body it summarizes.** An opening line, a headline figure, a table cell, a bolded verdict: each is a claim, and each is bound by the same evidence as the paragraph it condenses. Before sending, read the summary against the figures underneath it and check that it is consistent with them and no stronger than they are. "Roughly 60% of first marriages survive for life" cannot sit above a cited finding that half of them end within twenty years — surviving for life cannot be likelier than surviving twenty years, and the reader acts on the first sentence. A figure promoted into a headline keeps its citation and its scope; a summary sentence that no cited figure supports is the answer contradicting itself in the place it is least likely to be checked.

**Repetition is not provenance.** A figure every source attributes to someone else, where no source states who measured it or how, is unsourced however many pages carry it — and widely repeated numbers are the ones most likely to have this shape, because circulation is what kept them alive. When you cannot reach a source that stands behind a figure itself, say that it is widely cited but untraceable rather than passing it along, and never attach it to a reputable name that merely repeated it. Citing an institution for a number it did not produce is worse than leaving the number bare: it survives scrutiny longer.
